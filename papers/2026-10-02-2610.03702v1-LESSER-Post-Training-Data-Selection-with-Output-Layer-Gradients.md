# LESSER: Post-Training Data Selection with Output-Layer Gradients

**Authors:** Lyuxin David Zhang, Eric Wong, Surbhi Goel, Anton Xue
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03702v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03702v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that selecting training data for large language models using only output-layer gradients is nearly as effective as using full-parameter gradients, but costs 9–10× less to compute. The key insight is that you don't need expensive backward passes through the entire model to pick good training examples — a cheap forward pass to the output layer gives you the signals you need.

## Theoretical Background
Data selection for model training is motivated by the observation that not all training examples contribute equally to downstream performance. Prior work uses gradient-based selection: you compute gradients for candidate training samples and a validation set, then select samples whose gradients align well with validation gradients. The intuition is that samples whose learning signals point in similar directions to your target tasks will improve performance on those tasks. However, computing full-parameter gradients requires a backward pass through the entire model (backpropagation), which becomes prohibitively expensive when you have millions of candidate samples. LESSER builds on this framework but observes that gradient information from just the output layer — the final projection to predictions — captures enough signal to rank samples effectively, and computing these requires only a forward pass, not backpropagation.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical discovery**: Output-layer gradients alone suffice for effective data selection, maintaining downstream task performance while dramatically reducing computational cost.
2. **Computational efficiency**: Reduces feature-extraction cost by 9.7× for supervised fine-tuning (SFT) and 3.0× for reinforcement learning (RL) workflows — a practical breakthrough for scaling data selection to large candidate pools.
3. **Batch-level alignment insight**: Even when output-layer and full gradients rank individual samples differently, they select batches with aligned gradient signals — suggesting that sample-level ranking imperfections wash out in aggregate.
4. **Drop-in implementation**: LESSER is designed as a wrapper compatible with existing gradient-based selection methods, making adoption straightforward.

## Practical Applications
1. **At-scale fine-tuning**: Organizations with massive candidate datasets (e.g., internet-scale text corpora) can now afford to curate training data for domain-specific LLMs without prohibitive gradient computation.
2. **Rapid model adaptation**: Companies building custom LLMs for specific industries (finance, law, healthcare) can quickly select relevant training data from large unlabeled pools for efficient task-specific fine-tuning.
3. **Cost-aware RL training**: For reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and similar post-training pipelines, the 3× speedup makes iterative data selection practical where it was previously too expensive.
4. **Dynamic curriculum learning**: Continuously selecting and reweighting training examples during training becomes feasible, enabling adaptive curriculum strategies that were computationally infeasible before.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Output-layer approximation may fail for certain architectures or tasks**: The assumption that output-layer gradients capture alignment signals may break for models with unusual head designs, multi-task heads, or very shallow output projections.
2. **Batch-level averaging masks individual failures**: While batch-level alignment holds empirically, there may be edge cases (e.g., highly imbalanced or adversarial datasets) where sample-level rank disagreement compounds into worse batch selection.
3. **Limited theoretical justification**: The paper is primarily empirical. Why output-layer gradients suffice is not rigorously explained — this is a black-box observation, not a principled guarantee.
4. **Scalability to smaller models or noisy settings unclear**: The method is tested on modern LLMs; behavior on smaller models or very noisy validation sets (where gradient signals are weak) is not explored.
5. **Hyperparameter sensitivity not fully characterized**: It's unclear how sensitive the method is to batch size, validation set size, and other hyperparameters that might shift the cost-benefit tradeoff.

## Open Questions
1. **Why do output-layer gradients work?** A mechanistic explanation — perhaps via [[neural network lottery ticket hypothesis]], information bottlenecks, or gradient flow theory — would strengthen confidence in the method.
2. **Can this extend to other modalities?** Does the trick apply to vision models, multimodal models, or other architectures where the "output layer" concept may be less clear?
3. **How does performance scale with validation set size and composition?** The paper should systematically vary the validation set and characterize robustness.
4. **Can we get theoretical bounds?** Under what conditions does output-layer gradient selection provably approximate full-gradient selection? Are there convergence guarantees?
5. **Does this interact well with other efficiency techniques?** How does LESSER combine with [[quantization]], [[low-rank adaptation (LoRA)]], or [[knowledge distillation]]?
6. **What about distribution shift?** If the validation set is very different from the candidate pool, does output-layer selection still work?

## Key Takeaway
Output-layer gradients enable fast data selection for LLM training, cutting costs by 10× while matching full-gradient performance.
