# Confidence Calibration of Deep Learning Systems

**Authors:** Coby Penso
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12100v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12100v1)

## Summary
This thesis develops methods to ensure deep learning models output reliable confidence estimates—predictions that accurately reflect how likely they are to be correct—even when training data is noisy or comes from a different domain than where the model will be used. Confidence calibration matters in high-stakes settings like medicine or autonomous driving, where knowing *how uncertain* a model is can be as important as the prediction itself.

## Theoretical Background
Confidence calibration builds on the idea that a model's predicted probability for a class should match the actual frequency of correctness at that probability level. Traditional calibration methods (like temperature scaling or Platt scaling) assume clean, representative validation data. This work extends into more realistic scenarios.

The thesis draws on three foundational areas:

1. **Label noise literature**: Prior work has shown that unreliable training labels degrade model confidence; the thesis proposes modeling the noise process itself to recover clean confidence estimates.

2. **Conformal Prediction (CP)**: A framework that wraps any predictor to produce set-valued predictions with *finite-sample coverage guarantees* — i.e., the true label is guaranteed to be in the predicted set with high probability, regardless of the underlying model.

3. **Domain adaptation theory**: Models often degrade when deployed on data from a different distribution than training; the thesis estimates how much performance drops without access to labeled target data.

Privacy is added via **differential privacy**, a mathematical framework that adds controlled noise to prevent inference of individual data points.

## Core Contributions

1. **Noise-aware confidence reconstruction**: A framework that uses an estimated label noise model to transform noisy confidence estimates into clean ones, without requiring clean validation data.

2. **Conformal Prediction under label noise**: Extends CP (which normally assumes clean labels) to estimate valid conformity scores despite label corruption, preserving coverage guarantees.

3. **Unsupervised domain adaptation calibration**: A method to estimate target-domain calibration using only source-domain labels and domain discrepancy measurements, with no labeled target data.

4. **Locally differentially private conformal prediction**: A privacy-preserving variant of CP that provides uncertainty quantification while protecting user labels and model outputs, balancing three competing objectives (privacy, computation, reliability).

## Connections to Other Work

This work sits at the intersection of [[uncertainty quantification]], [[robustness]], and [[privacy-preserving machine learning]].

**Calibration broadly**: Related to recent work on selective prediction and rejection sampling, which also aim for reliable confidence in deployment.

**Conformal Prediction**: Builds directly on [[Conformal Prediction]] as a framework; prior work on CP assumed clean data, so extending it to noisy/domain-shifted settings is novel.

**Domain adaptation**: Complements recent unsupervised domain adaptation work (e.g., using domain discrepancy metrics like MMD or CORAL), but focuses specifically on confidence rather than point accuracy.

**Differential privacy in ML**: Extends the privacy-ML literature by applying it specifically to uncertainty quantification rather than point predictions.

## Practical Applications

1. **Medical diagnosis systems**: A doctor needs to know not just the predicted disease, but *how confident* the model is. Noise-aware calibration handles mislabeled training examples (common in historical medical data).

2. **Autonomous driving**: Models adapted to new weather/geographic regions must provide calibrated uncertainty; the method estimates accuracy without collecting and labeling target-domain footage.

3. **Federated learning in healthcare**: Hospitals train a shared model but cannot share patient data. Locally differentially private conformal prediction lets hospitals get uncertainty quantification while protecting privacy.

4. **Content moderation at scale**: Platforms retrain moderators as norms shift (domain shift); the method estimates when confidence drops without re-labeling.

5. **Loan approval systems**: Must provide calibrated confidence in approval decisions; handles historical label noise from disputed defaults.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **CalibrateAI**: A post-hoc confidence-calibration API for deployed ML models. Customers upload their noisy historical labels and get a calibration module that wraps their predictions, certifying confidence reliability to regulators.

2. **PrivateUQ-as-a-Service**: A privacy-compliant uncertainty quantification platform for federated learning. Hospitals/banks train local models, the service adds conformal prediction without collecting raw data.

3. **Domain Shift Auditor**: A SaaS tool that automatically detects and flags when a production model's confidence becomes unreliable due to domain shift (new user populations, seasonality, etc.), recommending retraining without requiring labeled new data.

4. **Conformal Prediction Toolkit**: Open-source + commercial support for building confidence-calibrated systems; focus on practitioners who need to handle noisy labels and domain shift together.

5. **Regulatory Compliance Dashboard**: For financial/medical AI systems, a platform that continuously audits whether model confidences remain calibrated post-deployment, generating evidence for regulators and flagging risky predictions.

## Technical Limitations

1. **Noise model estimation**: The method assumes you can reliably estimate the label noise process itself. If the noise is highly structured (not random), or if multiple types of noise interact, estimation may fail.

2. **Limited target-domain info**: In unsupervised domain adaptation, the method estimates target accuracy from domain discrepancies (e.g., feature distribution differences). This can break if the domain shift is *label-conditional* — i.e., the relationship between features and labels changes, not just the features themselves.

3. **Scalability of conformal prediction**: CP's computational cost grows with dataset size (must compute conformity scores for many examples). Privacy-preserving CP adds further overhead; may not scale to billions of examples.

4. **Differential privacy–utility tradeoff**: Adding privacy noise reduces prediction quality. The paper balances three objectives, but in some regimes (very high privacy, very large model), the utility loss may be unacceptable.

5. **Assumes known noise model class**: The framework requires choosing a parametric noise model (e.g., label-flipping probability per class). Misspecification can hurt calibration.

6. **Limited to classification**: Extensions to regression or ranking are not discussed.

## Open Questions

1. **Non-random label noise**: How do the methods perform under realistic, structured noise (e.g., annotators systematically confuse two similar classes, or noise depends on example hardness)?

2. **Combining multiple sources of unreliability**: What if data is simultaneously noisy *and* domain-shifted? The thesis addresses these separately; joint treatment is unclear.

3. **Calibration under distribution shift in features *and* labels**: The domain adaptation method focuses on feature shift. What if the label distribution also changes (e.g., disease prevalence shifts)?

4. **Scalability of private conformal prediction**: Can the method scale to large-scale deployments (billions of predictions)? What is the practical privacy–utility frontier?

5. **Interpretability of confidence**: How can practitioners understand *why* a model is (un)confident in a given prediction? Confidence estimates alone don't explain.

6. **Adapting noise model online**: As deployed systems encounter new types of label corruption, can the noise model update in real time without full retraining?

## Key Takeaway
Deep learning systems can now provide trustworthy confidence estimates even when trained on noisy data or adapted to new domains without target labels.
