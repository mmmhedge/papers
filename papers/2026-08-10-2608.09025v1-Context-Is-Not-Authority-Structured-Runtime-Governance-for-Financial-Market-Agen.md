# Context Is Not Authority: Structured Runtime Governance for Financial Market Agents

**Authors:** Rui Tang, Qiangqiang Liu, Yichi Zhang, Youwei Wang, Xi Chen et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-10 | **arXiv:** [2608.09025v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09025v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces SAGE-Fin, a runtime governance system that controls what financial agents are *allowed to do*, not just what they claim to do. Most safeguards focus on monitoring an agent's reasoning or outputs; SAGE-Fin instead enforces strict authority contracts—ensuring that before a trading commitment, customer-facing response, or policy change executes, it has explicit permission under current market conditions, account state, and institutional rules. The system was tested on 616 cases and validated in production at a real cryptocurrency platform.

## Theoretical Background
Financial systems rely on role-based access control and authorization frameworks—rules that specify who can do what. Traditional software uses capabilities-based or role-based security models to gate resource access. In multi-agent systems (systems with many autonomous software components), ensuring that agents respect institutional constraints is hard because agents can reason plausibly about context ("the market allows a large trade") without having the authority to act on that reasoning. SAGE-Fin borrows from formal methods (executable specifications, typed contracts) and applies them to the problem of authority delegation in agent-driven finance. The core insight is that authorization is not just about *understanding* context—it's about maintaining a runtime ledger of who holds permission for what action, and checking this ledger before any effect is committed.

## Core Contributions
1. **Effect-centric authorization contracts**: Rather than gate agent outputs through text-based rules, SAGE-Fin binds authorization to typed, concrete effects (e.g., "this exact trade proposal") and the adapters that consume them, ensuring the artifact matches the intended action.
2. **Coverage debt tracking**: The system explicitly records missing or stale institutional obligations (e.g., missing audit logs, expired compliance checks) as "coverage debt," making governance gaps visible.
3. **State-respecting authority re-check**: Authorization decisions are refreshed after material state changes (market moves, account balance shifts, policy updates, dialogue context), preventing stale permissions from enabling unauthorized acts.
4. **Executable conformance testing**: A deterministic specification framework compiles to 3,080 test cases with 100% reference parity, demonstrating formal correctness rather than heuristic safety.
5. **Production validation**: Real-world deployment at a digital-asset exchange, with independent operational assessment and user feedback supporting practical utility.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[alignment]] (ensuring agents behave as intended) and [[risk-management]] in autonomous systems. It relates to research on constrained reinforcement learning and safe multi-agent systems, which also aim to make agent behavior predictable and compliant. Unlike [[mechanistic-interpretability]] approaches (which try to understand *why* an agent makes a decision), SAGE-Fin enforces rules at the execution boundary—a complementary strategy. The formal specification method draws from contract-based design in software engineering. The production focus distinguishes it from purely academic safeguard proposals, grounding governance in real operational workflows.

## Practical Applications
1. **Cryptocurrency exchanges and custodians**: Preventing unauthorized trades, withdrawals, or policy changes by enforcing authority contracts before any customer-facing transaction.
2. **Algorithmic trading desks**: Ensuring that trade proposals comply with current risk limits, account funding, and regulatory constraints before order submission.
3. **Lending platforms**: Gating loan approvals and disbursements so they respect current borrower eligibility, collateral state, and lending policy without stale or missing checks.
4. **Regulatory compliance dashboards**: Automatically detecting and flagging "coverage debt" (gaps in authorization trails) for audit and enforcement teams.
5. **Multi-agent financial workflows**: Any system where software agents negotiate or commit on behalf of institutions—settlements, hedging, or cross-party agreements—can use SAGE-Fin to ensure no agent overreaches.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Authorization-as-a-Service for fintech**: A cloud service that sits between agent-driven trading platforms and market venues, enforcing SAGE-Fin–style contracts for third-party algorithmic traders. Revenue: per-transaction or per-API-call fees.
2. **Compliance audit tool for digital assets**: A B2B product that tracks "coverage debt" across crypto exchanges, lending protocols, and custodians, auto-generating regulatory reports. Revenue: subscription + audit-readiness certifications.
3. **Multi-party settlement governance**: A platform for OTC derivatives and syndicated loans where deal parties specify authority contracts that auto-enforce state-dependent permission rules. Revenue: per-deal or per-party fees.
4. **Risk-gated agent orchestration**: Developer toolkit for institutions running multiple autonomous systems (trading, lending, customer service) that need centralized authority control. Revenue: licensing + implementation services.
5. **Real-time policy engine for robo-advisors**: Ensures that wealth-management agents never recommend or execute actions that violate client constraints, account rules, or regulatory limits—with full audit trails. Revenue: per-advisor-account or per-AUM percentage.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Specification burden**: Authoring accurate, complete typed contracts is labor-intensive; incomplete specs leave coverage gaps (the "coverage debt" concept acknowledges this but doesn't fully solve it).
2. **State complexity**: Real financial systems have intricate, interdependent state (market data, account balances, regulatory status, dialogue history). Keeping authorization checks synchronized with all relevant state changes is fragile.
3. **Latency and performance**: Re-checking authorization on every state change adds runtime overhead; in high-frequency or latency-sensitive trading, this cost may be unacceptable.
4. **Predecessor-failure anecdotes, not prevalence**: The paper cites three de-identified failure cases that the system would have caught, but does not quantify how often such failures occur in practice, limiting impact estimation.
5. **Limited scalability evidence**: Testing on 616 cases is thorough for validation but doesn't demonstrate how SAGE-Fin scales to institutions with thousands of agents, policies, or concurrent workflows.
6. **No formal proof of completeness**: The system achieves reference parity on test cases but does not formally prove that all possible exploits are prevented—it's a practical safeguard, not a mathematical guarantee.

## Open Questions
1. **How does SAGE-Fin perform under adversarial agent behavior?** If an agent intentionally crafts ambiguous context or races state updates to bypass checks, what happens? Are there game-theoretic attacks on the authorization logic?
2. **Can coverage debt be automatically detected and repaired?** The paper records gaps but doesn't automate diagnosis or remediation—what would a learning system need to do so?
3. **Does this generalize beyond finance?** Could the same authority-contract approach work for e-commerce platforms, healthcare systems, or other multi-agent settings?
4. **What is the false-negative rate in production?** Did the system ever block a legitimate transaction, and if so, how often? How did operators respond?
5. **How do you evolve policies and contracts without breaking live agents?** The paper doesn't address versioning, deprecation, or gradual rollout of authorization rule changes.
6. **Can you combine SAGE-Fin with interpretability?** Could you integrate mechanistic interpretability (understanding agent reasoning) with authority contracts (controlling agent actions) for even stronger assurance?

## Key Takeaway
Governance of financial agents requires controlling *what they do*, not just monitoring what they output—SAGE-Fin enforces this through runtime authorization contracts tied to actual effects.
