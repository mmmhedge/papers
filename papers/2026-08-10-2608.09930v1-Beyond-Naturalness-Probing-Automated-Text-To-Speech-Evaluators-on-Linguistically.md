# Beyond Naturalness: Probing Automated Text-To-Speech Evaluators on Linguistically Grounded Dimensions

**Authors:** Oluwanifemi Bamgbose, Simon Rosen, Jash Shah, Lindsay Devon Brin, Hoang H Nguyen et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-10 | **arXiv:** [2608.09930v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09930v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that current automated speech quality evaluators—both traditional MOS predictors and newer Audio-LLM judges—fail to capture the full complexity of how humans perceive synthetic speech. The authors created a benchmark with linguistically-grounded dimensions (like prosody, articulation, and rhythm) to reveal that these evaluators collapse into measuring just acoustic quality, missing specific linguistic errors that listeners actually notice.

## Theoretical Background
Text-to-Speech (TTS) systems generate spoken audio from text. Traditionally, quality is measured via Mean Opinion Score (MOS)—humans rate naturalness on a numeric scale. More recently, large language models adapted for audio (Audio-LLMs) have been proposed as cheaper automated judges. The assumption underlying both approaches is that they correlate with human perception. This paper challenges that assumption by decomposing "naturalness" into distinct perceptual dimensions, building on linguistic phonetics (the study of how speech sounds are produced and perceived) and speech quality assessment research.

## Core Contributions
1. **Linguistically-grounded annotation schema**: The paper deconstructs "naturalness" into 10 distinct perceptual dimensions (e.g., articulation clarity, stress placement, speech rate appropriateness) rather than treating it as monolithic.

2. **First dimension-level TTS meta-evaluation benchmark**: 860 utterances annotated by trained linguists, enabling evaluation of how well automated judges capture each specific dimension.

3. **Systematic benchmarking of evaluators**: Shows MOS predictors collapse onto acoustic signal quality alone, while Audio-LLM judges show prompt-dependent behavior that doesn't generalize across dimensions.

4. **Public release**: Dataset, schema, and evaluation code to enable more targeted TTS evaluation research.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader efforts in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] of AI systems—understanding what different models actually measure versus what they claim to measure. The work mirrors similar decomposition efforts in [[large-language-models]] evaluation (e.g., breaking down "helpfulness" into sub-dimensions). It also connects to [[alignment]] challenges: if our evaluators don't measure what we think they do, systems optimized against those metrics may not actually improve in human-perceivable ways.

## Practical Applications
- **TTS system development**: Engineers can now identify which specific speech aspects their models struggle with, enabling targeted improvements rather than blanket "naturalness" optimization.
- **Quality assurance for voice services**: Companies deploying synthetic voices (virtual assistants, audiobooks, accessibility tools) can diagnose specific failure modes.
- **Regulatory compliance**: When synthetic speech is used in regulated contexts (voice banking, medical accessibility), dimension-level quality checks enable more precise specification of acceptable quality thresholds.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **TTS evaluation-as-a-service**: A cloud API that benchmarks TTS systems across linguistically-grounded dimensions, providing detailed diagnostic reports beyond just "naturalness" scores.

2. **Targeted TTS fine-tuning platform**: Auto-identify which linguistic dimensions underperform and suggest data/training adjustments to fix specific issues.

3. **Voice dataset quality auditor**: Tool for audiobook studios, podcast producers, and voice synthesis companies to validate voice talent or synthetic speech meets dimension-specific standards.

4. **Interactive TTS debugging tool**: IDE-like interface where developers can input text, generate speech, and see real-time breakdowns of which dimensions fail and why.

5. **Linguistic speech quality standards for accessibility**: Certification/badge system for synthetic voices used in accessibility contexts, guaranteeing minimum standards on articulation, prosody, and intelligibility dimensions.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scale and diversity**: The benchmark has 860 utterances—relatively small and potentially biased toward specific languages, accents, or speech contexts. Generalization to non-English languages or highly diverse speaker populations is unclear.
- **Rater subjectivity**: Even with trained linguists, inter-rater agreement on perceptual dimensions is not discussed; dimension definitions may be ambiguous.
- **Frozen evaluators**: MOS predictors and Audio-LLMs tested are fixed models; findings may not apply to future, larger models or those specifically trained on linguistic dimensions.
- **Dimension independence**: No analysis of whether the 10 dimensions are truly independent or if some are correlated proxies for others.
- **Limited TTS coverage**: Only four MOS predictors and four Audio-LLM judges tested; unclear how broadly conclusions hold.

## Open Questions
- How do dimension-level evaluator failures relate to end-user satisfaction in real applications (e.g., virtual assistants, audiobooks)?
- Can Audio-LLMs be prompted or fine-tuned to reliably detect all dimensions, or is there a fundamental limitation?
- Do human raters weight dimensions equally, or do some errors matter more for naturalness than others?
- How do these linguistic dimensions interact with non-native speaker synthesis, emotional speech, or domain-specific content (medical, technical)?
- Could a multi-task model trained explicitly on dimension prediction outperform generic MOS predictors?

## Key Takeaway
Automated speech evaluators miss specific linguistic errors humans hear because they only measure acoustic quality, not linguistic structure.
