# MQSS-Selector: RL-Guided Pass Selection for an MLIR Compilation Pipeline

**Authors:** Andre Youssefi, Ercüment Kaya, Minh Chung, Jorge Echavarria, Laura B. Schulz et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-24 | **arXiv:** [2609.30104v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30104v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes MQSS-Selector, a reinforcement learning system that automatically chooses which compiler optimizations to apply when compiling quantum circuits for hybrid classical-quantum computers. Instead of using fixed rules, the system learns to pick the best sequence of optimizations for each circuit, trading off multiple goals like circuit fidelity, compilation speed, and scheduling delay — addressing a real bottleneck in quantum computing where today's devices are noisy and resource-constrained.

## Theoretical Background
Quantum computing is at the NISQ (Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum) era — devices have 50–1000 qubits but suffer high error rates and limited coherence time. Classical compilers use fixed optimization "passes" (sequences of transformations); MLIR (Multi-Level Intermediate Representation) is a modern compiler framework that chains these passes together. Reinforcement learning (RL) — training an agent to maximize cumulative rewards through trial and error — has been successfully applied to code optimization in classical compilers. This paper extends that idea to quantum: treat pass selection as an RL problem where the "agent" learns which passes minimize errors and latency for different circuit types.

## Core Contributions
1. **Unified framework**: Integrates device selection, compiler-pass optimization, and job scheduling into one RL-guided system — traditionally separate stages.
2. **Multi-objective optimization**: Uses deep learning models to simultaneously optimize fidelity, compilation time, and scheduling latency, not just one metric.
3. **Dynamic adaptation**: The selector adapts its decisions based on circuit characteristics (depth, gate types, qubit count) and real-time device conditions (error rates, queue load).
4. **MLIR integration**: Demonstrates the approach on a realistic quantum compiler stack using MLIR, making it practically deployable.

## Connections to Other Work
This builds on prior work applying [[reinforcement-learning]] to classical compiler optimization (e.g., learning pass ordering for LLVM) and extends it to the quantum domain. It relates to [[graph-neural-networks]] for representing circuits and [[multi-agent-systems]] if multiple devices/queues are coordinated. The multi-objective aspect connects to Pareto optimization literature. Unlike prior quantum-specific work that uses hand-tuned heuristics, this learns from data, similar to neural architecture search (NAS) in deep learning.

## Practical Applications
- **Quantum software stacks**: Immediate use in IBM Qiskit, Google Cirq, or other quantum SDKs to auto-tune compilation.
- **Hybrid HPC-QC workflows**: Automatically balance classical and quantum job submission in converged data centers.
- **Device-specific optimization**: Deploy on quantum cloud platforms (IBM Quantum, AWS Braket) to reduce user-visible latency and errors.
- **Fidelity-critical applications**: Chemistry simulations, optimization problems where circuit fidelity directly impacts result quality.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Quantum Compilation-as-a-Service**: A cloud service that learns optimal pass sequences per user/device, offered as a managed layer above raw quantum backends (like GitHub Copilot for quantum code).
2. **Quantum Circuit Optimizer SaaS**: Standalone tool that integrates with existing quantum IDEs; charges per circuit compiled or per performance gains achieved.
3. **Enterprise Quantum DevOps Platform**: Unified dashboard managing device selection, compilation, queuing, and scheduling — targets pharmaceutical and finance firms running hybrid workflows.
4. **Quantum ML Benchmarking Suite**: Sell performance profiles and optimal pass recommendations for different circuit families, updated as new devices launch.

## Technical Limitations
- **Training data scarcity**: RL typically requires millions of environment interactions; quantum devices are precious and expensive — training may be slow or limited to simulators, risking poor sim-to-hardware transfer.
- **Generalization across devices**: A model trained on IBM quantum devices may fail on Rigetti or trapped-ion systems; retraining per device is costly.
- **Reward signal design**: Multi-objective RL is notoriously hard — how to weight fidelity vs. latency vs. compilation time? Wrong weights lead to poor solutions.
- **Non-stationary environment**: Device error rates drift over time and vary with temperature; the RL model may become stale.
- **Scalability to larger circuits**: NISQ circuits are small (< 100 qubits); unclear if the approach scales to fault-tolerant quantum computers with thousands of logical qubits.

## Open Questions
- How sensitive is the learned policy to the choice of reward weights? Can multi-objective RL find a single Pareto-optimal policy, or do you need multiple models?
- Does a model trained on simulated circuits transfer to real hardware, or is simulation bias a showstopper?
- How often does the model need retraining as devices age or new passes are added to MLIR?
- Can the framework handle online learning — updating the policy in production as new circuits arrive — without destabilizing performance?
- What is the minimum quantum device noise/fidelity threshold below which RL-guided optimization becomes net-negative (i.e., the overhead outweighs gains)?

## Key Takeaway
Reinforcement learning automatically tunes quantum compiler optimizations to balance fidelity, speed, and scheduling—removing manual tuning for noisy quantum devices.
