# Rational Clarification by Assistive Agents via Value-of-Information Reasoning

**Authors:** T. Duy Nguyen-Hien, Yee Whye Teh, Wee Sun Lee, Tan Zhi-Xuan
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.37588v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37588v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a practical problem: when users make ambiguous requests to AI assistants, should the assistant ask clarifying questions or just act on its best guess? The authors propose REVOIR, a reasoning method that decides whether to clarify by estimating how much an answer would improve the task outcome, balancing question-asking costs against the risk of misalignment. On two tasks, REVOIR outperforms existing approaches while asking far fewer questions.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on decision theory and information theory foundations. It extends classical "value of information" (VOI) reasoning — a framework for deciding whether acquiring information is worth its cost — to the domain of conversational AI. Standard uncertainty-reduction approaches (like information gain minimization) measure how much a question reduces entropy about user intent, but ignore whether that reduced uncertainty actually matters for task success. REVOIR instead computes the *value* of reduced uncertainty in terms of downstream task reward, a more direct optimization target. The paper also connects to [[RLHF|reinforcement learning from human feedback]] and preference-aligned AI, since it optimizes for user satisfaction rather than just accuracy.

## Core Contributions
1. **REVOIR framework**: A principled inference-time method for deciding when to clarify, based on value-of-information reasoning rather than uncertainty minimization alone.
2. **Integration of multiple trade-offs**: The method explicitly accounts for question-asking costs, the impact of uncertainty on task performance, and the option for users to provide corrections post-hoc without being asked.
3. **Empirical validation on two distinct tasks**: Demonstrates 13–15% improvement in preference satisfaction on ADAPT (household planning) versus fine-tuned baselines, with 5× fewer questions.
4. **No training required**: REVOIR works as an inference-time reasoning procedure, avoiding the need for fine-tuning or task-specific training data.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[Alignment|AI alignment]] — specifically the challenge of ensuring assistants act in line with user intent despite ambiguity. It also connects to broader work on [[agents|conversational agents]] and human-AI collaboration. The paper critiques simpler approaches (prompting, chain-of-thought, information gain) and shows they lack the adaptive reasoning to handle the full decision problem. The method bears conceptual similarity to [[RLHF]] in optimizing for user preference, but uses principled decision-theoretic reasoning rather than reward learning.

## Practical Applications
1. **Virtual assistants** (smart home systems, office assistants): Deciding when to ask "which room?" vs. attempting a guess, balancing annoyance against execution errors.
2. **Customer service chatbots**: Clarifying ambiguous requests (e.g., "I want to return something") only when the expected value of clarification justifies the conversation overhead.
3. **Medical/healthcare triage systems**: Determining whether to ask follow-up questions about symptoms or risk factors before recommending an action, weighting information value against patient time.
4. **E-commerce recommendation engines**: Asking for preference details ("budget range?", "style preference?") only when the answer will meaningfully improve recommendations.
5. **Collaborative planning systems**: Deciding when to ask humans for clarification on ambiguous task specifications versus proceeding with reasonable defaults.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Smart Clarification Middleware**: A plug-and-play service that wraps any LLM-based assistant and intelligently decides when to clarify user requests using REVOIR. Charges per clarification decision avoided (cost savings metric for enterprises).
2. **Adaptive Customer Support Platform**: A CRM-integrated chatbot that learns domain-specific VOI thresholds (tech support vs. billing inquiries have different clarification costs) and automatically tunes clarification behavior.
3. **Privacy-Preserving Home Automation SDK**: A smart-home framework that uses REVOIR to minimize user interruptions while maintaining alignment, targeting privacy-conscious consumers and enterprises.
4. **B2B SaaS Copilot for Complex Workflows**: A vertical-specific assistant (legal document review, data analytics) that auto-clarifies ambiguous requests with tunable risk tolerance — sold to enterprises wanting safer AI without constant interaction.
5. **Conversational UX Testing Tool**: A platform that measures and optimizes the "clarification tax" (user frustration from over-asking vs. task failure from under-asking) for chatbot designers, using REVOIR as the core algorithm.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Value-of-Information computation cost**: Estimating VOI requires reasoning over possible user intents and task outcomes, which may be expensive for complex domains or large intent spaces.
2. **Reward model dependency**: The method assumes access to a meaningful task reward signal. In domains where task success is ambiguous or long-horizon, defining this signal is non-trivial.
3. **User model assumptions**: REVOIR assumes a model of how users answer questions and provide corrections. If user behavior is highly idiosyncratic or adversarial, the model may fail.
4. **Limited evaluation scope**: Tested on only two tasks (question answering and household planning). Generalization to very different domains (e.g., creative writing, technical debugging) is unclear.
5. **Scalability with intent ambiguity**: As the number of possible user intents grows, the VOI computation may become intractable without approximations.
6. **No analysis of failure modes**: The paper doesn't deeply explore when REVOIR's assumptions break (e.g., if users give misleading answers, or if task rewards are misspecified).

## Open Questions
1. **How does REVOIR scale to very large intent spaces** (e.g., open-ended creative tasks)? Can approximations or hierarchical reasoning reduce computation?
2. **Can REVOIR learn or adapt its reward model from experience**, rather than relying on a pre-specified one?
3. **How sensitive is the method to errors in the user behavior model?** What happens if users don't behave as the assistant expects?
4. **Does REVOIR work with multi-turn, dynamic ambiguity resolution**, where clarifications themselves introduce new uncertainty?
5. **Can this integrate with learning-based approaches** (e.g., fine-tuning a clarification policy) to potentially do better than pure inference-time reasoning?
6. **How do cultural or demographic differences in user patience/tolerance for questions affect the optimal clarification strategy?**

## Key Takeaway
Assistants should ask clarifying questions only when the information gained meaningfully improves task success, not just to reduce uncertainty.
