# The Condition-Number Barrier in Sparse Least Squares

**Authors:** Honghao Lin, Vahab Mirrokni, David P. Woodruff
**Published:** 2026-08-03 | **arXiv:** [2608.02588v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02588v1)

## Summary
This paper proves a fundamental lower bound on the computational complexity of sparse least-squares regression — specifically, that any polynomial-time algorithm cannot substantially improve the dependence on the restricted condition number (a measure of numerical difficulty) without making strong unproven assumptions. The result matters because it identifies a barrier beyond which we cannot efficiently optimize: there exist hard problem instances where trading off sparsity and accuracy inevitably requires more computation.

## Theoretical Background
Sparse least-squares regression aims to solve $\min_{\|x\|_0 \leq k} \|Ax - b\|_2^2$, where $\|x\|_0$ counts nonzero entries (sparsity constraint). The restricted condition number $\kappa_r$ measures the ratio of largest to smallest eigenvalues within the space of sparse vectors — it controls convergence rates in iterative algorithms. Prior work by Axiotis and Sviridenko conjectured that algorithms must scale linearly with $\kappa_r$; this paper proves it under the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis (a hardness assumption from complexity theory analogous to P≠NP). The proof uses conditional hardness: it does not show the bound is unconditionally necessary, but rather shows it follows from a plausible (but unproven) hardness conjecture in the theory of Unique Games.

## Core Contributions
1. **Conditional lower bound**: Establishes that for any $\gamma \in (0,1]$, no randomized polynomial-time algorithm can achieve $s = O(k \cdot \kappa_{s+k}^{1-\gamma})$ sparsity in the output while approximately minimizing least-squares error — proving Axiotis–Sviridenko's conjecture under the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis.
2. **Hardness reduction**: Constructs an explicit reduction from weighted regular-graph expansion to sparse regression, showing how hard instances of expansion imply hard instances of least-squares.
3. **Meta-methodological contribution**: Demonstrates that an automated AI agent (Gemini-based system) successfully proved a rigorous lower bound in optimization theory, with human verification — a rare instance of automated theorem proving in complexity theory.

This connects to [[hardness of approximation]] (the field of conditional lower bounds) and [[sparse recovery]] problems like compressed sensing. It extends the Axiotis–Sviridenko framework and relates to broader questions about when we can efficiently trade off sparsity and numerical stability. The result also implicitly relates to [[gradient-based optimization]] convergence rates — many algorithms provably require steps proportional to condition number, and this paper suggests that polynomial-time improvements may be impossible.

## Practical Applications
1. **Feature selection in high-dimensional learning**: When datasets have many irrelevant features, sparse regression is essential; this result explains why certain datasets are inherently hard to optimize and should inform algorithm selection.
2. **Sparse neural network training**: Understanding sparsity–condition-number tradeoffs helps practitioners understand fundamental limits when training pruned or quantized models.
3. **Compressed sensing recovery**: In signal processing, guaranteed recovery under noise often depends on restricted condition numbers; this work clarifies limits on near-optimal sparse reconstruction.
4. **Regression with many correlated predictors**: In settings like genomics or finance where covariates are highly correlated (high condition number), this result justifies why heuristics like LASSO may be necessary — polynomial-time "optimal" sparse solutions may not exist.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Conditional result**: The lower bound holds only if the Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis is true — it is not an unconditional proof. If this hypothesis is false, the barrier may not exist.
2. **Worst-case hardness**: The result applies to the hardest instances; it does not rule out efficient algorithms for natural or structured problem instances (e.g., incoherent designs in compressed sensing may remain tractable).
3. **Gap to practice**: The hardness construction may produce artificial instances that do not arise in real applications; practical sparse regression may remain easier than the theoretical worst case.
4. **Approximate factor**: The result bounds $\kappa^{1-\gamma}$ dependence for arbitrarily small $\gamma > 0$, leaving open whether sub-polynomial improvements (e.g., $\kappa^{0.99}$) are possible.
5. **Limited to least squares**: The proof is specific to least-squares loss; hardness for other sparse convex objectives (Huber loss, logistic regression) remains open.

## Open Questions
1. Can the bound be unconditionally proven, or is Small-Set Expansion Hypothesis necessary? Are there other hardness assumptions that might suffice?
2. How tight is the reduction? Can we construct natural problem families where this hardness is realized, or are hard instances necessarily artificial?
3. Does the barrier extend beyond least squares to other sparse optimization objectives and loss functions?
4. Can we prove similar barriers for approximate or approximate-sparsity variants (e.g., allowing $O(k \log k)$ nonzeros in the solution)?
5. What is the landscape of polynomial-time algorithms in practice? Do practical solvers (LASSO, OMP, greedy methods) asymptotically achieve better constants or different tradeoffs than the hardness suggests?
6. Can the AI-assisted proof technique be scaled to other optimization lower bounds or complexity barriers?

## Key Takeaway
Some sparse-regression problems have no polynomial-time solution that balances sparsity and accuracy better than proven bounds allow.
