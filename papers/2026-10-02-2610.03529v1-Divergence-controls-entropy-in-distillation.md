# Divergence controls entropy in distillation

**Authors:** Nicolas Zucchet, Scott W. Linderman
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03529v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03529v1)

## Summary
This paper explains how different training objectives in knowledge distillation—the practice of training a smaller "student" model to mimic a larger "teacher" model—affect the randomness (entropy) of the student's outputs. The authors prove that forward KL divergence (used in standard cross-entropy training) inflates student entropy above the teacher's, while reverse KL deflates it, and show these effects matter practically for LLM pretraining and finetuning.

## Theoretical Background
Knowledge distillation trains a student model to match a teacher's output distribution, typically using [[KL divergence]]—a measure of how different two probability distributions are. Forward KL and reverse KL are two variants that prioritize different aspects: forward KL penalizes the student for assigning low probability to tokens the teacher finds likely; reverse KL penalizes the student for assigning high probability to tokens the teacher dislikes. Entropy measures the "randomness" or uncertainty in a probability distribution. This paper applies information-theoretic tools to understand how distillation objectives implicitly regularize entropy, which hasn't been formally characterized before for LLM training.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical proof** that forward KL divergence (including cross-entropy) forces the student to have higher entropy than the teacher—quantified and verified across pretraining and supervised finetuning experiments.
2. **Characterization of reverse KL behavior**: entropy deflates smoothly until the student diverges too far from the teacher, then rebounds.
3. **Identification of entropy as an implicit regularizer**: the choice of divergence hyperparameter indirectly controls student entropy. In self-distillation (distilling a model into itself), divergence choice must compensate for entropy loss from privileged information.
4. **Mechanistic explanation** that on-policy distillation's lower entropy comes from token-level reverse KL, not from on-policy sampling itself.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to [[knowledge distillation]] broadly and [[RLHF|preference learning]] (which also uses divergence-based objectives). It connects to implicit regularization in neural networks—the idea that optimization objectives indirectly control model properties beyond their explicit targets. The entropy perspective complements mechanistic studies of LLM behavior and touches on alignment concerns around model calibration and uncertainty. The self-distillation result echoes work on privileged information and knowledge extraction in model compression.

## Practical Applications
1. **LLM training efficiency**: Engineers can choose divergence objectives (forward vs. reverse KL) based on desired output calibration—forward KL for well-distributed, less confident outputs; reverse KL for sharper, more confident predictions.
2. **Model finetuning**: Supervised finetuning can be tuned to maintain or adjust student entropy relative to the teacher, improving downstream task performance where calibration matters.
3. **Self-distillation**: When distilling a model into itself (for compression or efficiency), the analysis explains why certain hyperparameter choices work better and how to compensate for information loss.
4. **Deployment optimization**: Smaller, distilled models can be tuned for specific entropy/calibration targets for different applications (e.g., high-confidence classification vs. uncertainty-aware prediction).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Entropy-aware model distillation platform**: A tool that automatically selects divergence objectives and hyperparameters based on a user's desired student entropy profile, validated on their data.
2. **Calibration-as-a-service for LLMs**: Offer pre-distilled model variants tuned to specific entropy targets, allowing practitioners to pick the calibration that suits their application.
3. **Model compression with predictable behavior**: Package distillation as a service that guarantees entropy properties, marketed to teams needing reliable uncertainty estimates (finance, healthcare, autonomous systems).
4. **Hyperparameter search optimizer**: Build a tool that tunes divergence hyperparameters in real time during finetuning to match a target entropy curve, selling it as part of an LLM workflow platform.
5. **Interpretability layer for distilled models**: Offer a diagnostic tool showing how divergence choices affect entropy, loss, and downstream metrics—helping teams debug distillation failures.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scope to KL divergences**: The analysis focuses on forward and reverse KL; other divergences (Wasserstein, JS divergence) are not rigorously characterized.
2. **Discrete vs. continuous tokens**: The theory assumes discrete token distributions; extension to continuous or latent representations is unclear.
3. **Teacher quality assumption**: The analysis assumes the teacher is fixed and well-trained; interactive or co-training scenarios may violate the framework.
4. **Scale validation**: Experiments shown are on pretraining and supervised finetuning; applicability to very large models (100B+ parameters) or RL-based training (e.g., with reward models) is untested.
5. **Entropy as sole metric**: Entropy doesn't capture everything about output quality—a low-entropy model can still be wrong. Task performance validation is limited in scope.
6. **Privileged information specificity**: Self-distillation results apply when privileged information is available; the principle may not generalize to other settings where student entropy naturally diverges from teacher.

## Open Questions
1. How do these entropy dynamics change in RL-based training (e.g., with reward models or [[reinforcement learning from human feedback]])?
2. Can we predict the optimal entropy level for a downstream task, and use this framework to automatically select divergence hyperparameters?
3. How do other divergences (Wasserstein, JS, or learned divergences) affect entropy, and when should practitioners use them over KL?
4. Does this framework extend to decoder-only vs. encoder-decoder architectures, or multimodal models?
5. Can entropy control be decoupled from divergence choice—e.g., by adding explicit entropy regularization?
6. How does layer-wise or token-type-specific entropy matter for interpretability or downstream performance?

## Key Takeaway
Forward KL divergence inflates student entropy; reverse KL deflates it—the divergence choice is an implicit entropy controller in distillation.
