# PoEM: Predicting RL Outcomes from Existing Policies

**Authors:** Kimia Hamidieh, Giannis Daras, Antonio Torralba
**Published:** 2026-09-24 | **arXiv:** [2609.30226v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30226v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes PoEM, a method to predict what a reinforcement learning (RL) policy will do under a new reward function without actually running RL training. The key insight is that policies trained on different rewards often lie in a low-dimensional space—meaning you can estimate a new policy by combining existing ones, saving massive computational cost. This matters because RL fine-tuning of foundation models is expensive and unstable, and companies often need to quickly test new reward objectives.

## Theoretical Background
RL post-training takes a pre-trained model and adjusts it to maximize a reward signal—this is how systems like ChatGPT are aligned to human preferences. The standard approach (e.g., [[RLHF]], DPO) requires running optimization loops from scratch each time the reward changes. This paper builds on two observations: (1) the log-policy (logarithm of the probability distribution the model assigns) under RL often forms a linear combination of log-policies from other rewards when rewards themselves are linear combinations, and (2) even when rewards aren't linearly related, the set of log-policies across many rewards appears to span a low-rank subspace—a mathematical property meaning you only need a small number of basis vectors to approximate any policy in that space.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical result**: Proves that if reward functions combine linearly, their corresponding RL policies (in log-space) also combine linearly.
2. **Empirical observation**: Shows that log-policies across diverse rewards often lie in an approximate low-rank subspace, even when rewards are nonlinearly related.
3. **Practical algorithm**: A method to estimate weighting coefficients for policy combinations using only forward passes through existing models and reward functions—no RL training needed.
4. **Multimodal validation**: Demonstrates the approach on both text (LLM) and image (vision model) tasks with synthetic and real reward functions.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] of RL policies and the structure of learned representations. It connects to policy distillation (learning one policy from another) and reward model composition. The low-rank subspace finding echoes work on intrinsic dimensionality in neural networks. The approach is orthogonal to recent methods like [[RLHF]], DPO, and IPO—it's a post-hoc prediction tool for any RL outcome, not a new training algorithm.

## Practical Applications
- **Rapid reward prototyping**: Test 100 candidate reward functions in hours instead of months of compute.
- **Multi-objective RL without retraining**: Combine safety, accuracy, and speed rewards without running new RL pipelines.
- **A/B testing at scale**: Content moderation teams can predict policy behavior under tweaked reward rules before deployment.
- **Federated reward adaptation**: Organizations share RL checkpoints; each uses PoEM to predict outcomes under local reward preferences without sharing rewards or retraining.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RL-as-a-Service platform**: Host pre-trained policy checkpoints and reward mixtures; customers upload new rewards and get predicted policy outputs instantly.
2. **Reward optimization dashboard**: Iteratively explore reward combinations visually, seeing predicted LLM/vision outputs in real-time without compute cost.
3. **Alignment audit tool**: Safety teams quickly stress-test reward changes on language models to catch unexpected behaviors before production.
4. **Foundation model customization SaaS**: Enterprises license pre-trained checkpoints + PoEM to rapidly adapt models to domain-specific metrics (legal compliance, industry safety) without GPU budgets.
5. **Multimodal reward synthesis**: Automatically blend text and image reward functions to predict joint policy behavior for content generation pipelines.

## Technical Limitations
- **Low-rank assumption breaks**: If the true policy space is high-rank or nonlinear in unexpected ways, prediction accuracy degrades; the paper doesn't quantify how many basis rewards are needed for a given domain.
- **Basis selection**: Requires choosing which pre-trained policies to use as basis; poor choices will make prediction fail. No automatic procedure is given.
- **Reward specification**: Assumes the new reward function is computable on all samples; black-box or human-in-the-loop rewards won't work.
- **Extreme extrapolation**: Predicting policies far outside the convex hull of training rewards is likely unreliable.
- **No uncertainty quantification**: The method doesn't estimate confidence in its predictions, making it risky for high-stakes decisions.
- **Scalability to very large models**: Experiments appear limited to moderate-scale models; unclear if the low-rank structure persists in trillion-parameter models.

## Open Questions
1. How many basis policies are needed to span the policy space for a given domain? Is there a principled way to select them?
2. Does the low-rank property hold for other RL algorithms beyond what's tested (e.g., actor-critic, PPO, A3C)?
3. Can you predict not just the final policy but also the RL training *trajectory*—i.e., intermediate optimization dynamics?
4. How does the method perform when the new reward is adversarial or designed to exploit the prediction?
5. Can you combine PoEM with active learning—automatically propose which new rewards to train on to expand the span of your basis policies most efficiently?

## Key Takeaway
Predict RL policy behavior under new rewards by combining existing trained policies in log-space, avoiding expensive retraining.
