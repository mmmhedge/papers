# SoftServe: A Scalable Quasi-Newton Method for Deep Learning

**Authors:** Joohwan Ko, Tetiana Parshakova, Diana Cai, Robert M. Gower
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02182v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02182v1)

## Summary
SoftServe is a new optimization algorithm that adapts quasi-Newton methods — classical techniques from convex optimization — to train deep neural networks. The paper shows that by carefully managing curvature estimates and using GPU-friendly matrix operations, quasi-Newton can match or beat popular modern optimizers like Adam on difficult problems like recurrent networks and physics-informed models, even at scales up to 136 million parameters.

## Theoretical Background
Quasi-Newton methods are classical optimization algorithms that approximate the inverse Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives) to take better steps toward the minimum. They work exceptionally well for convex problems (where the landscape has a single bowl shape), but deep learning faces two challenges: the loss landscape is non-convex (many local minima and saddle points), and neural networks have millions or billions of parameters, making Hessian approximations computationally expensive. Modern deep learning relies instead on first-order methods like SGD and Adam, which only use gradient information. This paper builds on recent work by Berglund et al. (2025) that proposed a variational objective to extract positive-definite curvature estimates even when the true Hessian has negative eigenvalues — a common problem in non-convex settings.

## Core Contributions
1. **SoftServe family of QN methods**: A framework that maintains positive-definite curvature estimates by construction, sidestepping the need for line searches or ad hoc fixes that plague QN in non-convex settings.
2. **Diagonal and Kronecker-factored variants**: Scalable approximations that reduce memory and computation while preserving positive definiteness — diagonal variants work for any architecture, while Kronecker variants exploit structure in fully connected and convolutional layers.
3. **Coupled Newton-Schulz iteration**: Replaces expensive matrix decompositions (like Cholesky) with iterative matrix multiplications, which GPUs accelerate naturally.
4. **Empirical validation on ill-conditioned problems**: Demonstrates lower final losses than Adam, Muon, and SOAP on recurrent networks, autoencoders, physics-informed neural networks, and a 136M-parameter diffusion model.

## Connections to Other Work
SoftServe sits at the intersection of classical optimization and modern deep learning. It relates to recent quasi-Newton revivals in deep learning (e.g., [[K-FAC]], which uses Kronecker-factored Hessian approximations) and newer methods like Muon and SOAP. The use of variational objectives to handle non-convexity connects to implicit regularization ideas in deep learning. Unlike [[first-order methods]] (SGD, Adam), SoftServe incorporates curvature information; unlike traditional QN, it handles the non-convex landscape without line searches. The work also touches on ill-conditioning problems studied in [[neural network training dynamics]].

## Practical Applications
1. **Recurrent neural networks (RNNs)**: These are notoriously ill-conditioned; SoftServe could accelerate training of LSTMs and GRUs for sequence tasks.
2. **Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs)**: The paper demonstrates strong results here—training networks to solve differential equations often requires optimization of poorly-conditioned problems.
3. **Variational autoencoders and generative models**: Deep autoencoders suffer from ill-conditioning; SoftServe could reduce training time.
4. **Fine-tuning large pretrained models**: When adapting foundation models to new domains with limited data, ill-conditioning often emerges; quasi-Newton could provide faster convergence.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Automated hyperparameter optimizer for deep learning**: Package SoftServe as a drop-in replacement for Adam, with automatic selection of diagonal vs. Kronecker variants based on architecture, targeting researchers and practitioners frustrated with tuning learning rate schedules.
2. **Specialized training platform for physics-informed ML**: Build a cloud service optimized for PINNs and scientific machine learning, where SoftServe's advantages are most pronounced; target computational physics and climate modeling companies.
3. **RNN training acceleration tool**: Offer a library that automatically detects RNN architectures and applies SoftServe, marketed to NLP and speech recognition teams using older models that still need training.
4. **Ill-conditioning diagnostics and optimizer selector**: Create a tool that analyzes a training problem's conditioning, predicts whether quasi-Newton will help, and recommends between SoftServe, Adam, and other optimizers—useful for ML practitioners who don't want to experiment blindly.
5. **Enterprise fine-tuning service**: Provide managed fine-tuning of large models using SoftServe, emphasizing lower wall-clock time and fewer hyperparameter sweeps compared to Adam, targeting companies with expensive compute budgets.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Positive-definiteness derivation depends on Berglund et al. (2025)**: The core innovation relies on a recent paper; if that theoretical foundation has gaps or the variational objective doesn't generalize as expected, SoftServe may lose its main advantage.
2. **Limited to unconstrained optimization**: No support for constrained problems (e.g., with weight penalties or projections), which are common in some domains.
3. **Kronecker-factored variant only works for specific layer types**: Fully connected and convolutional layers have exploitable structure; other architectures (attention, normalization layers) may fall back to diagonal approximations, reducing benefits.
4. **Comparison baselines may not be fully tuned**: Adam and other optimizers often improve significantly with hyperparameter tuning; the paper may not have invested equally in tuning all baselines.
5. **Scalability to trillion-parameter models unclear**: Experiments go up to 136M parameters; behavior on modern 1B+ parameter models (common today) is untested. Even Kronecker-factored variants may become prohibitive in memory or compute.
6. **Stochastic optimization not fully addressed**: Most experiments likely use small batches; behavior in the stochastic regime (small batch sizes) is less clear.

## Open Questions
1. **Does SoftServe scale to modern LLM sizes?** The largest experiment is 136M parameters; how does it perform on 7B, 70B, or 175B parameter models relative to Adam?
2. **How does SoftServe compare to other recent second-order methods?** Compare against [[natural gradient descent]], [[K-FAC]] variants, and other curvature-aware optimizers under equal compute budgets.
3. **What is the memory overhead in practice?** Store an approximation of the curvature matrix; how much extra GPU memory is needed, especially for Kronecker-factored variants?
4. **Can the method incorporate momentum or be combined with learning rate schedules?** Current paper doesn't discuss these; are they beneficial or harmful?
5. **How sensitive is SoftServe to the quality of the positive-definite curvature approximation?** What happens if the variational objective from Berglund et al. is slightly violated due to numerical errors?
6. **Does SoftServe help with regularization or generalization?** Classical second-order methods can hurt generalization; does SoftServe's specific curvature choice mitigate this?

## Key Takeaway
Quasi-Newton optimization, traditionally used for convex problems, now scales to deep neural networks and beats Adam on ill-conditioned problems like recurrent and physics-informed networks.
