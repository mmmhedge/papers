# Drawdown Risk Beyond Brownian Motion: A Monte-Carlo Framework, Non-Gaussian Extensions, and Long Memory

**Authors:** Francesco Landolfi
**Published:** 2026-07-31 | **arXiv:** [2608.00127v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.00127v1)

## Summary
This paper develops a practical framework for predicting how deep and long drawdowns (peak-to-trough declines) will be in trading strategies, moving beyond the standard assumption that returns follow a simple bell curve. The work matters because traders and fund managers need realistic estimates of worst-case losses to set proper risk limits—and assuming Gaussian returns significantly underestimates risk when returns have fat tails, skewness, or clustering (bunching) of volatility.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on **drawdown analysis**, a classical risk metric in finance that measures the decline from a strategy's peak value to its trough. Prior work by Rej, Seager and Bouchaud (2017) derived closed-form solutions for drawdown statistics under the assumption that returns are **Gaussian** (normally distributed). This work also connects to:
- **Brownian motion**: a continuous-time stochastic process where increments are independent and normally distributed—the mathematical foundation of most classical portfolio theory.
- **Sharpe ratio**: the excess return (above a risk-free rate) divided by volatility, a standard reward-to-risk measure.
- **Fractional Brownian motion (fBm)**: a generalization of Brownian motion that can exhibit memory (persistence), where past movements influence future ones.
- **Fat tails** and **skewness**: deviations from normality where extreme events are more likely or more negative than a bell curve predicts.
- **Volatility clustering**: periods when market swings are large tend to follow other large swings—a stylized fact in financial data.

## Core Contributions
1. **Monte-Carlo reformulation**: Translates the analytical results of Rej et al. into a transparent simulation framework, making the model more implementable and extensible.
2. **Four decision-relevant metrics**: Extends beyond maximum drawdown to also track maximum loss (largest single-day decline), final negative time (how long a strategy stays underwater), and longest recovery time (how long to get back to previous peak).
3. **Non-Gaussian extensions**: Empirically demonstrates that fat tails, skewness, and volatility clustering each move drawdown metrics *differently* from what Gaussian tables predict—a single lookup table is insufficient.
4. **Fractional Brownian motion analysis**: Shows that the apparent amplification of drawdown risk under long memory (persistence in returns) is largely an artifact of **self-similar scaling** (the T^(H−1/2) term), not genuine path geometry risk. This clarifies that the violation of square-root-of-time scaling is a property of the process itself, not a deeper danger.
5. **Practical tools**: Provides reproducible lookup tables and a calibration recipe for practitioners.

## Practical Applications
1. **Hedge fund risk limits**: Fund managers use drawdown caps (e.g., "strategy will not exceed 20% drawdown") to set allocation size; this paper provides realistic estimates for non-Gaussian strategies.
2. **Systematic trading strategy evaluation**: Quant traders backtest strategies and need to forecast expected maximum drawdown under stressed market conditions; the non-Gaussian framework better reflects real return distributions.
3. **Risk-adjusted performance reporting**: LPs (limited partners investing in funds) demand realistic worst-case scenarios; current Gaussian tables may underestimate risk in strategies with fat tails.
4. **Volatility clustering detection**: Traders can identify and adjust strategies that exhibit clustering using the framework's empirical comparisons.
5. **Portfolio diversification planning**: Knowing how long recovery takes helps allocate capital when one strategy is underwater, informing rebalancing schedules.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Risk dashboard SaaS**: A web tool where quant traders upload strategy return data, the system fits non-Gaussian parameters, and outputs personalized drawdown lookup tables and alerts—monetized per strategy or per user.
2. **Drawdown forecasting API**: A REST API that takes Sharpe ratio, volatility, skewness, and returns data, returns realistic max drawdown and recovery time distributions; integrate into portfolio management platforms.
3. **Institutional risk advisory**: Boutique consulting firm auditing hedge funds' published drawdown estimates (which often assume Gaussian) and providing corrected forecasts to LPs—capture LP due-diligence budgets.
4. **Automated strategy validation platform**: A tool that backtests trading strategies and flags if their drawdown assumptions match their empirical return distribution (Gaussian vs. non-Gaussian); sell to systematic trading firms.
5. **Volatility clustering detector & optimizer**: Software that identifies clustering in a strategy's returns and recommends portfolio adjustments or position-sizing changes to reduce drawdown amplification; monetize as a risk-engineering consulting tool.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Backward-looking calibration**: The framework requires historical return data to estimate skewness, kurtosis, and Hurst exponent; out-of-sample stability is not addressed (regimes change).
2. **Stationarity assumption**: The paper assumes return distributions are stationary (don't shift over time), which breaks during market structural breaks or regime changes.
3. **Model risk in fBm**: Fractional Brownian motion is a simplification; real markets may have multiple time-scales of memory, not captured by a single Hurst exponent.
4. **Copula structure not addressed**: The framework doesn't explicitly model the joint distribution of returns (e.g., does skewness correlate with drawdown direction?), which affects real-world correlations in multi-asset strategies.
5. **Computational cost**: Monte-Carlo simulation can be slow for high-frequency strategies or when calibrating across many parameter combinations; closed-form extensions remain limited.
6. **Sharpe ratio uncertainty**: The paper mentions Sharpe-estimation uncertainty but doesn't deeply explore how estimation error in Sharpe itself propagates to drawdown forecasts.

## Open Questions
1. **How do drawdown statistics change during regime transitions?** The paper assumes stationarity; what happens when volatility clustering intensity suddenly increases or skewness flips?
2. **Can a simpler parametric model (e.g., GARCH + student-t) capture the full benefit of the non-Gaussian extensions, or is Monte-Carlo necessary?**
3. **How do multivariate return distributions (across multiple strategies or assets) interact with drawdown risk?** Does diversification reduce drawdown depth/length as the model predicts?
4. **What is the optimal rebalancing or stop-loss rule given the forecasted drawdown distribution?** The paper predicts risk but doesn't prescribe optimal response.
5. **How does order execution and market impact (not captured in idealized returns) affect realized drawdowns?** Do real traders experience worse drawdowns because of slippage?
6. **Can machine learning improve non-Gaussian parameter estimation?** Current methods are classical statistical fits; could neural networks predict Hurst exponent or fat-tail index from limited data more robustly?

## Key Takeaway
Fat-tailed returns and memory in volatility fundamentally reshape drawdown forecasts; single Gaussian tables dangerously underestimate risk.
