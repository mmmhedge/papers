# Decentralized SGD under Heavy-Tailed Noise: Optimal Convergence Rates and the Role of Gradient Clipping

**Authors:** Aleksandar Armacki, Haoyuan Cai, Ali H. Sayed
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10527v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10527v1)

## Summary
This paper proves that decentralized stochastic gradient descent (DSGD) with gradient clipping can achieve optimal convergence rates when training on data with heavy-tailed noise — a common problem in real machine learning systems. The key insight is that clipping (truncating large gradient values) preserves important information better than normalization methods, allowing distributed learning across multiple agents to work reliably without sacrificing convergence speed.

## Theoretical Background
Decentralized learning distributes training across multiple agents (e.g., edge devices) that communicate locally rather than sending gradients to a central server. Heavy-tailed noise means that rare, extremely large gradient values occur more often than standard statistics predict — this breaks many classical optimization algorithms that assume bounded or Gaussian noise.

Prior work on decentralized optimization either used normalization (dividing by gradient magnitude) or clipping (capping gradient values), but neither approach was proven to converge at theoretically optimal rates under heavy-tailed noise. The paper's theoretical foundation rests on analyzing how local gradient updates (with clipping) propagate through a network of agents, carefully tracking the "consensus gap" — the divergence between local agent models and a hypothetical consensus model.

## Core Contributions
1. **Optimal convergence rates for clipped DSGD**: Proves that gradient clipping achieves order-optimal convergence under $p$-th moment bounded noise ($p \in (1,2]$), closing a gap in prior theory that showed clipping was suboptimal.

2. **Linear speedup with multiple agents**: Demonstrates that adding more agents proportionally speeds up convergence — a property not previously shown for decentralized methods using clipping.

3. **Sharp consensus gap analysis**: Develops new techniques to analyze how clipping affects the consensus error term in decentralized settings, showing that clipping's magnitude-preserving property prevents the consensus gap from dominating convergence.

4. **Clipping vs. normalization distinction**: Rigorously establishes that clipping and normalization behave fundamentally differently in decentralized settings — normalization can fail to converge while clipping succeeds.

## Connections to Other Work
This work refines the understanding of gradient clipping initiated by early robustness studies and extends [[stochastic gradient descent]] theory from centralized to distributed settings. It addresses limitations in prior decentralized non-convex optimization literature (e.g., works by Nedić, Boyd, and others on consensus-based methods) that either ignored heavy-tailed noise or required additional mechanisms like mini-batching or local momentum to converge.

The paper also relates to robustness in [[deep-learning]] training (where heavy-tailed gradients are empirically observed) and contributes to the intersection of optimization theory and [[multi-agent-systems]].

## Practical Applications
1. **Federated learning on edge devices**: Training models across smartphones, IoT sensors, or edge servers where communication is limited and gradient outliers are common (e.g., user-specific data anomalies).

2. **Decentralized training in wireless networks**: Distributed machine learning in scenarios without a central coordinator, where agents must rely on peer-to-peer communication (e.g., robotic swarms, sensor networks).

3. **Financial model training**: Collaborative learning across trading firms or institutions where data is sensitive and must remain local, while heavy-tailed returns are intrinsic to market data.

4. **Autonomous system coordination**: Multi-robot or multi-agent systems learning shared policies via local communication, where sensor noise can be heavy-tailed due to environmental outliers.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Smooth non-convex assumption**: Theory assumes smooth loss functions; does not directly address non-smooth objectives (e.g., ReLU networks with discrete phase transitions).

2. **Bounded moment requirement**: Requires $p$-th moment existence ($p \in (1,2]$); fails if noise has infinite variance or super-heavy tails beyond this regime.

3. **Network topology assumptions**: Analysis assumes fixed, connected communication graphs; dynamic topology changes (agent departure/arrival) not fully addressed.

4. **Clipping threshold selection**: Theory requires knowing or choosing an appropriate clipping level; practical guidance on threshold tuning in unknown noise regimes is limited.

5. **Scalability to very large networks**: While linear speedup is proven, experiments likely test modest agent counts; behavior with thousands of agents remains unclear.

6. **Synchronization**: Assumes synchronized local updates; asynchronous variants introduce additional complexities not analyzed here.

## Open Questions
1. Can similar guarantees extend to **asynchronous decentralized SGD** where agents update at different rates?

2. How does the method perform under **time-varying network topologies** (agents joining/leaving dynamically)?

3. Can the framework handle **non-smooth or composite objectives** (e.g., with $\ell_1$ regularization) common in sparse learning?

4. What is the **adaptive data-dependent clipping threshold** that minimizes convergence time without prior knowledge of noise distribution?

5. How do results transfer to **federated learning** settings with heterogeneous local data distributions (non-IID)?

6. Can clipping be **combined with other variance-reduction techniques** (SVRG, SAGA) in decentralized settings to further accelerate convergence?

## Key Takeaway
Clipped decentralized SGD provably converges optimally under heavy-tailed noise and scales linearly with agent count — better than normalization for distributed learning.
