# FriendBench: Benchmarking Dyadic Familiarity Inference in Humans and Multimodal Large Language Models

**Authors:** Jeffrey M. Girard, Jason Z. Zheng, Jacqueline R. Vertino, Antony D'Avirro, Benjamin Peloquin
**Published:** 2026-07-31 | **arXiv:** [2607.29602v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29602v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces FriendBench, a benchmark that tests whether AI models and humans can tell if two people already know each other or are strangers, based on a 20-second video of them having a conversation. The researchers find that the best AI models match human accuracy, but they get there differently — humans remain balanced in their judgments, while models tend to guess "stranger" more often. The work reveals that richer information (video beats audio beats text) helps both humans and AI, but only humans benefit from seeing body language in addition to hearing speech.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of social perception and multimodal AI. Social psychologists have long studied how humans infer relationships from nonverbal cues — facial expressions, posture, eye contact, and speech prosody all carry social information. Recent multimodal large language models (LLMs) like GPT-4V and Gemini can process text, audio, and video simultaneously, making them candidates for this kind of task. The benchmark methodology draws on comparative psychology: testing multiple modalities (text alone, audio, video) isolates which signals matter and whether different systems use information the same way. Accuracy measures alone miss important differences in *how* models and humans reach decisions — specifically, their "effective priors" or baseline tendency to predict one answer over another.

## Core Contributions
1. **FriendBench dataset**: 96 balanced dyads with controlled prompts, ensuring familiarity cannot be inferred from word choice — only from behavior. Includes human ratings and predictions from 26 models.
2. **Multimodal comparison framework**: Systematic ablation across text, audio, and video modalities for both humans and machines, revealing differential gains from richer channels.
3. **Prior analysis**: Demonstrates that top models and humans achieve equal accuracy but through different statistical strategies — models systematically bias toward "stranger," while humans maintain a balanced prior.
4. **Generalization insight**: Shows that only humans exploit visible behavior (body language, gesture) beyond speech information, suggesting models may miss crucial paralinguistic social signals.

## Connections to Other Work
This builds on work in [[Multimodal Learning|multimodal understanding]] and social signal processing, but applies it to a concrete, well-controlled inference task. It relates to broader questions in [[Large Language Models]] evaluation — whether achieving the same accuracy score means models understand the same way humans do. The finding about different priors echoes concerns in [[Alignment]] about whether AI systems and humans have different "default" assumptions. It also connects to [[Computer Vision]] work on action recognition and affect estimation from video, though this task requires joint reasoning over all modalities.

## Practical Applications
1. **HR and recruitment**: Pre-screening interviews to assess candidate comfort, social fit, or whether interviewers have unconscious bias toward seeming unfamiliar.
2. **Relationship counseling platforms**: Automated analysis of couples' interactions to identify relationship stage or attachment quality.
3. **Clinical psychology**: Assessing social withdrawal or isolation in depression/autism screenings by analyzing dyadic conversation quality.
4. **Sales training**: Analyzing whether salespeople build rapport differently with new vs. returning customers.
5. **Dating apps and matchmaking**: Analyzing initial video calls to estimate match quality or comfort level before deeper connection.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RelationshipAI**: A therapist-assistant tool that analyzes couples' conversation videos and flags behavioral patterns (withdrawal, defensiveness, synchrony) linked to relationship health outcomes.
2. **RecruitScreen**: An HR platform that runs blind audio/video interviews through FriendBench-like models to flag hiring bias or predict interview quality, de-biased by human-model comparison.
3. **SocialCoachAI**: A personal coaching app for neurodivergent users that analyzes their real interactions and provides real-time feedback on social reciprocity and familiarity-building.
4. **MeetingQualityMetrics**: Enterprise software for hybrid work that analyzes team video calls to measure psychological safety, trust, and whether remote participants feel included.
5. **DateAnalyzer**: A dating app feature that lets users see a brief automated readout of rapport signals from their first video chat, helping them reflect on connection quality.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Balanced dataset bias**: With 50/50 familiar/stranger splits, models may learn spurious patterns absent in real populations, where existing relationships dominate.
2. **Short clips**: 20-second clips may not capture longer-term rapport cues (shared humor, conflict resolution). Real relationships unfold over minutes.
3. **Modality fusion ceiling**: The paper does not explain *why* humans use body language more effectively than models. The mechanism — whether it's attention, features, or something deeper — remains opaque.
4. **Generalization across populations**: All dyads appear to be English-speaking. Cross-cultural familiarity cues (eye contact, personal space norms) vary widely.
5. **Prior mismatch**: Models lean "stranger" while humans balance. This bias could stem from training data imbalance or learned dataset shortcuts, but the paper doesn't isolate the cause or show how to correct it.
6. **Multimodal feature disentanglement**: It's unclear whether better performance from video comes from motion, appearance, or synchrony — all are conflated.

## Open Questions
1. Why do models fail to exploit body language the way humans do? Is this a feature extraction, attention, or architectural limitation?
2. Can you debias model priors to match human priors, and does that improve transfer to real-world imbalanced scenarios?
3. How do these patterns generalize across age, gender, and cultural groups? Are there reliable modality and prior differences?
4. What happens with longer clips (2–5 minutes) where rapport has time to develop? Do relative gaps shrink?
5. Can you train a model using human behavioral feedback (e.g., which frames humans attend to) to close the body-language gap?
6. Do the same modality and prior effects hold for other social inference tasks (attraction, deception, emotional congruence)?

## Key Takeaway
AI matches human accuracy at judging familiarity, but differs fundamentally in what signals it uses and its default assumptions about strangers.
