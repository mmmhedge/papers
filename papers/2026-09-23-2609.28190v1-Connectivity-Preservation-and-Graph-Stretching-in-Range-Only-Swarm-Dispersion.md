# Connectivity Preservation and Graph Stretching in Range-Only Swarm Dispersion

**Authors:** Ariel Barel
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28190v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28190v1)

## Summary
This paper solves a fundamental problem in multi-robot systems: how can robots spread out while staying connected to each other, using only distance measurements to nearby neighbors—without knowing bearings, having communication, or sharing a coordinate system? The authors prove that a simple rule (move halfway toward the edge of your sensing range, in a random direction) preserves connectivity and causes robots to disperse, with theoretical guarantees and extensive simulation validation.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on distributed multi-agent coordination, which seeks algorithms where simple identical agents achieve global objectives through local interactions. A key challenge is that in swarm robotics, agents typically have minimal sensing and no centralized control. Range-only sensing (measuring distances but not directions) is particularly constrained compared to full pose information. The work leverages graph connectivity theory—viewing robots as nodes where edges exist when agents are within sensor range—and random walk analysis to characterize convergence behavior. The idealized model assumes synchronous motion and perfect distance measurement, contrasting with most robotics work that addresses noise and asynchrony.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical characterization**: Derives the largest provably safe isotropic displacement from range-only measurements alone, proving it depends only on the farthest neighbor distance.
2. **Explicit dispersion rule**: Proposes a minimalist algorithm—move in random direction by half the remaining visibility margin—that provably preserves all existing connectivity edges.
3. **Two-agent analysis**: Proves positive conditional drift in squared distance, almost-sure boundary convergence, and estimates expected time to reach near-boundary separation (~9.5 rounds from coincident positions).
4. **General swarm validation**: Monte Carlo and Bellman equation methods confirm safety guarantees scale across different swarm topologies and identify topology-dependent diameter bounds.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to distributed algorithms for [[multi-agent-systems]] dispersion and flocking, where connectivity maintenance is a classical concern. It connects to [[graph-neural-networks]] thinking (local graph structure drives global behavior) and optimization under information constraints. The range-only model is a harder variant of problems typically solved with bearing information or communication channels. The work bridges theoretical distributed computing (where anonymity and minimal state are studied) with practical swarm robotics (where sensing is limited).

## Practical Applications
1. **Underground/subaquatic robot teams**: In GPS-denied environments (caves, underwater), range-only sensors (sonar, ultrasound) are more reliable than bearing estimates; spreading teams while preserving communication mesh.
2. **Search and rescue swarms**: Deploy many small drones/robots to cover an area; range-only measurements simplify hardware (no IMU/compass) while maintaining network connectivity for coordinated search.
3. **Satellite constellation deployment**: Satellites with only ranging transponders can disperse from deployment without full pose feedback.
4. **Micro-robot swarms**: In microscale robotics, directional sensing is difficult; this rule enables emergent spreading with minimal on-board sensing.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Swarm coordination firmware for budget drones**: Embed the dispersion algorithm in low-cost quadcopter flight controllers using only neighbor-distance measurements (via UWB beacons); sell as a library for multi-robot search/mapping startups.
2. **Autonomous underwater vehicle (AUV) fleet software**: Target oceanography and offshore inspection; provide connectivity-preserving formation dispersion for sonar-only sensing.
3. **Wireless sensor network deployment service**: Offer robots that auto-disperse while maintaining network backbone for IoT coverage in hard-to-reach areas (factories, mines); use range measurements for placement.
4. **Robotics-as-a-service for inspection**: Rent swarms that autonomously spread across inspection sites (power plants, bridges) with guaranteed comms connectivity, using only onboard ranging hardware.
5. **Safety certification tool for swarm algorithms**: Build a formal verification suite that validates swarm dispersion rules under range-only constraints; license to robotics companies for compliance and simulation.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Synchronous motion assumption**: Real robots have asynchronous, latency-prone actuators and sensors; breaks guarantees.
2. **Perfect distance measurement**: Assumes no noise or bias in ranging; real sonar/UWB has significant multipath and environmental error.
3. **Idealized visibility model**: Binary on/off at range boundary ignores gradual signal attenuation and sensor dropout.
4. **Isotropic displacement only**: Restricts moves to random directions; doesn't exploit bearing information if available, or allow anisotropic strategies.
5. **Scalability unstudied**: Theory holds for any swarm size, but simulations max at ~1,000 runs; behavior at thousands of agents unclear.
6. **No obstacle avoidance**: Assumes free space; collision with environment not modeled.
7. **Anonymous agents only**: Cannot implement differentiated roles or heterogeneous strategies.

## Open Questions
1. What convergence rates hold for asynchronous updates or delayed/noisy distance measurements?
2. Can tighter bounds on expected dispersion time be derived analytically (beyond Monte Carlo)?
3. How does the algorithm perform with **partial visibility** (e.g., only nearest-k neighbors) versus all-in-range neighbors?
4. Can agents coordinate bearing estimates *without communication* to improve dispersion, or does this violate the minimal-sensing principle?
5. What happens at network topology phase transitions (e.g., when density crosses a connectivity threshold)? Is there a critical regime?
6. How does finite precision in distance measurement degrade the connectivity guarantee, and what quantization schemes preserve safety?
7. Can hybrid strategies (mixing random and adversarial neighbor moves) be analyzed, and do they yield faster dispersion?

## Key Takeaway
Robots can safely spread out using only distance measurements to neighbors, with a simple rule that preserves connectivity and is proven to converge.
