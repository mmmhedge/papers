# Shared Gaussianization: What Gaussian Regularizers Certify About Contrastive Learning, and What They Miss

**Authors:** Ruoyu Zhao, Yuting Chen, Jinheng Zhang, Zhehao Zou, Tong Che
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10299v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10299v1)

## Summary
This paper analyzes what Gaussian regularizers (like those in LeJEPA) actually certify about contrastive learning methods such as InfoNCE. The authors introduce "shared Gaussianization" (SG), a statistical test that detects both misalignment and non-uniformity in learned representations, and prove that SG loss bounds InfoNCE loss with a dimension-free rate. The work reveals both what these regularizers guarantee and where they fall short—particularly how they can accidentally preserve unwanted per-view structure when not properly weighted.

## Theoretical Background
Contrastive learning (learning from pairs of similar and dissimilar examples) has become central to self-supervised representation learning. InfoNCE is a foundational contrastive loss that encourages aligned, uniformly-distributed representations across different "views" (e.g., augmented versions) of the same image. Recent methods like LeJEPA add Gaussian regularizers (distribution-matching terms) to stabilize learning, but it was unclear what theoretical guarantees these provide.

The paper builds on characteristic-function theory (Fourier-domain tests for statistical properties) and mean-embedding distance methods from kernel learning. The authors analyze these through the lens of alignment (views should agree) and uniformity (representations should spread uniformly on the sphere), two competing objectives in contrastive learning.

## Core Contributions
1. **Shared Gaussianization as a unifying test**: Proposes SG, which tests whether the average of two normalized views behaves like a Gaussian random variable scaled by an independent χ_d radius. This single test detects both misalignment and non-uniformity.

2. **Sharp excess bounds**: Proves that SG loss upper-bounds InfoNCE excess loss at a √(SG loss) rate with a dimension-free constant (4·3^(3/4)β), and proves this rate is optimal—no squared mean-embedding distance can do better.

3. **Characterization of when alignment helps**: Shows that rotation-invariant uniformity tests give linear bounds if and only if their spectral kernel dominates InfoNCE's exponential kernel e^(βu^T v). Identifies exactly which Gaussian kernels qualify.

4. **Mechanism for learning unwanted structure**: Demonstrates that pure SG without explicit alignment can actually minimize its loss by retaining per-view nuisance variation (style) when the shared code is non-uniform. Provides a critical alignment weight rule to prevent this.

5. **Batch-size effects and U-statistic bias**: Identifies how batch size affects the optimal alignment weight for LeJEPA and proposes an off-diagonal U-statistic correction for finite-sample bias.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[self-supervised learning]], [[contrastive learning]] theory, and [[kernel methods]]. It advances understanding of InfoNCE and relates to concurrent work on understanding uniformity and alignment trade-offs. The analysis of Gaussian regularizers connects to recent interest in distribution matching in [[representation learning]]. The paper implicitly critiques pure moment-matching objectives (which the analysis shows cannot achieve linear bounds) and validates exponential kernels like those in InfoNCE.

## Practical Applications
- **LeJEPA tuning**: Practitioners can use the critical SIGReg weight formula to set hyperparameters as a function of batch size, rather than via grid search.
- **Debugging learned representations**: The SG loss can be monitored during training to diagnose whether the model is learning unwanted per-view variation or true shared structure.
- **Representation auditing**: The characterization of which kernels qualify for linear bounds helps practitioners choose or design better regularizers.
- **Transfer learning validation**: Use SG loss to certify that a frozen encoder will transfer well (high alignment, high uniformity).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Automated representation quality scorer**: A library that computes SG loss and related diagnostics on trained vision models, certifying representation quality and predicting downstream task performance without fine-tuning.

2. **Contrastive learning hyperparameter optimizer**: A tool that automatically tunes alignment weights, batch sizes, and regularizer choices based on SG loss curves and the paper's theoretical rules, reducing hyperparameter search.

3. **Foundation model auditor**: SaaS platform that tests whether large pre-trained models have learned spurious per-view artifacts (style, batch effects) using SG analysis, then recommends re-training or fine-tuning fixes.

4. **Self-supervised learning as a service**: Managed service that trains custom contrastive encoders with principled SIGReg weighting, guaranteed to achieve certified uniformity/alignment levels.

5. **Representation drift detector**: Monitoring software for production ML systems that alerts when embeddings drift away from the SG-certified regime (e.g., due to distribution shift), triggering retraining.

## Technical Limitations
- **Theory requires equal marginals**: The linear excess bound assumes equal marginal distributions across views; real augmentations may violate this.
- **Optimality of SG is local**: The sharp rate applies at the aligned, uniform minimizer; away from it, SG and InfoNCE objectives diverge significantly, and the theory offers less guidance.
- **Finite-sample analysis incomplete**: While U-statistic corrections are proposed, full finite-sample complexity (sample complexity vs. dimension) is not fully characterized.
- **Gaussian assumption**: SG tests for Gaussianity, but this may not be the "right" test for all applications; the paper doesn't empirically validate that SG loss predicts downstream task performance.
- **Limited to vision setup**: Experiments are on image models; applicability to text, audio, or other modalities is unclear.
- **Optimization gap unexplained**: The paper observes that InfoNCE reaches lower SG loss than SG training from scratch, suggesting a fundamental optimization difficulty with pure SG that remains unresolved.

## Open Questions
1. Can the optimization gap between InfoNCE and pure SG training be closed? Is SG non-convex or ill-conditioned?
2. Does SG loss empirically predict transfer learning performance across diverse downstream tasks? How predictive is the bound in practice?
3. Can the analysis extend to more complex augmentations or non-equal marginals?
4. How does SG interact with batch normalization, which also imposes distributional constraints?
5. Can SG's theory guide the design of better regularizers beyond Gaussian kernels (e.g., learned kernels)?
6. Does SG generalize to contrastive methods beyond InfoNCE (e.g., SimCLR, MoCo variants)?

## Key Takeaway
Gaussian regularizers in contrastive learning guarantee alignment and uniformity with a sharp √loss rate, but must be carefully weighted to avoid learning unwanted per-view structure.
