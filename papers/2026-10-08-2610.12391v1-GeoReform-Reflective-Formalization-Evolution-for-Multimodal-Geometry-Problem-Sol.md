# GeoReform: Reflective Formalization Evolution for Multimodal Geometry Problem Solving

**Authors:** Jialu Wang, Ruichen Zhang, Xiaoou Liu, Hua Wei, Tianlong Chen
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.12391v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12391v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles a critical failure mode in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) — they struggle to understand geometric relationships in diagrams because converting visual information into text is error-prone and fragile. The authors propose GeoReform, a framework that treats textual formalization of geometry not as a one-shot parser output, but as a learnable policy that improves through trial-and-error on actual geometry problems, achieving 34% absolute accuracy gain on standard benchmarks.

## Theoretical Background
Multimodal LLMs like Qwen combine vision and language processing to reason over both images and text. Recent geometry-solving approaches convert visual diagrams into explicit textual descriptions (facts about objects, relations, constraints) so the language model can apply logical reasoning. This "formalization" step is inspired by classical AI symbolic reasoning — the intuition that making relationships explicit helps reasoning. However, the paper reveals a core problem: naive extraction of geometric facts creates noise (redundant or ambiguous statements) that actually hurts reasoning rather than helps it. The work builds on ideas from reinforcement learning and program synthesis — treating a non-differentiable process (formalization) as an optimizable policy through rollout-and-improve cycles.

## Core Contributions
1. **Diagnosis of formalization brittleness**: Empirical evidence that current structure injection methods introduce almost as many errors as they fix (28 fixed, 13 new errors per 200 examples on Geometry3K).
2. **GeoReform framework**: A reflective optimization loop that (a) executes reasoning with current formalization, (b) detects which formalizations caused failures, (c) mutates the formalization policy by adjusting how entities are selected, grounded, grouped, and presented.
3. **Concrete gains**: Improves Qwen3VL-2B from 42% to 56% accuracy on Geometry3K — a substantial 34-point lift.
4. **Generality**: Demonstrates that formalization quality is the bottleneck, not model capacity or reasoning depth.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[Prompt Engineering]] and [[In-Context Learning]] in LLMs — showing that how you structure input matters more than raw information content. It echoes ideas from [[RLHF]] (using feedback to improve outputs) and chain-of-thought prompting, but applied to the symbolic grounding layer rather than language generation. The reflective diagnosis-and-mutation approach is reminiscent of program synthesis and neuro-symbolic AI — bridging neural models with structured representations. Connects to broader challenges in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] of multimodal systems: why do models fail on visual reasoning, and how do we debug them?

## Practical Applications
1. **Educational software**: Automated tutoring systems for geometry and math that generate human-readable solutions to visual problems.
2. **Document understanding**: Parsing technical diagrams, engineering blueprints, and architectural drawings to extract actionable constraints and relations.
3. **Mathematical proof assistants**: Converting hand-drawn or scanned geometry proofs into formal logic that can be verified by proof checkers.
4. **Scientific figure interpretation**: Extracting structured information from diagrams in research papers (physics, chemistry, biology).
5. **Accessibility tools**: Converting visual math/geometry content into rigorous text for screen readers and blind users.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **GeoTutor Pro**: An AI tutoring app that solves geometry problems step-by-step from photos, explaining *why* each constraint matters — targeting high school / competition math students.
2. **DiagramParser API**: A commercial service that ingests technical diagrams (circuit schematics, flowcharts, CAD drawings) and outputs machine-readable structured data — for enterprise document automation.
3. **MathPaperMiner**: A research tool that crawls published papers, extracts all figures and diagrams, and builds a searchable database of geometric/mathematical patterns with executable code.
4. **SketchToCode**: A design tool for architects/engineers where freehand sketches are auto-converted to formal constraints and simulatable models.
5. **AccessibleMath**: A browser extension + API that detects math/geometry images in web content and generates screen-reader-friendly textual formalizations in real-time.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability of mutation search**: The paper doesn't discuss computational cost of the reflective loop — mutating and re-executing reasoning multiple times could be expensive at scale.
2. **Limited to geometry**: Approach is specialized to geometric reasoning; unclear if it generalizes to other diagram types (graphs, networks, flowcharts) or non-Euclidean reasoning.
3. **Depends on rich supervision**: Needs full reasoning traces and ground-truth solutions to diagnose failures — not clear how it performs with partial or weak feedback.
4. **Formalization still manual design space**: The paper defines a set of mutation operations (select, ground, group, present) but doesn't show how to automatically discover new ones; framework is somewhat constrained.
5. **Baseline MLLM brittleness**: Gains are strong but absolute accuracy (56%) is still modest; reflects limitations of underlying vision-language models, not just formalization.
6. **No long-tail reasoning**: Geometry3K may not capture complex multi-step proofs; unclear if method scales to competition-level hard problems.

## Open Questions
1. Can this reflective formalization approach transfer to other visual reasoning domains (chemistry, physics, networks)?
2. How sensitive is the method to the choice of mutation operators? Could learned or meta-learned mutations outperform hand-designed ones?
3. What is the sample efficiency? How many failed rollouts are needed to converge to good formalizations?
4. Does the method work with weaker supervision — e.g., only final correctness signal, no intermediate reasoning traces?
5. Can you combine this with recent advances in vision transformers to improve initial diagram understanding, or is formalization the true bottleneck?
6. How does performance scale with problem complexity and diagram density?

## Key Takeaway
Explicitly formalizing visual geometry for language models is error-prone; treating formalization as a learnable policy that improves from failure feedback is more effective than fixed parsers.
