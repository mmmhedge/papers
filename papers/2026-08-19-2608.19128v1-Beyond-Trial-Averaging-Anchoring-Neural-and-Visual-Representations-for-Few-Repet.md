# Beyond Trial Averaging: Anchoring Neural and Visual Representations for Few-Repetition Brain-to-Image Retrieval

**Authors:** Zhenyao Cui, Siyuan Kan, Dingkun Liu, Dongrui Wu
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.19128v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19128v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles brain-to-image retrieval — decoding what visual images someone is seeing from brain signals — with a focus on making it work with very few repetitions of the same stimulus. Current methods require averaging 80+ brain recordings per image, which is impractical in real settings; the authors show this performance drop with few repetitions isn't just noise, but also misalignment between representations, and propose NEAR (neural-anchor-based retrieval), a framework that uses high-repetition data as an anchor to guide both query denoising and image placement.

## Theoretical Background
Brain-to-image retrieval builds on the principle that visual cortex activity encodes information about perceived images. Recent approaches treat this as a retrieval problem: extract a neural representation from brain signals (EEG, MEG, or fMRI), then find the matching image in a gallery using similarity metrics. Prior work focused on trial averaging — collecting many repeated exposures of the same image and averaging the neural responses to reduce noise and improve signal stability. The paper introduces a concept from alignment research: "non-transitive alignment," where two entities can each align with a central reference but not directly with each other. This observation reframes the few-repetition problem: it's not purely a noise issue, but a representation geometry problem.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical insight**: Demonstrating that the few-repetition performance drop stems from both query noise *and* misaligned representation geometry (non-transitive alignment), not noise alone.
2. **NEAR framework**: A bidirectional anchoring approach using high-repetition center as reference: a denoiser network pulls noisy low-repetition queries toward the anchor, while a small predictor estimates each image's "pseudo-anchor" in the neural space and pulls image representations toward alignment.
3. **Consistent cross-modal gains**: Validation across four diverse datasets (EEG, MEG, fMRI) showing 5.7–9.3 percentage point improvements in top-1 accuracy on single/few-trial retrieval.
4. **Practical viability**: Reduces reliance on repeated stimulus acquisition, making real-world deployment feasible without the latency and user burden of multiple repetitions.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader challenges in [[domain-adaptation]] and representation alignment — similar problems arise in [[metric-learning]] and [[contrastive-learning]] where alignment between modalities (here: neural vs. image) is non-trivial. The trial-averaging baseline echoes classical signal processing denoising, but NEAR's dual-sided anchoring resembles manifold alignment techniques used in multi-view learning. It also connects to neuroscience-inspired deep learning (bridging neuroscience and vision), and indirectly to [[generative-models]] when considering future dream-decoding applications that might generatively reconstruct images from brain activity.

## Practical Applications
1. **Brain-computer interfaces (BCIs)**: Rapid image communication for locked-in patients or augmentative communication — decode intended visuals with minimal repetitions.
2. **Neuroscience research**: Faster, cheaper cognitive studies of visual perception — fewer trials needed to probe visual representation in the brain.
3. **Dream decoding**: Eventually enabling reconstruction of dream imagery with reduced overnight data collection burden.
4. **Rehabilitation**: Faster feedback in stroke or vision-loss recovery by decoding neural intent from fewer noisy samples.
5. **Sleep research**: Non-invasive monitoring of sleep stages and REM-associated imagery with single-trial fMRI/EEG snapshots.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **BCI communication platform**: A cloud-based service for ALS/locked-in patients where a lightweight EEG headset plus NEAR retrieval decodes intended images or text in near-real-time with minimal setup cost (vs. current 80-trial protocols).
2. **Cognitive research SaaS**: Web-based neuroscience experiment hosting that uses NEAR to reduce per-subject data collection 10–15×, lowering participant compensation and lab overhead.
3. **Wearable neuro-feedback device**: Consumer EEG headset paired with a smartphone app that decodes visual imagery on-the-fly for meditation or attention training, with no calibration burden.
4. **Sleep insight app**: Wearable combo (EEG + accelerometer) that logs sleep imagery in real-time, selling anonymized dream datasets to researchers or offering users a personalized "dream journal."
5. **Neuro-marketing analytics**: Lightweight in-store or streaming ad testing using NEAR to decode viewer attention/interest from minimal EEG samples, replacing costly eye-tracking or fMRI studies.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Dataset-specific tuning**: The anchor (high-repetition center) must be pre-computed; if test domains or stimuli differ significantly from training, anchor quality degrades.
2. **Modest absolute gains**: While 5–9 percentage-point improvements are meaningful, top-1 accuracy on 200-way retrieval is still ~15–20%, limiting immediate real-world confidence.
3. **Scalability to larger galleries**: All four datasets use relatively small image sets (e.g., 1000s of images); performance on truly large-scale galleries (100k+) is untested.
4. **Modality specificity**: Models trained on EEG may not transfer to MEG or fMRI without retraining; generalization across neuro-recording modalities is limited.
5. **Assumption of alignment**: The framework assumes a stable, learnable high-repetition center; this breaks if brain state drifts or if stimulus is novel/unfamiliar.
6. **Limited theoretical justification**: Why the pseudo-anchor predictor for images specifically helps is empirically shown but not rigorously justified; failure modes are unclear.

## Open Questions
1. **Can NEAR extend to zero-shot or cross-subject transfer?** Current work seems within-subject; can anchors learned on one person help another?
2. **How does NEAR scale to open-ended, unconstrained imagery** (not curated datasets like THINGS)?
3. **What is the minimal repetition threshold?** At what point does adding more trials yield diminishing returns?
4. **How sensitive is NEAR to anchor quality?** If the high-repetition center itself is noisy or biased, what degrades?
5. **Can we interpret the learned anchor geometry?** Do the pseudo-anchors capture semantically meaningful visual features, or are they task-specific artifacts?
6. **Generative extension**: Can NEAR anchor a generative model to reconstruct pixel-space images rather than just retrieve them?

## Key Takeaway
Anchoring neural and visual representations via high-repetition centers enables few-trial brain-to-image retrieval, removing the need for dozens of repetitions and bringing neural decoding closer to practical deployment.
