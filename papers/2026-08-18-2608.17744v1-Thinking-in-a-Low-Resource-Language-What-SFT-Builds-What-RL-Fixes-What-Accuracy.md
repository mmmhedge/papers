# Thinking in a Low-Resource Language: What SFT Builds, What RL Fixes, What Accuracy Cannot See

**Authors:** Ayoub Kirouane, Christos Petrocheilos
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.17744v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17744v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates what happens when you fine-tune large language models to reason in low-resource languages (Greek), and finds that standard accuracy benchmarks are too noisy to detect meaningful changes. Instead, the authors develop behavioral measurement techniques showing that supervised fine-tuning (SFT) successfully teaches models to reason *in* the target language rather than translate internally, while reinforcement learning (RL) fixes format violations and reasoning-channel leakage that SFT cannot address. The work matters because it reveals that model capability changes invisible to standard metrics are real and measurable — and that different training approaches fix different problems.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of three areas: (1) **language model adaptation**, which typically assumes that fine-tuning on task-specific data improves performance on task-specific benchmarks; (2) **mechanistic interpretability** — understanding what happens inside models rather than just measuring outputs — and (3) **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** and its variants, which use reward signals to steer model behavior beyond what supervised learning can achieve.

The paper builds on the observation that accuracy is often a poor proxy for what models actually do. It applies ideas from behavioral testing (measuring not just correctness but *how* models arrive at answers) and extends them to the multilingual reasoning setting. The authors rely on mixture-of-experts (MoE) models — networks with multiple specialized sub-networks that activate conditionally — which are increasingly standard in frontier LLMs.

## Core Contributions
1. **Null result as signal**: Demonstrates that accuracy benchmarks for low-resource language reasoning are below-noise (±7.7pp from seed variance alone), making traditional evaluation useless at this scale.

2. **Six behavioral dimensions**: Proposes measurement axes that capture meaningful changes SFT and RL achieve *beyond* accuracy — e.g., whether reasoning actually happens in the requested language, whether output format is respected, whether answers leak into reasoning channels. Critically, these metrics are gated to reject correlation with output length (a known confound).

3. **SFT's reach and limits**: Shows SFT reliably shifts models from 0% internal reasoning in Greek to ~98%, improving grammaticality and preserving base capability. But SFT cannot eliminate format skips (24%), reasoning leakage (3.5%), or instruction obedience gaps.

4. **RL's surgical fixes**: Demonstrates RL with pre-registered verifiable rewards fixes format and leakage problems almost completely (24% → 2.5%, 3.5% → 0%), with ablations (flat random-reward controls) confirming causality.

5. **Methodology**: Provides transferable instruments, control mechanisms, and pre-registration protocols for measuring behavioral change in any low-resource language setting.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends [[RLHF]] and related RL-in-LLMs frameworks by showing RL solves problems that SFT systematically *cannot* — a finding that challenges the assumption that better SFT alone suffices. It relates to work on **language model interpretability** and mechanistic probes (understanding what models encode internally), but focuses on behavioral rather than representational change.

The paper engages with the critique that accuracy metrics miss important model properties — a thread running through work on **model auditing**, **fairness evaluation**, and **robustness testing**. The emphasis on pre-registered reward design and control experiments echoes best practices in causal inference and replication science, rarely applied to LLM training.

It also touches on **multilingual and low-resource NLP**, though most work in that space assumes access to target-language data; this paper asks what you can do with minimal data and frontier models.

## Practical Applications
1. **Multilingual AI assistants**: Companies deploying models to non-English-speaking regions need to ensure internal reasoning happens in the user's language for trust and auditability. This work provides a concrete path and measurement toolkit.

2. **Compliance and safety**: Financial, legal, or medical AI systems must produce reasoning that auditors and regulators can read in their own language. SFT + RL protocols from this work enable that.

3. **Low-resource language tooling**: Developers building NLP tools for languages with scarce training data (e.g., endangered or minority languages) can apply these behavioral measures to verify that expensive fine-tuning actually worked.

4. **Model evaluation benchmarks**: Testing labs and model cards can adopt these behavioral dimensions instead of relying on accuracy-only leaderboards, catching real capability gaps that benchmarks miss.

5. **Fine-tuning-as-a-service**: Cloud platforms (e.g., fine-tuning APIs) can offer users these measurement tools to verify that custom fine-tuning actually changed model behavior as claimed.

## Product & Startup Ideas

2. **Low-Resource Language Model Fine-Tuning Service**: Specialized fine-tuning platform for non-English languages using the SFT + RL pipeline from this paper. Market to governments, NGOs, and companies serving underserved language communities.

3. **Reward Design and Pre-Registration Framework**: Tooling that helps teams write, version-control, and pre-register reward functions before training, reducing hidden degrees of freedom in RL. Sell to safety-conscious AI teams and regulated industries.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Language selection bias**: Greek is a written, literate language with adequate Unicode and tokenizer coverage. Results may not transfer to truly low-resource languages (endangered or minority languages with minimal digital text, poor tokenization, or script coverage issues).

2. **Mixture-of-experts specificity**: Findings come from 3.6–4.0B parameter MoE models (Alibaba, OpenAI, NVIDIA). Unclear whether patterns hold for dense models, much smaller models, or models orders of magnitude larger.

3. **Shallow RL evaluation**: The paper uses "verifiable rewards" (presumably deterministic, easy-to-check constraints like format compliance). It does not explore what happens with nuanced, subjective, or adversarially gamed rewards — a real-world challenge.

4. **No measurement of reasoning quality**: The paper confirms models *reason in Greek*, but does not validate whether that reasoning is *correct* or *auditable by humans*. A model can use the right language and still produce nonsense.

5. **Generalization to other tasks**: All experiments focus on reasoning/problem-solving tasks. Whether these SFT + RL patterns hold for generation, summarization, or open-ended dialogue is unstudied.

6. **Human evaluation costs**: Relying on judged grammaticality and behavioral assessment requires human annotation, limiting scale and reproducibility.

## Open Questions
1. **Why does RL fix what SFT cannot?** The paper documents the gap empirically but does not mechanistically explain why format compliance and instruction obedience require RL gradients specifically. Is it a gradient flow issue, a representational one, or something about the loss landscape?

2. **Can pre-registered rewards generalize?** The paper pre-registers rewards for Greek reasoning. Do those same reward specifications work for other languages, or must they be re-tuned per language/task?

3. **What about dense models and scale?** Do the same behavioral gaps (24% format skips, 3.5% leakage) appear in dense 7B, 70B, or larger models? Do they shrink with scale?

4. **Human validation of behavioral metrics**: How well do the six proposed dimensions correlate with human judgments of "good reasoning" or "trustworthy output"? Are there behavioral metrics the paper missed?

5. **Adversarial robustness of the RL fix**: Once a model learns to format correctly and avoid leakage, can adversarial prompts or in-context examples corrupt that behavior? Is the RL-induced capability robust?

6. **Cost-benefit of RL**: SFT is cheaper than RL. For a given resource budget, is SFT-only sometimes better than SFT + RL? What's the decision boundary
