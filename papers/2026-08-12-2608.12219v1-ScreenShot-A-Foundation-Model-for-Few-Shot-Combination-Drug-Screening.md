# ScreenShot: A Foundation Model for Few-Shot Combination Drug Screening

**Authors:** Antoine de Mathelin, Christopher Tosh, Wesley Tansey
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12219v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12219v1)

## Summary
ScreenShot is a foundation model that predicts how cancer cell samples will respond to drug combinations, trained on 40 publicly available screening datasets. Instead of requiring expensive lab measurements or per-patient retraining, it uses a hierarchical transformer to make predictions from just a few observations of a new patient's cells, and can even suggest which drug combinations to test next to save time and resources.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on recent advances in foundation models — large neural networks pretrained on broad datasets that can adapt to new tasks with minimal additional training. It specifically uses transformers (neural networks that process sequences using attention mechanisms to weigh the importance of different inputs) organized in a hierarchical structure that mirrors how drug screening data is naturally nested: multiple drugs tested on multiple cell samples from multiple patients. The in-context learning approach (where a model learns from a few examples shown at inference time, without parameter updates) draws from successes in large language models like GPT, but applied here to biomedical data. The active learning component (selecting which experiments to run to maximize information gain) is a classical optimization strategy for reducing experimental costs.

## Core Contributions
1. **Hierarchical transformer architecture** designed specifically for nested drug screening data structure (patient → sample → drug combinations), rather than applying generic architectures.
2. **Few-shot in-context learning** for drug response prediction that requires no fine-tuning, no molecular profiling, and works directly from functional measurements (raw experimental readouts).
3. **Large-scale pretraining** on 40 integrated datasets covering 3,700 drugs and 6,000 samples, creating the first foundation model for drug combination screening.
4. **Active learning integration** using learned representations to guide experimental design, achieving equivalent hit detection with one-third of the screening budget.
5. Empirical validation on held-out datasets showing superiority over existing methods in both prediction accuracy and identification of selective treatments.

## Connections to Other Work
This work parallels the success of foundation models in NLP ([[Transformers]], [[BERT]], [[GPT]]) and computer vision, extending that paradigm to a new domain: biomedical high-throughput screening. It relates to meta-learning and few-shot learning literature (learning to learn quickly from limited data) and builds on in-context learning mechanisms now standard in large language models. The active learning component connects to optimal experimental design and uncertainty quantification in machine learning. Unlike traditional drug discovery pipelines that rely on molecular structure or genetic profiling, ScreenShot operates on *functional* readouts, sidestepping expensive molecular characterization — this is conceptually similar to black-box optimization approaches that don't require explicit feature engineering.

## Practical Applications
1. **Personalized cancer combination therapy**: Clinicians use ScreenShot to predict optimal drug pairs for a patient's tumor in days rather than weeks, based on a small patient-derived sample.
2. **Cost reduction in drug screening**: Pharmaceutical companies reduce screening budgets by 60-70% through active learning recommendations, testing only the most informative combinations.
3. **Resistance management**: Hospitals treating drug-resistant infections (bacterial, fungal, parasitic) rapidly identify effective combination therapies without waiting for full susceptibility panels.
4. **Clinical trial design**: Researchers use ScreenShot's predictions to pre-screen promising combination therapies, reducing the number of arms needed in early-phase trials.
5. **Rare disease treatment**: For patient populations too small to support per-cohort machine learning, ScreenShot generalizes across cohorts to predict responses immediately.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Pretraining data bias**: Model is only as good as the 40 datasets it saw; rare cancer types, cell lines, or drug classes not well-represented will generalize poorly.
2. **No mechanistic understanding**: ScreenShot predicts *that* a combination works, not *why* — it cannot explain the molecular basis for synergy, limiting interpretability for clinicians and drug designers.
3. **In vitro to in vivo gap**: All training data are cell-based assays; predictions may not translate to whole-organism pharmacokinetics, toxicity, or patient heterogeneity.
4. **Limited context length**: Hierarchical transformer has capacity constraints; cannot leverage very large numbers of prior observations per patient, unlike unlimited in-context learning.
5. **Active learning assumes static distribution**: The weighted k-means strategy assumes the distribution of effective combinations doesn't shift; fails if a patient has unusual resistance patterns or an untested drug class is introduced.
6. **Combination explosion**: Even with active learning, the space of possible 3+ drug combinations is exponential; model handles pairs well but higher-order synergies are not systematically explored.

## Open Questions
1. **Mechanistic interpretability**: Can we extract interpretable rules or pathway annotations from ScreenShot's representations that explain *why* specific combinations synergize? (Relates to [[mechanistic-interpretability]])
2. **Transfer to in vivo**: How much do predictions degrade when moving from cell assays to mouse xenografts or patient-derived xenografts? What adaptation is needed?
3. **Temporal dynamics**: Can ScreenShot model how drug resistance evolves over time, and predict which combinations delay resistance longest?
4. **Multi-patient pooling**: Should the few-shot context include data from similar patients, or does that hurt generalization? How do we measure "similar"?
5. **Higher-order combinations**: Do the learned representations scale to triple or quadruple drug combinations, or does the model lose signal?
6. **Robustness to distribution shift**: How does ScreenShot perform on datasets from a new cell line, tissue type, or institution not seen during pretraining?

## Key Takeaway
Foundation models pretrained on large drug screening datasets can predict effective cancer drug combinations for new patients using only a few observations, with no per-patient retraining.
