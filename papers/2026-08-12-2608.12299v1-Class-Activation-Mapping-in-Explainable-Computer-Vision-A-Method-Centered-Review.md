# Class Activation Mapping in Explainable Computer Vision: A Method-Centered Review of CNN, Transformer, and Foundation-Model-Era Visual Explanations

**Authors:** AmirHossein Eshghi, Hamid Saadatfar, Seyyed Ali Hoseini, AmirMohsen Eshghi, Siavash Arjomand Bigdel
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12299v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12299v1)

## Summary
This paper reviews Class Activation Mapping (CAM) — a family of techniques that explain what parts of an image a visual AI model is "looking at" when making a decision — from its 2016 origins through modern foundation models like CLIP and SAM. The review synthesizes 57 papers and shows how the field has evolved from simple heatmaps on CNNs to sophisticated multi-layer, probabilistic, and token-aware explanations, while identifying fragmentation in how these methods are evaluated.

## Theoretical Background
Class Activation Mapping originated from the observation that convolutional neural networks (CNNs — image-processing neural networks that learn hierarchical visual features) learn to localize objects without pixel-level supervision. Early CAM converted the final layer's evidence into a spatial heatmap by weighted-averaging feature maps. The field has since built on multiple conceptual foundations: gradient-based attribution methods (computing how input changes affect output), attention mechanisms (used in transformers — neural networks that process sequences by computing relationships between all elements), and ablation studies (systematically removing parts to measure their importance). Foundation models like CLIP (which learns joint vision-language representations) and DINO (which learns self-supervised visual features) introduced new architectures requiring explanation methods adapted to their token-based and multi-modal structure.

## Core Contributions
The paper's primary contribution is a structured taxonomy organizing 57 CAM-family methods across three dimensions: (1) attribution mechanism (gradient-based, score-based, ablation-based, etc.), (2) architectural dependence (CNN-specific vs. transformer-aware vs. foundation-model compatible), and (3) evaluation objective (localization, faithfulness, robustness, etc.). Beyond taxonomy, the review identifies the core historical trend: movement from explaining a single class score in one CNN layer to comparative, multi-layer, probabilistic explanations that handle transformer tokens and foundation-model features. The paper also explicitly documents evaluation fragmentation — different papers use different protocols for faithfulness, localization quality, and human trust — and maps which later methods address gaps left by earlier work.

## Connections to Other Work
This review sits at the intersection of [[mechanistic interpretability]] (understanding how neural networks make decisions) and explainable AI (XAI). CAM methods relate to broader attribution work like LIME (local linear approximations), SHAP (game-theoretic explanations), and saliency maps, though CAM is specialized for visual and layer-wise explanation. The evolution toward transformer and foundation-model explanations connects to recent work on understanding [[transformers]] and [[attention is all you need]]-style models. The paper also touches on weakly supervised learning and object localization, where CAM-style heatmaps serve as implicit supervision signals rather than pure explanation tools.

## Practical Applications
- **Medical image diagnosis**: Clinicians use CAM heatmaps to verify that models focus on relevant anatomical structures (tumors, lesions) rather than spurious correlations, building trust before clinical deployment.
- **Autonomous vehicle safety**: Visualizing which image regions drive steering or braking decisions helps engineers debug failure modes and regulators validate model behavior.
- **Object detection for quality control**: Manufacturing systems use CAM to confirm that defect-detection models attend to actual defects rather than background features.
- **Content moderation**: Social platforms apply CAM to understand why models flagged images, aiding appeals and model improvement.
- **Bias detection and mitigation**: Researchers use CAM heatmaps to reveal if models exploit demographic or contextual shortcuts rather than task-relevant features, enabling debiasing interventions.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Model Audit SaaS for Computer Vision**: A cloud platform that automatically generates CAM reports and evaluation metrics (faithfulness, robustness, localization agreement) for any uploaded model and dataset, helping companies certify models before deployment in regulated domains (healthcare, autonomous driving).

2. **Interactive Model Debugging IDE**: A development tool for vision engineers that displays real-time CAM heatmaps alongside model predictions, allowing live manipulation of images and immediate feedback on how explanations shift — reducing iteration time for model improvement.

3. **Bias Testing & Certification Service**: Specializing in running adversarial CAM analysis — generating synthetic or perturbed images to expose if models rely on spurious correlations — targeting enterprises in finance, hiring, and criminal justice.

4. **Foundation Model Explanation API**: A wrapper service over CLIP, DINO, SAM that provides native, tokenized CAM explanations out-of-the-box, marketed to companies building vision applications on foundation models who lack internal XAI expertise.

5. **Legal/Regulatory Compliance Reports**: Automated system generating human-readable CAM visualizations and faithfulness certifications for model-driven decisions subject to EU AI Act or similar regulations, bundled with legal templates.

## Technical Limitations
- **Evaluation fragmentation**: There is no universal protocol for measuring explanation quality. Faithfulness, localization, robustness, and human trust are measured inconsistently across papers, making it hard to compare methods or choose one for a specific application.
- **Post-hoc vs. inherent trade-off**: Most CAM methods are post-hoc (applied after training); they don't guarantee the model's decision actually depends on the highlighted regions — a highlighted region might be correlated but not causal.
- **Computational cost under-reported**: Many papers omit the computational overhead of generating explanations, making it unclear which methods are practical for real-time or large-scale systems.
- **Foundation model instability**: CLIP and similar models were not designed with explainability in mind; token-level CAM on these models is still experimental and may not reflect true decision logic.
- **Evaluation dataset bias**: Most CAM papers evaluate on standard benchmarks (ImageNet); performance on out-of-distribution or long-tailed datasets remains unclear.
- **Human evaluation scarcity**: Few papers include human studies validating that heatmaps actually improve user trust or decision-making, undermining claims of practical utility.

## Open Questions
- How can we develop a unified evaluation protocol that practitioners can agree on, making CAM methods directly comparable?
- Do CAM heatmaps actually improve human decision-making and trust in deployed systems? Where is the rigorous evidence?
- Can we develop CAM methods that certify *causality* rather than mere correlation — i.e., prove the model would change its decision if the highlighted region were removed?
- How do CAM methods scale to very high-resolution images (satellite, pathology slides) without prohibitive computational cost?
- What is the relationship between CAM faithfulness and model robustness to adversarial perturbations?
- Can gradient-free, foundation-model-aware CAM methods match the resolution and interpretability of gradient-based CNNmethods?

## Key Takeaway
Visual explanation methods have evolved from simple CNN heatmaps to sophisticated multi-layer, token-aware explanations for modern foundation models, but lack consensus on how to evaluate them.
