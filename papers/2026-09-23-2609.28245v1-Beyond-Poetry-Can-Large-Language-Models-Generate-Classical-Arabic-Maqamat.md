# Beyond Poetry: Can Large Language Models Generate Classical Arabic Maqamat?

**Authors:** AbdulRahman A. Morsy, Aya Zirikly
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28245v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28245v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can generate maqama—a classical Arabic literary form defined by intricate rhymed prose, dense rhetorical ornamentation, and episodic structure. The authors conduct the first systematic evaluation of five LLMs using different prompting strategies and find that few-shot and rule-based prompting significantly improve stylistic quality, though no model fully masters the form's conventions.

## Theoretical Background
Large language models are neural networks trained on vast text corpora that can generate fluent text across many domains. Prior work has established that LLMs excel at surface-level fluency and poetry generation, but their ability to capture culturally specific, formally constrained literary traditions—where syntax, rhythm, and semantic density matter equally—remains poorly understood. The maqama is a classical Arabic prose form that demands not just grammatical correctness but mastery of saj (rhymed prose), rhetorical devices, and narrative episodicity. Evaluating whether models can internalize such deep stylistic constraints moves beyond measuring fluency toward assessing genuine literary competence.

## Core Contributions
- **First systematic maqama evaluation study**: This is the first controlled comparison of LLM-generated maqama across multiple models and prompting strategies (zero-shot, few-shot, rule-based).
- **Multi-dimensional evaluation framework**: The authors measure performance on four orthogonal dimensions—rhetorical richness, saj density, structural coherence, and stylistic authenticity—using both human annotation and LLM-as-judge methods.
- **Quantified prompting effects**: The paper shows that prompting strategy is model-dependent; few-shot improves saj density consistently, while rule-based prompting benefits stronger models (GPT-4o, GPT-5.4-mini) on rhetoric and coherence.
- **Calibration with non-LLM proxies**: Results are validated against independent LLM judges and non-neural metrics of saj, reducing bias from single evaluation sources.

## Connections to Other Work

## Practical Applications
- **Digital humanities and Arabic literary scholarship**: Automated maqama generation could aid scholars in understanding stylistic variation, archival indexing, or pedagogical tools for learning classical Arabic rhetoric.
- **Cultural AI systems**: Museums, libraries, or cultural institutions could use LLMs to generate maqama-inspired content for exhibits or educational materials about Islamic literary heritage.
- **Machine translation and style transfer**: Insights about constrained prose generation may improve translation of classical Arabic texts or style-preserving machine translation.
- **Interactive storytelling**: Generative narratives rooted in classical forms could enhance cultural storytelling games or virtual experiences.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ArabicLit Studio**: A web platform that generates classical Arabic literary forms (maqama, qasida) from user prompts, with explainable analysis of rhetorical and structural quality for scholars and writers.
2. **Cultural Language Model Fine-Tuning Service**: A B2B tool that helps institutions fine-tune LLMs on classical texts from specific cultural traditions, enabling high-fidelity generation tailored to regional literary standards.
3. **Automated Maqama Annotation Engine**: Software that scores existing maqama texts on saj density, rhetoric, coherence, and authenticity—useful for literary databases, educational platforms, and archival projects.
4. **Interactive Maqama Writing Assistant**: A creative writing tool where authors compose maqama with real-time feedback on rule compliance, saj density, and stylistic alignment with classical conventions.
5. **Benchmark & Leaderboard for Classical Text Generation**: A public evaluation suite and leaderboard for testing LLMs on non-modern, culturally constrained literary forms across Arabic, Persian, Hebrew, and other traditions.

## Technical Limitations
- **Model-dependent results**: Findings on prompting strategy effectiveness are tied to specific model architectures and versions; generalization to future models is uncertain.
- **Saj measurement proxy limitations**: Non-LLM proxies for saj may not fully capture the form's phonetic and semantic subtleties; human annotation is limited in scale.
- **Lack of native speaker diversity**: Human evaluations likely involve a small number of raters; inter-rater reliability on stylistic authenticity for a specialist form may be understated.
- **Short-horizon evaluation**: The paper evaluates output quality but not whether models genuinely internalize maqama structure or are merely pattern-matching; longer, more complex narratives may expose shallower understanding.
- **No comparison to human-authored baselines**: Without human-generated maqama in the evaluation set, it is difficult to judge whether LLM outputs approach expert-level performance.

## Open Questions
- Do stronger models exhibit emergent understanding of maqama rules, or are they simply better at following surface cues in prompts?
- How do model performance and prompting effects scale with maqama length and narrative complexity?
- Can fine-tuning on classical Arabic corpora outperform prompt engineering, and what is the data efficiency trade-off?
- Do the same prompting strategies transfer to other classical literary forms (e.g., Persian poetry, Quranic exegesis)?
- Can LLM evaluation metrics (like GPT-as-judge) be validated against expert human literary critics, or do they introduce systematic biases?
- How do multilingual models (e.g., trained on Arabic and English) differ in maqama generation quality compared to Arabic-focused models?

## Key Takeaway
Large language models can improve classical Arabic maqama generation through prompting, but no model yet masters its formal constraints without external guidance.
