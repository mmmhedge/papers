# Interpretable EEG biomarkers with bag-of-waves: Spatial and temporal waveform dictionaries for low-data regimes

**Authors:** Athanasios Papastathopoulos-Katsaros, Steven T. Lee, Lin Yao, Ajay Thomas, Junseok Park et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22508v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22508v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces bag-of-waves, a simple and interpretable method for analyzing EEG brain signals that learns a small dictionary of recurring waveform patterns without labeled data. Unlike deep neural networks that are black boxes or hand-crafted features that embed researcher bias, bag-of-waves recovers clinically meaningful brain wave shapes that neurologists can directly understand — and it works well even with very small datasets (e.g., 16 animals).

## Theoretical Background
EEG analysis typically follows one of two paths: (1) experts manually define spectral features (like alpha waves or theta rhythms) that they believe are informative, but this embeds strong assumptions about what matters; or (2) researchers train deep neural networks that learn features automatically but are opaque and data-hungry. This work builds on shift-invariant k-means — a classical unsupervised clustering method that finds repeated patterns in sequences while being robust to small shifts in time. The key insight is that EEG waveforms are inherently repetitive and localized; instead of learning millions of parameters (as transformers do), you can represent the entire signal as a sequence of tokens, where each token is a reference to one of a small set of learned atom templates. The method extends this with n-grams (short sequences of atoms) to capture temporal dependencies, and with spatial atoms that combine information across brain regions.

## Core Contributions
1. **Bag-of-waves framework**: A minimal, unsupervised method that learns interpretable waveform atoms via shift-invariant k-means, turning continuous EEG into discrete token sequences suitable for simple downstream classifiers.
2. **Temporal extension (n-grams)**: Atom transitions capture temporal structure in EEG, moving beyond bag-of-words-style frequency counting.
3. **Spatial atoms**: Extension from single-channel to regional and cross-channel atoms for multichannel EEG, preserving spatial information without architectural complexity.
4. **Low-data validation**: Competitive performance with state-of-the-art deep and foundation models on three diverse benchmarks — including a 16-animal genotype clustering task where heavy models would typically fail.
5. **Clinical interpretability**: Every atom is a visualizable waveform that can be validated against known clinical morphologies (e.g., sleep spindles, seizure bursts).

## Connections to Other Work
Bag-of-waves sits between classical signal processing (e.g., wavelet decomposition, Fourier analysis) and modern deep learning. It relates to [[mechanistic-interpretability]] work in that it prioritizes understanding *what* the model learned (actual waveforms) over *how* it learned them. The shift-invariant k-means core echoes dictionary learning and sparse coding methods. In contrast to [[transformers]] and large foundation models that require massive data and compute, this approach aligns with the growing interest in sample-efficient, interpretable alternatives — similar to recent work on prototype networks and concept-based models in vision. It also complements [[RLHF]]-style human-in-the-loop approaches by producing outputs (atoms) that humans can directly validate without needing to reverse-engineer a neural network.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical neurology**: Rapid screening for epilepsy, sleep disorders, and dementia using EEG — especially in under-resourced settings where large labeled datasets and GPU infrastructure are unavailable.
2. **Neonatal and pediatric monitoring**: Low-data regimes (e.g., monitoring 10–20 infants with atypical brain activity) where interpretability and sample efficiency are critical.
3. **Drug development**: EEG biomarker discovery for novel neurological treatments, where researchers need to understand *which* waveform changes correlate with efficacy, not just get a prediction.
4. **Wearable EEG devices**: Lightweight, interpretable inference on battery-constrained wearables (consumer headsets, hospital monitors) — atoms are tiny compared to neural networks.
5. **Neuroscience research validation**: Confirming that learned atoms match known electrophysiology (e.g., known patterns from animal models), bridging computational and classical neuroscience.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **EEG-as-a-Service for rural clinics**: SaaS platform that ships a lightweight, pretrained bag-of-waves model to low-bandwidth clinics; users upload local EEG files, get instant dementia or seizure risk scores, plus a report listing which atoms triggered the alert (fully interpretable for non-AI experts).
2. **Wearable sleep & stress monitor**: Consumer device (headband or earpiece) that learns personalized waveform atoms from a user's baseline EEG, detects deviations in real time, and alerts them to sleep stage transitions or stress spikes — no cloud required.
3. **Clinical decision support for neurologists**: Plug-in software for hospital EEG systems that highlights clinically relevant atoms found in a patient's recording and cross-references them against a database of known pathologies (seizure bursts, sharp-wave complexes, etc.), accelerating diagnosis.
4. **Federated EEG diagnostics**: Privacy-preserving multi-site diagnostic network where hospitals train a shared atom dictionary on their own data without sending raw EEG elsewhere, then use that dictionary for classification — critical for HIPAA compliance.
5. **Biomarker discovery platform for pharma**: Automated pipeline to identify and validate EEG atom signatures predictive of drug response in clinical trials, reducing time to reliable biomarker approval.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Atom interpretability is not guaranteed**: While atoms *can* be visually inspected, there is no guarantee they align with clinically known morphologies — domain expert review is still required, and some learned atoms may be noise or artifact.
2. **Scalability to very high-dimensional multichannel EEG**: The paper focuses on a handful of channels; extending spatial atoms to 64+ electrode grids may require careful design to avoid combinatorial explosion.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The number of atoms, n-gram order, and spatial grouping are not learned; they require manual tuning, which partially re-introduces the bias problem the method aims to avoid.
4. **Artifact handling**: EEG is notoriously noisy (muscle activity, eye blinks, 60-Hz powerline interference); the paper does not detail artifact removal or robustness. Learned atoms may capture artifacts if preprocessing is weak.
5. **No temporal alignment mechanism**: Shift-invariant k-means tolerates small time shifts, but large jitter or variable latencies in waveform onsets might not be handled gracefully.
6. **Comparison fairness**: The deep baselines tested may not have been hyperparameter-tuned for small data; a more rigorous low-data baseline (e.g., pre-trained foundation model with lightweight fine-tuning) would strengthen claims.

## Open Questions
1. **Atom discovery dynamics**: How do learned atoms change as you add more data? Is there a principled way to detect when the atom dictionary has converged or become redundant?
2. **Spatial atom validation**: Do learned spatial atoms actually correspond to known brain networks (default-mode network, motor cortex, etc.) or are they purely statistical correlations? Could atlas-guided initialization improve interpretability?
3. **Cross-subject and cross-species generalization**: Can atoms learned from one patient or animal population transfer to another? Is there a universal EEG "language" or do atoms need to be patient-specific?
4. **Pathology-specific atoms**: Can you enforce constraints during atom learning (e.g., "find atoms enriched in seizure records but absent in controls") to directly discover diagnostic signatures?
5. **Temporal modeling beyond n-grams**: Would a Markov chain or HMM over atoms capture EEG state transitions more naturally than fixed n-grams?
6. **Artifact and noise robustness**: How does atom quality degrade with different EEG recording equipment, electrode placement errors, or presence of muscle artifact? Can you automatically detect and exclude noisy atoms?

## Key Takeaway
Interpretable EEG analysis via learned waveform atoms achieves competitive performance in low-data regimes while remaining transparent to neurologists.
