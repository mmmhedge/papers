# Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies

**Authors:** Peter Kirgis, Sayash Kapoor, Andrew Schwartz, Stephan Rabanser, David Africa et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **arXiv:** [2607.27191v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27191v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether AI agents can conduct genuine open-ended research by having frontier AI systems tackle unpublished research questions from real NeurIPS papers, with the original authors grading the results. The agents successfully handled engineering tasks but failed to make meaningful progress on the core research questions, revealing critical gaps in how agents approach complex, unstructured research problems.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on growing interest in AI-assisted R&D automation — the idea that AI systems could accelerate or replace human researchers. Current evaluation methods fall into two camps: narrow benchmarks (which test specific, well-defined tasks but miss open-ended research) and blind peer review of AI-generated papers (which is noisy, slow, and unreliable). The work also draws on agent research — systems that use language models to plan, execute, and iterate on complex tasks over multiple steps. Prior work on [[RLHF]] and AI capability measurement provides context for why measuring true research capability is hard.

## Core Contributions
1. **Shadow evaluation methodology**: A novel evaluation approach where agents tackle open research questions and are graded by the original (expert) authors. This avoids the noise of peer review while being genuinely open-ended.
2. **Two in-depth case studies**: Concrete evidence from two real NeurIPS 2026 papers showing where frontier agents succeed (engineering, implementation) and fail (research judgment, creative problem-solving).
3. **Five failure modes identified**: Poor judgment about publishability, uncreative responses to design flaws, ineffective backtracking, weak resource management, and instruction drift — specific, recurring patterns beyond generic "agent incompetence."
4. **Reproducibility artifacts**: Full release of expert reviews, agent logs, and repositories, enabling future work to diagnose and improve agent research capability.

## Connections to Other Work
This work directly challenges common forecasts about explosive AI progress through autonomous R&D (e.g., speculations about AI agents replacing researchers). It complements narrow benchmarks like [[ARC]] or code-generation tasks by showing that engineering competence does not translate to research competence. The failure modes relate to broader agent robustness issues studied in the [[alignment]] and mechanistic reasoning literature — agents struggle with metacognition (knowing when they've failed) and creative reframing, not just execution. The methodology also echoes human-in-the-loop AI evaluation, but with the twist that humans are grading agents on their own work.

## Practical Applications
1. **AI research infrastructure tools**: Targeting the gap in "research judgment" — platforms that help human researchers use AI for engineering while keeping humans in the loop for conceptualization and validation.
2. **Research process optimization**: Identifying bottlenecks in AI research workflows that could be semi-automated (e.g., hyperparameter sweeps, literature review) while flagging tasks requiring human judgment.
3. **Training evaluation systems**: Using shadow evaluations to benchmark frontier models' research capability, providing a more meaningful signal than narrow benchmarks for labs evaluating internal models.
4. **Reproducibility and code auditing services**: Leveraging agents' strength in engineering to help researchers debug, refactor, and document code in existing papers.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"Research Copilot"**: An IDE-like environment where human researchers direct AI agents to execute engineering subtasks (experiments, ablations, hyperparameter searches), with explicit checkpoints where humans review findings before proceeding — monetize as a subscription for labs.
2. **"AI Research Grader"**: A service that runs frontier agents on unpublished preprints and generates shadow evaluations (using author feedback loops) to give researchers honest, early feedback on whether their contribution is substantial.
3. **"Experiment Automation as a Service"**: A platform that translates research papers into executable experiment templates, then uses agents to run variants (different datasets, architectures, hyperparameters) and summarize results — useful for replication and robustness analysis.
4. **"Research Dead-End Detector"**: A tool that monitors agent progress in real time and flags when an agent is thrashing (cycling through similar failed approaches) or exhausting compute, alerting the human researcher to intervene and reframe.
5. **"Post-hoc Research Auditing"**: A service where agents re-run published research to verify claims, identify implementation bugs, and generate detailed reports — valuable for publishers and meta-science initiatives.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited sample size**: Only two case studies. Patterns may not generalize across domains (vision, NLP, theory) or problem types (empirical, algorithmic, proof-based).
2. **Snapshot evaluation**: Agents tested for six days with fixed compute budgets. Real research is iterative over weeks/months; unclear how agent performance scales with extended timelines.
3. **Frontier model dependency**: Results are specific to agents built on current frontier LLMs. Improved underlying models (e.g., reasoning-focused variants) might close gaps not captured here.
4. **Grading ambiguity**: Author grading is subjective. Two authors grading the same agent output might disagree; no inter-rater reliability reported.
5. **Failure mode diagnostics**: The five failure modes are observed but not mechanistically explained. Why do agents have poor judgment? Is it a training issue, tokenization issue, or architectural limitation? Unclear.
6. **No baseline**: No human researcher attempting the same task on the same timeline for comparison — hard to judge whether agent gaps are agent-specific or reflect difficulty of the tasks.

## Open Questions
1. **Domain generality**: Do the same failure modes appear in theory, vision, and NLP research? Are certain research areas (empirical ML vs. algorithmic vs. mathematical) more amenable to agent automation?
2. **Instruction engineering**: How much can carefully designed prompts or structured scaffolds mitigate the five failure modes? What does the Pareto frontier of prompt engineering look like for research tasks?
3. **Hybrid human-agent workflows**: What fraction of research can realistically be automated if humans stay in the loop at key decision points? Where are the breakeven points?
4. **Temporal scaling**: How do agents perform with weeks or months of compute vs. six days? Do they recover from dead ends better with more time?
5. **Smaller models**: Do the failure modes hold for smaller or cheaper models, or are they specific to frontier agents? Is there a speed-accuracy tradeoff?
6. **Longitudinal improvement**: As frontier LLMs improve month-to-month, how quickly does agent research capability advance? Can we forecast when agents might close the observed gaps?

## Key Takeaway
Frontier AI agents can execute research engineering tasks but consistently fail at higher-level research judgment, creativity, and problem diagnosis — automating AI research remains far harder than automating coding.
