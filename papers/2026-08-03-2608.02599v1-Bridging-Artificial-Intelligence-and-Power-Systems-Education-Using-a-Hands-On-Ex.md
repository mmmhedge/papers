# Bridging Artificial Intelligence and Power Systems Education Using a Hands-On Executable Framework

**Authors:** Junjie Yin, Buxin She, Xinyu Feng,  Fangxing,  Li
**Published:** 2026-08-03 | **arXiv:** [2608.02599v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02599v1)

## Summary
This paper presents an open-source educational framework that teaches AI concepts through hands-on power systems applications. Rather than treating AI as a generic black box, the framework grounds AI workflows in domain-specific engineering rules, with progressive modules covering neural networks for forecasting, physics-informed models, and reinforcement learning for control—all delivered as executable Jupyter notebooks and validated through an IEEE course that attracted 590+ attendees.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on established deep learning practices (neural networks for function approximation and pattern recognition) and reinforcement learning theory (Markov decision processes for sequential control problems). It also draws on physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) — a recent paradigm that embeds known physical equations (like power system swing dynamics) directly into loss functions to improve model accuracy and interpretability. The core motivation is "engineering-grounded AI" (EGAI), which contrasts with task-agnostic black-box approaches by requiring AI pipelines to respect domain constraints and established power-systems rules. The framework assumes learners come from diverse backgrounds and need scaffolded, low-barrier entry points rather than specialized monolithic applications.

## Core Contributions
1. **Progressive modular curriculum**: Maps foundational AI concepts (DNNs, CNNs, optimization, DRL, PINNs) onto representative power-systems problems with a clear difficulty ladder.
2. **Domain-coupled surrogate models**: A CNN specifically designed to learn power-flow dynamics for a 5-bus system, demonstrating how neural networks can replace expensive physics simulations.
3. **Physics-informed architectures**: Integration of PINNs to solve the swing equation (power system dynamics), showing how to embed governing equations into neural models.
4. **Reproducible, accessible delivery**: Complete Jupyter notebooks (executable locally or on cloud platforms) rather than closed-source software, lowering barriers to adoption.
5. **Community validation**: Survey evidence (92% barriers, 94% desire for power-specific courses) and high engagement metrics (590 live attendees, 344 repo visits in 2 weeks).

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader efforts in [[Physics-Informed Neural Networks]] and applies [[Reinforcement Learning]] principles (e.g., DQN-style agents for battery storage optimization) to infrastructure control. It aligns with the pedagogical shift toward executable, open-source ML education (similar to fast.ai or Andrew Ng's Coursera approach) while adding domain specificity absent in most generic ML courses. The emphasis on "engineering-grounded AI" contrasts with the trend toward [[large-language-models]] as catch-all problem solvers, instead advocating interpretable, constrained models for safety-critical domains. Related to [[optimization]] and control in power systems, but positioned as an educational bridge rather than a novel algorithmic contribution.

## Practical Applications
1. **Grid operator training**: Power utility companies use the framework to train control room operators on AI-assisted forecasting and dispatch.
2. **Renewable energy integration**: Utilities employ DRL modules to optimize battery storage scheduling in grids with high solar/wind penetration.
3. **Microgrid design**: Engineers use the surrogate models to rapidly prototype neural network-based power-flow solvers for local microgrids without running expensive full simulations.
4. **Interdisciplinary hiring**: Companies use the course to evaluate and onboard computer scientists and physicists who lack power-systems background but want to contribute to grid digitalization.
5. **Undergraduate research**: Universities adopt modules as capstone projects linking AI coursework to real infrastructure problems.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scale and realism**: Modules are demonstrated on toy systems (5-bus) that vastly underrepresent real grids (thousands of nodes, complex market rules, latency/communication constraints).
2. **Physics fidelity trade-offs**: PINNs and surrogates are approximations; the paper does not quantify failure modes or robustness to distribution shift (e.g., climate change, new equipment).
3. **Offline training**: All models appear to be trained offline; no discussion of online learning, adaptation, or handling non-stationary grid conditions (demand patterns, renewable generation variability).
4. **Safety and validation**: No formal safety certification or adversarial robustness analysis; unclear how learners validate that their trained models won't cause blackouts or instability.
5. **Generalization**: Surrogates and DRL policies may not transfer across different grid topologies, load profiles, or regulatory contexts; unclear how to adapt for other systems.
6. **Computational resources**: Requires learners to have reliable internet/compute; no discussion of offline or edge-device deployment.

## Open Questions
1. **Transfer and scaling**: Can a model trained on a 5-bus system transfer (via fine-tuning or domain adaptation) to real grids with thousands of buses? What is the sample complexity?
2. **Interpretability**: How do learners verify that the AI model is learning physically sensible patterns vs. exploiting spurious correlations? Can we visualize and debug neural network decisions?
3. **Uncertainty quantification**: Do the modules teach Bayesian or ensemble methods to estimate prediction confidence? Critical for operators making high-stakes decisions.
4. **Long-horizon planning**: Current modules seem focused on single-timestep control; how do we teach multi-step planning (e.g., unit commitment, market bidding) with RL?
5. **Human-in-the-loop**: Do the DRL agents work well when a human operator can override? What feedback mechanisms help operators learn from AI suggestions?
6. **Regulatory and adversarial robustness**: How robust are the trained models to adversarial attacks, data poisoning, or malicious manipulation? Essential for critical infrastructure.

## Key Takeaway
An open-source educational framework teaches AI for power systems via progressive, executable modules that respect domain rules—validated by 590+ course attendees demanding hands-on power-specific training.
