# Forecast Collapse in Time-Series Foundation Models

**Authors:** Shu Wan, Miles Ma, Hank Zhu, Guangqi Liu, Stephen Wang et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14106v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14106v1)

## Summary
Time-series foundation models produce flat, uninformative predictions when forecasting stock returns — a failure the authors call "forecast collapse" — even though they perform well on other financial data like trading volume. The paper identifies that this collapse stems from a fundamental tradeoff between calibration (accuracy of predicted magnitudes) and ranking (ability to distinguish which stocks will outperform), and proposes CalibRank, a new objective function that recovers predictive power by optimizing both simultaneously.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on recent advances in time-series foundation models (TSFMs) — large neural networks pretrained on diverse temporal datasets to handle many forecasting tasks simultaneously, similar to how large language models work across text. Standard forecasting evaluation uses per-series metrics like mean squared error (MSE), which measure prediction accuracy for individual time series independently. The work identifies a gap between these standard metrics and what downstream financial decisions actually need: the ability to rank assets by expected returns (cross-sectional correlation), not just predict absolute values. The tension between calibration (squared-error loss encourages matching target magnitudes) and ranking (which requires preserving relative order across many series) has been studied separately in forecasting, but the paper shows this creates a critical blind spot when using foundation models on financial data.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical discovery**: Systematically document forecast collapse across 97 benchmark configurations, showing it's a widespread phenomenon in TSFMs and deep learning forecasters on low-predictability targets (stock returns), but not on more predictable targets (volume).

2. **Root cause analysis**: Decompose the failure into two mechanisms — low target predictability naturally limits forecast amplitude via calibration, and per-series training objectives fail to learn cross-series structure (which assets are relatively more predictable).

3. **CalibRank objective**: Introduce a practical loss function that jointly optimizes squared error and ranking metrics, demonstrating ~3x improvement in cross-sectional correlation on Finance1K dataset while maintaining calibration.

4. **Evaluation critique**: Expose that standard per-series metrics (MSE, MAE, MAPE) can mask catastrophic failures in cross-series rankings — a critical gap for financial applications.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader challenges in [[deep-learning]] for finance, where models often overfit to noise or fail on truly hard prediction problems. It echoes issues seen in [[RLHF]] and alignment research: standard loss functions don't capture what end users actually care about. The calibration-ranking tradeoff mirrors classical finance problems in [[risk-management]] and portfolio construction, where you need both accurate return estimates and reliable orderings. The multi-objective optimization angle connects to recent work on Pareto-efficient objectives in machine learning. The paper's focus on cross-sectional structure is also relevant to [[market-microstructure]] research, which emphasizes relative pricing and ranking within asset universes.

## Practical Applications
1. **Systematic equity trading**: Quantitative hedge funds and asset managers can use CalibRank-trained TSFMs for daily stock ranking and portfolio construction, where ranking quality directly drives P&L.

2. **Volatility-adjusted position sizing**: Forecasts of trading volume and other calibrated quantities can inform risk limits and position concentration, while return rankings drive asset selection.

3. **Pairs trading and statistical arbitrage**: Cross-sectional correlation improvements enable better identification of relative mispricings between stocks.

4. **Factor allocation**: Ranking predictions help distinguish which style factors (value, momentum, quality) will outperform, enabling dynamic factor rotation.

5. **Risk model improvements**: Better cross-sectional structure in forecasts improves covariance estimation and correlation matrices used in portfolio optimization.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **FinML Platform**: Sell a SaaS tool where asset managers upload their own return/volume/price data, train CalibRank models on it via API, and get daily cross-sectional rankings of equities or other assets, with automated backtesting.

2. **Foundation Model for Finance**: Build and monetize a proprietary TSFM pretrained on 50+ years of market data, offered via API for ranking predictions; target quant funds and robo-advisors.

3. **Calibration Analytics Dashboard**: Offer a monitoring tool that flags when foundation models experience "forecast collapse" on a user's data, with automated alerting and retraining triggers.

4. **Embedded Rankings Engine**: White-label ranking module for investment platforms (brokers, portfolio management tools) that replaces naive momentum or volatility signals with learned cross-sectional rankings.

5. **Multi-Asset Ranking Service**: Extend CalibRank to commodities, currencies, and crypto; sell daily/hourly ranking feeds to trading desks and algorithmic traders.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Low signal regime**: The paper works on stock returns, which are genuinely hard to predict. CalibRank improves ranking, but absolute predictability may still be near noise, limiting real alpha.

2. **Scalability questions**: Unclear how CalibRank scales to thousands of assets or how to set the relative weight between squared-error and ranking terms across different asset classes.

3. **Stationarity assumption**: The method assumes the cross-series structure is stable over time; in regime shifts (market crashes, policy changes), relative rankings may collapse unpredictably.

4. **Training data requirements**: TSFMs need massive pretraining; requires access to diverse, high-quality historical data that may be expensive or unavailable for new markets or assets.

5. **Transaction costs ignored**: Backtests and evaluation don't account for trading friction, bid-ask spreads, or market impact, which can erase gains from improved ranking on small portfolios.

6. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The balance between calibration and ranking objectives likely requires careful tuning per dataset and asset class; no principled guidance given.

## Open Questions
1. **Optimal weighting**: How should practitioners set the weight between squared-error and ranking losses for their specific use case? Is there a data-driven way to choose this?

2. **Regime adaptation**: Can you detect and adapt to regime shifts where cross-sectional correlations break down? Should CalibRank be retrained with higher frequency?

3. **Higher-order structure**: Does the method preserve higher-order properties (e.g., factor structure, mean-reversion patterns) beyond pairwise ranking? How does it interact with covariance matrix estimation?

4. **Out-of-sample generalization**: How do rankings trained on one market period (e.g., bull market) perform in different regimes (bear market, high-volatility periods)?

5. **Multi-step forecasting**: The paper focuses on 1-step ahead; what happens to ranking quality in 5-, 20-, or 252-step forecasts? Does collapse reappear?

6. **Domain transfer**: Can a CalibRank model trained on US equities transfer to other asset classes (bonds, commodities) or markets (Europe, Asia) with minimal retraining?

## Key Takeaway
Standard neural network forecasting fails to rank stocks by expected returns; a new loss function balancing accuracy and ranking recovers the signal lost in low-predictability regimes.
