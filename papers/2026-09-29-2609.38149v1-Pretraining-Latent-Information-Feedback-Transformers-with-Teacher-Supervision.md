# Pretraining Latent Information Feedback Transformers with Teacher Supervision

**Authors:** Dor Tirosh, Ido Amos, Mor Geva
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38149v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38149v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces LIFT (Latent Information Feedback Transformer), a new architecture that allows language models to feed internal representations backward through layers during generation, breaking the feed-forward constraint of standard Transformers. Using teacher supervision during pretraining — where a pretrained model's state predictions guide learning — the authors show that models with this recurrent feedback consistently outperform standard Transformers at similar computational budgets, while remaining fully parallelizable during training.

## Theoretical Background
Standard Transformers process sequences in a feed-forward manner: information flows only forward through layers and backward only through the decoded token at each step. This creates an information bottleneck — the model must encode all necessary context into a single token at each generation step, forcing redundant computation and discarding alternative reasoning paths. Prior work on recurrent neural networks and state-feedback architectures explored similar ideas, but these were typically not parallelizable during training. The paper leverages teacher forcing — a standard technique in sequence modeling where a model learns to predict targets given ground-truth inputs — adapted here to learn latent state representations derived from a teacher model's next-token distribution.

## Core Contributions
1. **LIFT architecture**: Extends Transformers with bidirectional feedback loops that allow deep layers to communicate back to shallow layers, enabled by predicting and feeding back latent states rather than only tokens.
2. **Teacher-supervised pretraining method**: Makes state-feedback learning tractable by using precomputed teacher states (from an off-the-shelf LM) as training targets, maintaining full parallelism during pretraining while enabling recurrence only at inference.
3. **Empirical demonstration**: Shows consistent gains over standard Transformers on language modeling, reasoning tasks, and procedural tasks under token-matched budgets, with models of 135M–1B parameters.
4. **Decoupling learning from inference**: Proves that models can learn to exploit deep-to-shallow feedback even when trained on states from a teacher that fails the target task, suggesting the architecture captures general state-tracking principles.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several threads: recurrent language models (which trade parallelism for expressiveness), [[Attention Is All You Need]] and the feed-forward Transformer paradigm it established, and teacher-forced learning frameworks common in sequence-to-sequence models. It also connects to mechanistic interpretability questions about how Transformers reuse computation across positions. The use of an auxiliary teacher model for state supervision echoes knowledge distillation and multi-task learning approaches, though applied in a novel way to enable architectural feedback loops.

## Practical Applications
1. **More efficient reasoning in LLMs**: Reducing redundant computation by allowing models to propagate intermediate reasoning states could lower inference latency or energy for complex reasoning tasks (e.g., multi-step math, code generation).
2. **Improved in-context learning**: State feedback may allow models to better compress and reuse information from long contexts, improving few-shot performance on domain-specific tasks.
3. **Real-time dialogue systems**: Reduced token overhead at inference could enable lower-latency conversational AI or streaming text generation.
4. **Procedural task execution**: The demonstrated gains on procedural tasks suggest applications in instruction-following agents or task-oriented assistants.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LIFT-optimized inference API**: A commercial inference service that deploys LIFT models for reasoning-heavy tasks (legal document analysis, scientific Q&A), offering lower latency or cost per token than standard LLM APIs.
2. **Efficient fine-tuning platform**: A tool that applies LIFT-style feedback to user-provided models, enabling enterprise users to train smaller, cheaper models that outperform standard baselines on domain-specific reasoning.
3. **Streaming AI assistant**: A real-time collaborative writing or coding assistant leveraging LIFT's reduced inference overhead for sub-100ms latency, targeting professional writers and developers.
4. **State-caching middleware**: A library/service that manages teacher-model state precomputation and feedback routing for deployed LLMs, allowing retrofitting of existing systems.
5. **Education/tutoring system**: An interactive learning platform where LIFT's improved state tracking enables better error detection and multi-step explanations for math or science tutoring.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Teacher dependency**: Pretraining requires a high-quality pretrained teacher model; the method's gains depend on teacher quality, introducing a circular dependency and increased pretraining cost.
2. **Inference overhead**: While claimed to decrease with model size, the feedback computation at inference still adds overhead; for very small models or latency-critical settings, the gain may not justify the cost.
3. **Limited scale**: Experiments only go up to 1B parameters; gains may not hold at larger scales (10B+), where standard Transformers' efficiency advantages may reassert.
4. **State design**: The paper does not thoroughly explore how to choose or design the state representation; relying on a teacher's next-token distribution may be suboptimal for other objectives.
5. **Generalization**: The method is evaluated primarily on language modeling and procedural tasks; generalization to other modalities or task families is unclear.

## Open Questions
1. **Scaling behavior**: How do LIFT's gains scale to 10B–100B+ parameter models? Does the feedback mechanism remain beneficial when Transformers are already very expressive?
2. **State design alternatives**: Can learned or task-specific state representations outperform teacher-derived ones? Is the next-token distribution optimal?
3. **Theoretical justification**: Why does feedback help, mechanistically? Does the model learn qualitatively different algorithms or simply compress computation better?
4. **Multi-task and transfer**: Can a LIFT model pretrained on one teacher transfer to different downstream tasks or teachers? How robust is the approach?
5. **Comparison to other feedback mechanisms**: How does LIFT compare to other recurrent or residual-feedback designs (e.g., deep recurrence, auxiliary heads)? What trade-offs exist?

## Key Takeaway
Transformers can learn to feed latent state backward through layers during pretraining, improving efficiency without sacrificing parallelism or requiring architectural retraining.
