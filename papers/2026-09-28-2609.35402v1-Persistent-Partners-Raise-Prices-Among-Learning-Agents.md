# Persistent Partners Raise Prices Among Learning Agents

**Authors:** Paul-Peter Arslan, Yubin Kim, Xiao Xiao
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35402v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35402v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether repeated interaction between pricing agents raises the prices they learn through Q-learning, and whether this involves learned punishment. Using a controlled experiment in a Bertrand duopoly setting, the authors show that agents paired with the same partner consistently achieve higher profits and prices compared to those facing rotating opponents—even when unable to observe or punish rival price cuts. The finding matters because it suggests that matching algorithms on digital platforms can inadvertently facilitate collusion-like outcomes among learning agents.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on [[Calvano et al.]]'s seminal experiments showing that reinforcement learning agents can discover collusive pricing without explicit communication. Q-learning is a tabular reinforcement learning algorithm where agents learn action-value functions through repeated interaction. Bertrand duopoly is a market model where two firms compete by simultaneously setting prices. The paper extends this tradition by isolating the causal effect of *matching stability* (same partner vs. rotating partners) on learned pricing behavior, controlling for information visibility (whether agents can observe rivals' prices) and communication capability (whether agents can send messages). The gap between competitive, Nash equilibrium, and monopoly profit levels provides a scale for measuring how far learned prices deviate from purely competitive outcomes.

## Core Contributions
1. **Causal evidence for matching effects**: A pre-registered randomized experiment demonstrates that persistent partnerships raise equilibrium prices by approximately 0.27 of the competitive-to-monopoly gap, with all 20 paired runs showing positive effects. This is a clean, replicable causal finding.
2. **Dissociation of price rises from visible punishment**: When rival prices are hidden, prices still rise but agents cannot condition punishment on observed cuts (since they cannot see them). This challenges the narrative that learned collusion relies on observable punishment mechanisms.
3. **Partial role of static best response**: Forced-deviation probes reveal that only 33–50% of apparent punishment can be attributed to learned responses; the remainder may come from static best-response behavior, making "learned punishment" as a mechanism inconclusive.
4. **Reproducibility across algorithms**: The effect holds robustly in 25 additional Q-learning blocks, and partially replicates with untrained language models (Qwen2.5) under specific prompt conditions, suggesting the phenomenon is not unique to a single learning mechanism.

## Connections to Other Work
This research extends [[Calvano et al.]]'s foundational work on emergent collusion in [[reinforcement-learning]] agents. It relates to broader literature on [[multi-agent-systems]] learning dynamics and implicit coordination. The paper also engages with mechanism design: platform designers (via matching algorithms) can influence market outcomes without explicit collusion. The hidden-price condition connects to information economics and the role of transparency in competition. The language model experiments touch on whether [[large-language-models]] exhibit similar economic behavior to tabular learners, a nascent area. The work complements recent work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] by asking *what* agents learn to do, even if not *how* they do it.

## Practical Applications
1. **Platform regulation and antitrust**: Regulators monitoring marketplaces (e.g., ride-sharing, accommodation pricing) can use matching stability as a diagnostic signal for potential collusion risk.
2. **Dynamic matching algorithm design**: E-commerce and gig platforms can adjust matching policies to minimize collusion incentives—e.g., enforcing partner rotation or introducing noise into assignment.
3. **Market surveillance**: Financial exchanges and payment processors can flag unusual price stability correlating with repeated bilateral matches.
4. **Automated market monitoring**: Platforms can monitor whether agents' learned prices diverge from competitive benchmarks when pairings become persistent, triggering audits.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Collusion-detection SaaS for marketplaces**: Real-time monitoring tool that flags when platform matching patterns correlate with above-competitive pricing, with causal inference diagnostics and regulatory reporting.
2. **Dynamic matching optimization engine**: Sell matching algorithms to platforms that balance marketplace efficiency with collusion prevention by introducing controlled partner rotation and opacity.
3. **Antitrust risk assessment for platforms**: Consulting service that simulates reinforcement learning dynamics on a client's matching algorithm before deployment, predicting collusion risk.
4. **Decentralized marketplace protocol**: Design peer-to-peer market protocols with built-in randomized matching and information constraints to structurally prevent learning-based collusion.
5. **Regulatory technology for exchanges**: Sell monitoring and inference tools to securities regulators to detect algorithmic collusion in equity/derivative markets using learned behavior signatures.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Small-scale experiments**: Tests involve 2-agent duopolies; scalability to realistic market structures (many agents, product differentiation, entry/exit) is unclear.
2. **Artificial learning setup**: Tabular Q-learning with discrete prices and simple state spaces may not capture real pricing dynamics (continuous prices, rich product info, demand learning).
3. **Incomplete punishment mechanism identification**: The paper acknowledges that the "punishment" channel remains inconclusive even after probing. The rise in hidden-price conditions is documented but not mechanistically explained.
4. **Language model experiments are preliminary**: LLM results are inconsistent across prompts and model families, and models were untrained (no fine-tuning on the task), raising questions about whether the findings generalize to practical AI systems.
5. **Limited strategy space**: Agents can only adjust a single price dimension; real competition involves quantity, product attributes, and intertemporal strategies.
6. **Assumes perfect observability of opponent identity**: In practice, agents may not reliably identify repeat partners, especially in large-scale markets.

## Open Questions
1. **Mechanism in hidden-price regime**: Why does price rise persist when punishment cannot be observed? Is it due to convergence to a correlated equilibrium, implicit coordination on focal prices, or something else?
2. **Scalability and heterogeneity**: Do the effects survive with more agents, heterogeneous learning rates, or partial information about the matching structure?
3. **Optimal matching design**: What matching algorithm minimizes collusion risk while maintaining platform efficiency (e.g., matching speed, allocation welfare)?
4. **LLM collusion in practice**: Can fine-tuned or in-context-learned language models replicate the effect consistently? Does scale matter?
5. **Asymmetric information and commitment**: What happens if agents can commit to price sequences or if they have private information about costs?
6. **Robustness to deviation costs**: How sensitive are results to the assumption that deviations are costless (instantaneous punishment)?

## Key Takeaway
Repeated pairings between pricing agents raise learned prices even without the ability to punish competitors, suggesting platform matching alone facilitates collusion-like outcomes.
