# Large Signal Libraries: Equal-Weight Limits and the Divergent Spectra of Signals and PnL

**Authors:** Marc da Costa Nunes
**Published:** 2026-09-11 | **arXiv:** [2609.12477v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.12477v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates why large portfolios of trading signals often align unexpectedly well with market structure—specifically, why ~3,000 signals across 20 assets showed 90% correlation with the market's leading return component. The author develops population theory to distinguish between signal geometry, their principal components, and actual profit-and-loss (PnL) outcomes, revealing that crowding alone doesn't explain such alignment and that signal libraries follow predictable spectral properties under different research designs.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on portfolio optimization and signal analysis in quantitative finance. It treats cross-sectional signals (predictions about relative asset movements) as high-dimensional geometric objects lying on a unit sphere. The paper leverages principal component analysis (PCA — a technique that identifies the dominant directions of variation in data) and spectral theory to understand how signal libraries behave. Key concepts include: equal-weight portfolio construction, axial symmetry (rotational invariance in high dimensions), and eigenvalue decomposition of signal covariance kernels. The motivation stems from empirical observations in systematic trading that signal ensembles often concentrate their explanatory power in a single dominant direction—a phenomenon the author seeks to explain theoretically rather than attribute to luck.

## Core Contributions
1. **Four-object distinction**: The paper formally separates equal-weight signals, signal principal components, equal-weight PnL, and PnL principal components—showing these are distinct mathematical objects with different spectral properties.
2. **Geometric threshold for alignment**: Under axial symmetry, signal PC1 (principal component 1) aligns with the mean signal if and only if its longitudinal second moment exceeds $1/q$ (where $q = d-1$ is the ambient dimension for $d$ assets).
3. **Spectral contraction bounds**: Combining design weights into composite signals contracts their angle to PC1 by a factor of at most $\sqrt{\lambda_2/\lambda_1}$, where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2$ are leading eigenvalues of the signal kernel—a sharp bound.
4. **Target-aligned frame**: A geometric decomposition that separates transverse signal geometry (irrelevant to PnL) from dispersion weighting and temporal centering effects.
5. **Design-distribution dependence**: Independent libraries converge to their design mean; exchangeable libraries retain random conditional means—illustrating how research process design shapes population properties.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper engages with classical portfolio theory and signal processing in systematic finance. It relates to long-standing questions about [[factor models]] and [[principal component analysis]] in asset pricing. The concern with "crowding" (many signals capturing the same underlying factor) echoes literature on factor decay and signal redundancy in quantitative trading. The geometric treatment on the unit sphere connects to work on high-dimensional statistics and random matrix theory. Unlike empirical studies of real trading signals, this is pure population theory—complementary to practical backtesting but addressing the theoretical foundations of why large signal libraries cluster in low-dimensional structures.

## Practical Applications
1. **Signal portfolio construction**: Practitioners can use spectral contraction bounds to estimate how many truly independent signals they actually own versus redundant copies of a few common factors.
2. **Research design auditing**: The design-distribution framework helps quant teams diagnose whether their signal pipeline is truly discovering new alpha or just regressing toward market structure.
3. **Overfitting risk assessment**: Teams can estimate population alignment thresholds to detect when signal libraries are overcrowded and facing degraded out-of-sample performance.
4. **Dimensionality reduction**: The target-aligned frame helps separate meaningful signal diversity from noise, informing which signals to retain and which to discard.
5. **Research budget allocation**: Understanding spectral limits helps allocate resources—if 158 signals per dimension already exhaust geometric alignment, adding more signals yields diminishing returns.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Signal Library Auditor (SaaS)**: A tool that ingests a firm's signal library, decomposes it using the paper's framework, and reports the true number of independent factors, redundancy indices, and spectral concentration risk—alerting teams before they deploy overcrowded portfolios.
2. **Geometric Backtester**: A backtesting engine that distinguishes transverse (market-irrelevant) signal geometry from PnL-relevant structure, helping traders separate overfitting from genuine edge.
3. **Research Process Validator**: Consulting or software product that audits quant research workflows to determine whether they follow independent or exchangeable designs, and provides population-level risk estimates.
4. **Synthetic Signal Generator**: A tool for generating calibrated synthetic signal libraries matching specified spectral properties, enabling teams to test their pipelines and estimate robustness without market data.
5. **Factor Exhaustion Monitor**: A real-time dashboard showing whether a firm's signal portfolio is approaching or exceeding the population geometric limits, signaling when diversification efforts plateau.

## Technical Limitations
1. **No real data validation**: The paper presents no empirical results on actual market signals—only synthetic examples. Population theory may diverge sharply from finite-sample reality, especially under model misspecification.
2. **Axial symmetry assumption**: The main theoretical results assume symmetric signal distributions. Real signals often exhibit skewness, fat tails, and directional biases that break this assumption.
3. **Time-invariance**: The theory treats signal libraries as static populations. In practice, signal correlations, factor structures, and PnL relationships drift over time—a dynamic not captured.
4. **Linear geometry only**: The framework is purely linear (unit spheres, PCA, eigenvalue decomposition). Nonlinear signal interactions or regime-dependent clustering may dominate in practice.
5. **Design distribution specification**: The distinction between independent and exchangeable libraries requires knowing the research process's true design distribution—which is often unknown or misreported in practice.
6. **Finite-sample estimation**: The paper doesn't address how to reliably estimate spectral quantities (eigenvalues, alignment angles) from limited historical data, a critical practical bottleneck.

## Open Questions
1. **Finite-sample theory**: What are the estimation errors and confidence bounds for spectral quantities when learning from $T$ historical periods and $d$ assets simultaneously?
2. **Temporal dynamics**: How do signal correlations and principal components evolve through market regimes? Does the theory extend to time-varying design distributions?
3. **Nonlinear signals**: Can the framework be extended to nonlinear signal combinations or neural-network-based features? How does nonlinearity change the spectral picture?
4. **Real-world validation**: Applying the framework to published or proprietary signal libraries—do observed spectral concentrations match population predictions?
5. **PnL-signal divergence**: When and why do equal-weight PnL spectrum diverge most sharply from equal-weight signal spectrum? Can this be predicted from signal geometry alone?
6. **Optimal design**: Given constraints on research effort, what design distribution maximizes the number of independent signals subject to population-level limits?

## Key Takeaway
Large trading signal portfolios inherently cluster into low-dimensional structures; geometry, not luck, explains the alignment.
