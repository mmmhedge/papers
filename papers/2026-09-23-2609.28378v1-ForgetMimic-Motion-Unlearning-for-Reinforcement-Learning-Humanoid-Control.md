# ForgetMimic: Motion Unlearning for Reinforcement Learning Humanoid Control

**Authors:** Xukun Luan, Zhongxiang Lei, Chen Gong, Shaowei Li, Yuanguo Bi et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28378v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28378v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces ForgetMimic, a method for selectively "unlearning" specific motions from humanoid robot policies trained via reinforcement learning. The work addresses safety, privacy, and regulatory compliance by allowing removal of malicious, poisoned, or copyrighted motions while preserving performance on all other learned behaviors — a previously unexplored problem in robot control.

## Theoretical Background
Humanoid robot control typically relies on learning from human demonstrations combined with reinforcement learning (RL) — algorithms that train agents to maximize reward signals through trial and error. Prior work has achieved impressive results in locomotion by imitating human motion patterns. The paper builds on this imitation-learning paradigm but introduces a novel challenge: **machine unlearning**, the problem of removing learned associations from trained models without full retraining. This concept has been studied in computer vision and language models but remains underdeveloped for embodied RL agents. The authors identify that standard unlearning techniques fail in robot control due to two specific training mechanisms — likely related to how motor policies generalize across motion primitives.

## Core Contributions
1. **First motion-level unlearning framework for humanoid control**: ForgetMimic is the first method explicitly designed to forget specific motions in physical robot policies.
2. **Identification and resolution of unlearning failure modes**: The paper identifies two key mechanisms in robot training that cause naive unlearning approaches to fail, then proposes solutions.
3. **Empirical validation on real hardware**: Extensive experiments on Unitree G1 and H2 robots across 12 diverse motions (Dance, Fight, Flip, etc.) demonstrate the method preserves non-target motion performance while degrading target motions.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[reinforcement-learning|RL]], imitation learning from human demonstrations, and machine unlearning. It relates to broader alignment and safety concerns in autonomous agents (e.g., removing harmful behaviors), similar to debates in [[RLHF|reinforcement learning from human feedback]]. The regulatory motivation (GDPR right to be forgotten) echoes concerns in [[mechanistic-interpretability|mechanistic interpretability]] and alignment about transparency and control in learned models. Unlike prior unlearning work in vision or language models, ForgetMimic must handle the continuous, embodied control challenge where motions interact through shared network weights and physics constraints.

## Practical Applications
1. **Safety in human environments**: Remove dangerous or aggressive motions (e.g., "Fight") from robots deployed in factories or care facilities.
2. **Copyright compliance**: Delete proprietary or licensed dance/movement sequences from commercial humanoid robots.
3. **Malware/poisoning remediation**: Remove backdoored motions injected during training without complete policy retraining.
4. **Privacy protection**: Comply with GDPR or similar regulations requiring deletion of training data tied to specific individuals' movements.
5. **Model refinement**: Iteratively improve policies by discarding learned erratic or suboptimal motions mid-deployment.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Robotic Policy Sanitization Service**: SaaS platform where robot manufacturers upload policies; users request removal of specific motions; the service returns cleaned policies certified compliant with GDPR/privacy laws.
2. **Motion Library Licensing Platform**: Marketplace for certified, copyrighted humanoid motions with automatic unlearning tooling to ensure licensed motions can be removed if rights expire or licenses lapse.
3. **Safety Certification Tool for Humanoid Robots**: Pre-deployment auditing software that identifies risky motions in learned policies and automatically unlearns them before robots enter human-shared spaces.
4. **Federated Robot Learning Network**: Decentralized platform where robots share learned skills but can request deletion of specific motions across the network without full model retraining — preserving privacy across robot fleets.
5. **Adversarial Robustness as a Service**: Detect and remove poisoned motions from pre-trained humanoid policies, protecting enterprises from supply-chain attacks on third-party models.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability unclear**: Tested on only 12 motions; unclear how performance degrades with hundreds or thousands of motion categories.
2. **Interaction effects**: The paper doesn't fully characterize interference — removing one motion might degrade others if they share motor representations, and this trade-off isn't quantified.
3. **Generalization across robot morphologies**: Validation is limited to two Unitree models; transferability to other humanoid designs (Boston Dynamics, Tesla Bot) is unknown.
4. **Computational cost not reported**: No comparison of unlearning time vs. full retraining; may be impractical for time-sensitive deployments.
5. **Partial vs. complete forgetting**: Method "degrades" performance on target motions but may not achieve zero performance; adversarial recovery of forgotten skills is not addressed.
6. **Assumptions on motion independence**: Assumes motions can be meaningfully separated; highly coupled behaviors may resist unlearning.

## Open Questions
1. **Why do standard unlearning methods fail in RL?** What is the precise mechanism linking the two identified failure modes to policy generalization?
2. **Can you unlearn motion semantics hierarchically?** If Dance is composed of sub-motions (step, spin, sway), can the method unlearn at different granularities?
3. **Adversarial robustness of forgetting**: Can an attacker recover the forgotten motion through carefully chosen state trajectories or perturbations?
4. **Multi-task transfer**: If a humanoid trained on 50 motions learns the 12 used in this work, does selective unlearning of those 12 harm generalization to the full 50?
5. **Theoretical guarantees**: Is there a lower bound on performance degradation on non-target motions as a function of how different target and non-target motions are?

## Key Takeaway
ForgetMimic enables selective erasure of learned robot motions for safety and privacy compliance without full policy retraining.
