# On a joint simultaneous learning of relevant feature subsets and subspaces in regression-like problems

**Authors:** Illia Horenko
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28080v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28080v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Entropy-Optimal Manifold Regression (EOMR), a method for simultaneously identifying which features matter and what lower-dimensional structures (subspaces) underlie complex, nonlinear prediction problems. The work shows that EOMR outperforms state-of-the-art machine learning tools—including deep neural networks and transformers—on chaotic dynamical systems, while remaining computationally efficient and producing interpretable models.

## Theoretical Background
The paper extends Entropy-Optimal Manifold Clustering (EOMC), a framework rooted in information-theoretic principles for discovering natural groupings in data. The core idea is that real-world phenomena often live on lower-dimensional manifolds (curved surfaces embedded in high-dimensional spaces) rather than filling all available dimensions. Feature selection and dimensionality reduction are classical machine learning problems—selecting which inputs matter and projecting data onto simpler subspaces—but doing both jointly and robustly is hard. The paper tackles nonstationary (time-varying) and nonlinear regression, where the relationship between inputs and outputs changes over time or is curved rather than flat.

## Core Contributions
1. **Joint feature and subspace learning**: EOMR simultaneously identifies relevant feature subsets *and* the optimal low-dimensional subspaces they inhabit, rather than treating these as separate sequential steps.
2. **Complexity guarantees**: The method achieves linear scaling in iteration and memory complexity, meaning it remains computationally practical as data size grows.
3. **Empirical superiority on dynamics**: On two benchmark problems from chaotic and plasma physics (Lorenz-96 and Hasegawa-Wakatani), EOMR achieves orders of magnitude better prediction error than gradient boosted forests, deep networks, and TabPFN, while using far fewer parameters (e.g., 8 parameters for one case versus thousands for competitors).
4. **Interpretability**: Solutions distill to simple, causal, stationary autoregressive models that humans can understand.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of classical dimensionality reduction (PCA, manifold learning) and modern ML. It compares directly against [[deep-learning]] methods (neural networks, [[transformers]]-based tools like TabPFN) and gradient boosting, showing where entropy-optimal approaches outperform black-box neural methods. It relates to sparse learning and feature selection literature, but emphasizes nonlinear, nonstationary regimes where most standard tools struggle. The focus on dynamical systems connects to physics-informed machine learning and scientific machine learning (SciML), though the paper frames itself as a general regression tool rather than domain-specific.

## Practical Applications
1. **Climate and weather modeling**: Chaotic atmospheric dynamics require robust, interpretable predictions; EOMR's success on Lorenz-96 suggests utility for weather forecasting.
2. **Fusion energy (plasma control)**: The Hasegawa-Wakatani benchmark is drawn from tokamak physics; the method could guide real-time plasma confinement control.
3. **Turbulence modeling**: Fluid dynamics are highly nonlinear and chaotic; engineers need fast, interpretable surrogates for design and control.
4. **Financial time series**: Nonstationary, nonlinear dynamics are endemic to markets; EOMR could identify relevant features (e.g., which instruments drive a portfolio) and subspaces without overfitting.
5. **Sensor networks and edge inference**: Linear-memory complexity makes the method suitable for embedded and distributed systems monitoring complex phenomena (e.g., industrial equipment, infrastructure).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Scientific ML platform for dynamical systems**: A SaaS tool for scientists and engineers to fit, analyze, and forecast chaotic systems (climate, turbulence, plasma) with interpretable models and automatic feature discovery—marketed as "the transparent alternative to black-box neural networks for physics-based problems."
2. **Plasma control software for fusion startups**: Real-time EOMR-based controllers for tokamak and stellarator designs, enabling faster iteration and cheaper experiment cycles for companies pursuing commercial fusion (Commonwealth Fusion, TAE, etc.).
3. **Financial data distillation service**: A quant trading platform that uses EOMR to automatically identify minimal, causal factor models in high-frequency or alternative-data environments, reducing overfitting risk and improving interpretability for institutional investors.
4. **Edge-deployed predictive maintenance**: Deploy EOMR on IoT devices in manufacturing to predict equipment failure using raw sensor streams; the small memory footprint and speed enable on-device learning without cloud latency.
5. **Climate digital twin toolkit**: Productized software for climate agencies and NGOs to build interpretable, low-parameter surrogate models of regional climate dynamics for policy impact assessment and scenario planning.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited novelty in core algorithm**: The paper extends EOMC rather than proposing a fundamentally new framework; the novelty is primarily in the regression extension and empirical validation.
2. **Benchmark selection bias**: The two benchmarks (Lorenz-96, Hasegawa-Wakatani) are from chaotic dynamical systems—arguably EOMR's sweet spot. Performance on other nonlinear problems (e.g., high-dimensional sensor data, image/language tasks) is not shown.
3. **Lack of theoretical guarantees**: No convergence proofs, sample complexity bounds, or conditions under which EOMR is optimal. The paper is primarily empirical.
4. **Comparison fairness**: Baselines (random forests, standard DNNs, TabPFN) are "general-purpose"; none are specialized for dynamical systems (e.g., physics-informed neural networks, neural ODEs, reservoir computing). A fair comparison would pit EOMR against domain-tuned baselines.
5. **Hyperparameter and tuning details unclear**: How sensitive is EOMR to its own hyperparameters? How much manual tuning was required for the benchmarks vs. the baselines?
6. **Scalability to very high dimensions**: Linear memory is good, but no experiments with >100s of features; unclear performance as feature dimension grows very large.
7. **Nonstationarity handling**: The paper claims to handle nonstationary regimes, but the extent to which EOMR adapts online vs. requiring retraining is unclear.

## Open Questions
1. **Why does EOMR outperform neural networks so dramatically on these problems?** Is it that neural networks are genuinely mismatched to chaotic dynamics, or is it an optimization/architecture choice? Would [[mechanistic-interpretability]] tools reveal what the neural networks are struggling to learn?
2. **How does EOMR generalize to other problem classes?** What are the necessary and sufficient properties of a regression problem for EOMR to excel? Can you characterize this analytically?
3. **Can EOMR be combined with neural networks?** Could a hybrid approach use EOMR for feature/subspace discovery and then fit a smaller, more interpretable neural network on the reduced space?
4. **Online and streaming variants**: How would EOMR behave in a true online learning setting where data arrives sequentially and nonstationarity is continuous?
5. **Causal inference**: The paper mentions "causal" autoregressive models. Can EOMR provably identify causal structure, or is this post-hoc interpretation? How does it relate to causal discovery literature?
6. **Extension to other modalities**: Can EOMR be applied to image, text, or multimodal data, or is it fundamentally suited to tabular/temporal data?

## Key Takeaway
Entropy-optimal manifold regression finds sparse, interpretable regression models on chaotic systems orders of magnitude more accurate than deep networks while remaining computationally lean.
