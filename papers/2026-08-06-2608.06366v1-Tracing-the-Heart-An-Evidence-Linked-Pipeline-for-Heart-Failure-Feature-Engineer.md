# Tracing the Heart: An Evidence-Linked Pipeline for Heart-Failure Feature Engineering

**Authors:** Soorya Ram Shimgekar, Michelle Hu, Dorisa Shehi, Daniel Kang, Roy Ka-Wei Lee et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-06 | **arXiv:** [2608.06366v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.06366v1)

## Summary
Electronic health record (EHR) feature engineering is a major bottleneck in clinical research and AI, accounting for 39-45% of data scientists' workload. This is especially pronounced in heart failure, which affects an estimated 6.7 million U.S. adults and requires integrating fragmented EHR data with disease-specific, guideline-based clinical reasoning. Existing rule-based and large language model (LLM)-based approaches offer only partial automation with limited maintainability and evidence traceability.

## Theoretical Background
High-stakes predictive systems require traceable inputs, calibrated uncertainty, and evidence that their outputs generalize beyond the development data. Operational use also demands clear failure modes and human oversight.

The paper is positioned against prior approaches described in its abstract and asks whether a more targeted representation, evaluation method, or optimization procedure can improve the relevant trade-off.

## Core Contributions
1. We developed the Nimblemind Multi-Agent System (nMAS), an evidence-linked, rubric-grounded pipeline for automated heart-failure feature engineering, and evaluated it on 500 dummy patient records from nine EHR source tables. nMAS generated 132 structured and 70 rubric-scored aggregated features, verified for structural integrity, rubric compliance, and provenance, and audited by a restricted LLM.
2. These results demonstrate the feasibility of automated, auditable feature engineering for complex cardiovascular EHR data, though evaluation was limited to a single-institution cohort and external validation is needed.

## Technical Limitations
This note is based on the title, metadata, and abstract rather than a full-paper audit. The abstract cannot establish implementation completeness, statistical robustness, or whether all baselines were tuned comparably. Reported gains may depend on the selected datasets, models, task definitions, and compute environment. Deployment would require independent validation on out-of-distribution inputs, measurement of total runtime and cost, and analysis of failure cases. Any high-stakes use would also need human oversight and domain-specific governance.

## Open Questions
1. Do the results hold across substantially different datasets, model families, and deployment conditions?
2. Which component contributes most to the reported improvement under a controlled ablation?
3. How does the method compare under matched total compute, latency, and engineering effort?
4. What are the dominant failure modes, and can they be detected before they affect downstream decisions?
5. Can the approach scale without weakening its interpretability, guarantees, or data efficiency?
