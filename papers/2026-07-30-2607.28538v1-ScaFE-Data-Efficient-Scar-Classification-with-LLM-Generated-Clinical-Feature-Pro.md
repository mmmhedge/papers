# ScaFE: Data-Efficient Scar Classification with LLM-Generated Clinical Feature Programs

**Authors:** Ruman Wang, Hangting Ye
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28538v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28538v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces ScaFE, a system that uses large language models to generate interpretable feature extraction programs for classifying pathological scars (keloids vs. hypertrophic) from clinical photographs. Instead of relying on vision-language models that send raw images to external services, ScaFE generates local, auditable Python programs that measure clinically-relevant scar attributes, then trains a simple Random Forest classifier on the resulting features—achieving better accuracy than standard baselines while keeping patient data private and preserving reproducibility.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on several established ideas: (1) **transfer learning** — leveraging pre-trained models (here, LLMs) to solve downstream tasks with limited labeled data; (2) **interpretable machine learning** — the principle that medical AI systems should show their reasoning, not just predictions; (3) **vision-language models (VLMs)** — neural networks trained on image-text pairs that can understand visual content and respond to natural language; and (4) **SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations)**, a game-theory-inspired method to explain model predictions by attributing importance to individual features.

The core innovation is the *inversion* of the typical VLM pipeline: instead of asking a model "what disease does this image show?", ScaFE asks "what measurable visual attributes should we extract?", then runs those programs locally.

## Core Contributions
1. **LLM-to-code generation for medical imaging**: A novel workflow where an LLM synthesizes executable Python programs that implement clinical domain knowledge, rather than using the LLM directly for diagnosis.
2. **Data efficiency through feature engineering**: By transferring clinical priors as programs, the system achieves 72% accuracy with only 10% of training data—an 11.8-point margin over baselines—suggesting feature-based learning is far more sample-efficient than end-to-end deep learning for this task.
3. **Cross-site generalization**: Leave-one-hospital-out evaluation shows the approach generalizes across acquisition variation (different cameras, lighting, protocols) better than comparison methods.
4. **Auditable and privacy-preserving pipeline**: Raw images never leave the hospital; only aggregate statistics and feature-level explanations are sent to the LLM for refinement, enabling local governance compliance and reproducible decisions.
5. **Iterative refinement loop**: A semi-automated process to validate and repair generated programs, raising the executable-program rate from 67% to 95%.

## Practical Applications
1. **Hospital scar assessment pipelines**: Deploy locally at dermatology or plastic surgery clinics to triage keloid vs. hypertrophic scar cases, flag severe cases for expert review, and track treatment efficacy.
2. **Regulatory compliance in regulated markets**: Healthcare systems in regions with strict data residency laws (GDPR, HIPAA) can use ScaFE without sending patient images to cloud-hosted APIs.
3. **Low-resource clinical settings**: Clinics without on-site pathologists or dermatologists can use the system as a diagnostic aid to decide whether to refer patients to specialists.
4. **Clinical trials and outcome tracking**: Automated, standardized scar assessment for burn units, trauma centers, or cosmetic surgery follow-ups, reducing observer bias.
5. **Medical device integration**: Embed the feature-extraction pipeline in medical photography hardware or mobile apps, enabling real-time decision support at the point of care.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ClinicAI for Dermatology**: A HIPAA-compliant, on-premise SaaS platform offering LLM-generated diagnostic feature programs for common skin conditions (scars, wound healing, lesions), with iterative improvement via clinician feedback loops.
2. **ScarScore**: A mobile app for patients and clinicians to document scar progression over time using phone photos; the app runs feature extraction locally and generates auditable reports suitable for clinical notes and insurance.
3. **Feature Generator for Biotech**: A B2B tool that helps pharmaceutical and medical device companies rapidly generate interpretable feature extractors from clinical images for pre-clinical and clinical trials, shortening annotation requirements.
4. **Compliance-First Medical Analytics**: A startup offering LLM-driven feature engineering as a service specifically for hospitals with strict data governance; markets as "your clinical data never leaves the firewall."
5. **Scar Registry and Benchmarking**: A federated platform where clinics upload locally-extracted scar features (not images) to a central database, enabling large-scale real-world evidence generation without privacy risk—with ScaFE as the underlying engine.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Feature program synthesis quality**: The system's success hinges on LLM-generated code being correct and clinically meaningful. A 33% initial failure rate (only 67% of generated programs execute) suggests LLMs still struggle with precise algorithmic reasoning; higher stakes could warrant human code review.
2. **Limited to interpretable attributes**: The approach assumes clinically-relevant features can be expressed as deterministic programs; subtle, high-dimensional image patterns (e.g., micro-texture correlates of collagen alignment) may be lost compared to deep learning.
3. **Feature-level SHAP disclosure**: While raw images remain local, aggregated SHAP values might leak sensitive information in small datasets or with adversarial analysis; privacy guarantees are not formally quantified.
4. **Scalability of iterative refinement**: The workflow requires expert engagement for validation and program repair; unclear if it scales to dozens of conditions or hospitals.
5. **Evaluation on single condition**: Results are for keloid/hypertrophic scar classification only; generalization to other scar types (atrophic, contractures) or skin conditions is unproven.
6. **Domain specificity**: LLM performance likely degrades for rare, atypical presentations; no analysis of failure modes on edge cases.

## Open Questions
1. **How much expert data is truly needed?** Can the 10% data regime be pushed further, or is there a hard floor where end-to-end learning becomes necessary?
2. **Formal privacy guarantees**: Can you prove differential privacy on aggregated SHAP values, or characterize membership inference attacks?
3. **Multi-condition generalization**: Does the same framework work for other skin conditions, wounds, or diagnostic tasks? Is there a meta-pattern for *when* feature-program generation beats end-to-end learning?
4. **Automated program validation**: Can the system self-assess program correctness without human review (e.g., via synthetic data, consistency checks, or adversarial inputs)?
5. **Continual learning**: How do you update feature programs as new clinical knowledge emerges or new hospitals join the network?
6. **Failure analysis**: What scar phenotypes or hospital acquisition settings cause the system to degrade, and can they be predicted in advance?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs can transfer clinical knowledge as executable feature-extraction programs, enabling data-efficient, auditable medical image classification while keeping patient data local.
