# CausalForge: A Formally Grounded, Self-Improving Agentic Framework for Automated Research in Causal Inference

**Authors:** Jiyuan Tan, Vasilis Syrgkanis
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22511v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22511v1)

## Summary
CausalForge automates theoretical research in causal inference by combining formal verification (via the Lean proof assistant) with an AI agent pipeline. The system generates, formalizes, and proves causal theorems while checking that formal statements actually match the intended scientific claims — solving a critical problem where language models alone unreliably verify research correctness.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two distinct traditions. First, formal verification using proof assistants (Lean is a dependently-typed language where every statement must be machine-checked against axioms). Second, agentic AI systems that decompose complex tasks into sub-steps: research planning, hypothesis generation, formalization, and proof construction. The paper addresses a specific failure mode of current LLM-based research automation: language models can fabricate plausible-sounding papers and evaluate their own work at near-chance accuracy. The authors introduce *statement auditing* — comparing formal theorems back to informal scientific claims to catch cases where the proof is valid but doesn't express what was intended.

## Core Contributions
1. **Causalean**: A foundational Lean library with 7,035 machine-checked declarations for causal inference, developed with LLM assistance but under human design oversight.
2. **CausalSmith pipeline**: A self-improving agentic system that cycles through topic selection, result proposal, formalization, proof construction, and human-inspectable output.
3. **Statement audit layer**: A verification method that checks whether formal theorems faithfully capture informal scientific intent, decoupling proof correctness from semantic correctness.
4. **Empirical evaluation framework**: Demonstrations using actual autonomous research runs showing the system's ability to produce valid, semantically meaningful causal theorems.

## Connections to Other Work
This addresses known limitations in [[large-language-models]] for research verification (referenced critique: "Bad Scientist, 2025"). It differs from pure LLM-based research automation by grounding claims in formal logic rather than relying on natural-language evaluation. The formal verification approach parallels recent work using proof assistants for machine learning guarantees, but applied to mathematical discovery in causal inference. The human-in-the-loop aspect resembles [[alignment]] concerns about reliably steering AI systems toward intended outcomes.

## Practical Applications
1. **Accelerated causal discovery in econometrics and epidemiology**: Automated generation of valid theorems about causal relationships in observational data, with certified correctness.
2. **Regulatory compliance in biomedical research**: Producing formally verified causal claims suitable for regulatory submissions where proof of logical validity is required.
3. **Educational tools**: Interactive systems that teach causal inference by generating and explaining formally verified examples and counterexamples.
4. **Theory generation for policy**: Automated derivation of causal theorems informing policy decisions in health, labor, or environmental domains.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **CausalCert**: A SaaS platform for researchers to submit causal hypotheses and receive machine-verified proofs with audited statements, targeting econometrics and biostatistics teams.
2. **ProofAI for Economics**: White-label research automation for central banks and policy institutions needing formally verified economic causal claims.
3. **Lean Tutor for Causal Inference**: An interactive education product where students propose causal theorems and the system formalizes, proves, and grades them in real time.
4. **Research Validation as a Service**: A consultancy offering formal verification of published causal claims in academic papers, identifying gaps or errors in inference logic.
5. **Theorem Marketplace**: A platform where researchers publish formally verified causal theorems as reusable, composable components for downstream research.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Formalization bottleneck**: Converting informal scientific claims into precise Lean statements requires significant expertise; the system does not fully automate this step and relies on human review.
2. **Limited scope**: Currently focused on causal inference; generalization to other theoretical domains (number theory, combinatorics, etc.) is unclear.
3. **LLM reliability in proof search**: While formal verification ensures proof correctness, the LLM must still generate candidate proofs. For complex theorems, this may fail or require extensive search.
4. **Semantic gap remains**: Even with statement auditing, subtle mismatches between informal intuition and formal definition can slip through human review.
5. **Scalability**: Maintaining a Lean library with 7,000+ declarations and extending it grows in complexity; proof search times and library dependencies may become bottlenecks.
6. **Assumption transparency**: A machine-checked proof only verifies that conclusions follow from stated axioms—it does not validate whether those axioms match real-world causal mechanisms.

## Open Questions
1. How does the system perform on novel causal theorems outside the training distribution of Causalean? Does it struggle with theorems requiring complex proof strategies?
2. Can the statement audit be formalized itself, or does it inevitably require human judgment? What fraction of statements currently fail the audit?
3. How much does theorem complexity (proof length, number of lemmas) affect success rates? Is there a practical ceiling on tractable theorems?
4. Can the framework extend to causal inference under unverifiable assumptions (e.g., hidden confounding)? How would formal verification handle existential uncertainty?
5. What is the human effort cost per published theorem? Can the system identify which theorems are most valuable to pursue next, or is topic selection still ad hoc?
6. How would this scale to collaborative multi-agent research where multiple CausalForge instances propose and critique each other's work?

## Key Takeaway
Lean-formalized proofs plus statement auditing solve LLM unreliability in automated causal inference research discovery.
