# Creative Transformation in Literary Texts: Modelling Change Across Representational Levels

**Authors:** Ioana-Roxana Boriceanu, Liviu P. Dinu
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28513v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28513v1)

## Summary
This paper models literary creativity as selective transformation across multiple levels of textual representation—lexical, semantic, structural, and narrative—rather than pure invention. By comparing documented literary relationships using quantitative alignment methods, the authors show that creative works typically preserve certain aspects of source texts while diverging in others, providing a computational framework for understanding how imitation and originality coexist in literature.

## Theoretical Background
The paper draws on classical theories of imitation and cultural evolution, particularly Gabriel Tarde's work on imitation as a driver of social change and James Mark Baldwin's theories of creativity emerging through variation and selection. Traditional literary criticism treats creativity as novelty production, but this work adopts a different lens: treating literary works as transformations of prior artifacts along measurable dimensions. The framework uses directional alignment (comparing how one text aligns with another in a particular direction) and control-calibrated similarity measures—statistical techniques that assess how similar two texts are while controlling for baseline expectations. These are grounded in computational linguistics and information theory, bringing quantitative methods to a traditionally humanistic domain.

## Core Contributions
1. A multi-level framework explicitly modeling creativity as transformation across five distinct representational levels (lexical, semantic, conceptual, structural, narrative) rather than a single notion of "similarity."
2. Introduction of directional alignment methods and control-calibrated similarity measures tailored to detect which representational levels show preservation vs. divergence.
3. Empirical demonstration that different literary pairs exhibit distinct "transformation profiles"—some preserve narrative structure while innovating lexically, others do the opposite—showing that imitation is selective and multi-dimensional.
4. A quantitative operationalization of how classical theories (Tarde, Baldwin) of imitation-based creativity can be measured computationally in real texts.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[stylometry]] and authorship attribution (computational methods for analyzing textual patterns), though applied to transformation rather than identification. It connects to work on [[paraphrase detection]] and [[semantic similarity]] in NLP, but extends beyond single-level comparison. The theoretical framing echoes [[intertextuality]] studies in literary criticism and connects to broader work on [[computational creativity]] and [[cultural evolution modeling]]. It also touches on [[interpretability]] questions: understanding how neural models might learn multi-level transformations implicitly.

## Practical Applications
- **Literary analysis tools**: Publishers and scholars could use transformation profiles to automatically map influence relationships in literary corpora, identifying which historical works shaped later writing and in what specific ways.
- **Plagiarism and authorship verification**: More nuanced detection of derived works by identifying which representational levels show suspicious alignment with source texts.
- **Creative writing assistance**: Tools that suggest transformations at specific representational levels (e.g., "keep the narrative structure but vary semantic content") to help writers remix or adapt existing stories.
- **Cultural heritage indexing**: Large-scale computational analysis of how literary traditions evolve across centuries, documenting which elements persist and which diverge in adaptations, remakes, and translations.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Literary Influence Map**: A search engine where writers query "show me all texts that transformed X by changing structure but keeping semantics," helping them discover stylistic precedents and creative patterns.
2. **Adaptive Writing Coach**: A tool that analyzes a user's draft against a source text and suggests specific representational levels to modify, giving real-time feedback on imitation vs. originality.
3. **Copyright & Attribution Analytics**: SaaS platform for legal teams and publishers to assess derived work claims by quantifying which representational levels overlap with alleged source materials.
4. **Creative Remix Generator**: Given a source text, algorithmically generate candidate transformations at specified representational levels (e.g., "new lexicon, same structure"), helping screenwriters, novelists, or game designers brainstorm adaptations.
5. **Academic & Cultural Heritage Explorer**: A university library tool that visualizes how canonical texts were transformed across centuries, showing students how literary traditions evolve—useful for humanities education.

## Technical Limitations
- **Ground truth ambiguity**: Defining what counts as "preservation" vs. "divergence" at each representational level requires subjective judgment. The paper relies on historically documented relationships, but many real literary influences are implicit or disputed.
- **Scalability and representation selection**: The five levels chosen (lexical, semantic, conceptual, structural, narrative) are illustrative, not exhaustive. Different genres or languages may require different representational schemes.
- **Alignment method assumptions**: Directional alignment and similarity measures assume texts can be meaningfully mapped to one another, which breaks down for very distant or loosely related works.
- **Lack of neural baseline**: The paper appears to use classical NLP methods; comparison with modern [[large-language-models]] or [[transformers]] for encoding representations is absent, limiting claims about generalizability.
- **Small-scale evaluation**: The work is demonstrated on "historically documented literary relationships," likely a modest corpus. Scaling to thousands of texts and validating findings against broader datasets remains unclear.

## Open Questions
- How do the discovered transformation profiles correlate with critical reception, novelty, or commercial success of derived works? Does divergence at particular levels predict critical acclaim?
- Can this framework scale to non-Western literary traditions or oral literature, where representational levels and notions of imitation differ?
- How do modern [[large-language-models]] implicitly capture multi-level transformation? Do they learn separate representational levels or conflate them?
- Can the framework predict *which* representational level an author *should* transform to maximize novelty while maintaining recognizability?
- How does the framework handle translation, adaptation across media (novel-to-film), or works spanning centuries, where language and context shift radically?

## Key Takeaway
Creative works transform prior texts at different representational levels; quantifying which levels preserve vs. diverge reveals how imitation and originality intertwine.
