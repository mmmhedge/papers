# Towards Communication-Efficient Social Intelligence in Language Agents

**Authors:** Linxiao Gong, Yijie Xu, Tianfu Wang, Yin Wu, Yili Wang et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35749v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35749v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles a practical problem: how can AI agents communicate with humans and other agents efficiently—saying just enough to achieve their goals without wasting words or attention. The authors propose Teacher-Assisted Communication Training (TACT), a method that trains agents to negotiate and coordinate better while using fewer tokens, tested on social interaction benchmarks where it achieves strong goal completion with substantially reduced communication costs.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on recent progress in [[large-language-models]] for multi-turn dialogue and agent interaction. It draws on two key ideas: (1) **communication efficiency** as a design objective in agent training, borrowing from economics and game theory where agents face tradeoffs between clarity and cost; (2) **on-policy distillation**, a reinforcement learning technique where a student model learns from feedback generated by executing candidate actions rather than from fixed offline data. The paper also references [[SOTOPIA]] and [[AgentSense]]—benchmarks designed to evaluate how well language agents handle complex social scenarios involving negotiation and coordination.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal characterization of communication efficiency**: The paper breaks down efficiency into two dimensions—**action strategy** (what the agent should try to achieve) and **expression** (how to say it concisely)—and shows these effects propagate through multi-turn dialogue.

2. **TACT framework**: A two-specialist teacher system where an **expression specialist** removes unnecessary detail while preserving intent, and a **strategy specialist** proposes alternative approaches that better satisfy a partner's constraints. Crucially, both specialists test their revisions by sampling partner responses before the teacher decides which revision to use.

3. **Balanced selection criterion**: Rather than greedily maximizing goal attainment, TACT selects which teacher revision to use by weighing both goal support and action-token cost, preventing the agent from "over-communicating" to guarantee success.

4. **On-policy student training**: The selected teacher reference guides distillation on the student's own generation prefixes, enabling the student to act independently at deployment without needing teacher guidance.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[reinforcement-learning]] and [[agents]], extending prior work on agent instruction-following and fine-tuning (like SFT+SDPO baselines) by adding an explicit efficiency objective. It relates to [[RLHF]] in spirit—using feedback signals to train better behavior—but applies that idea to efficiency and social reasoning rather than just helpfulness. The negotiation and coordination framing connects to [[multi-agent-systems]] literature on protocol design and efficient information exchange. The distillation approach resembles teacher-student frameworks in knowledge distillation, but applied to dialogue-level policy refinement.

## Practical Applications
1. **Customer service chatbots**: Reduce response length and number of clarification rounds, lowering operational cost while maintaining customer satisfaction.
2. **Negotiation and procurement agents**: Agents that strike deals or resolve conflicts with minimal back-and-forth, useful in supply chain and contract automation.
3. **Multi-agent coordination in constrained bandwidth**: Agents operating in environments with communication limits (e.g., swarm robotics, distributed sensor networks) need to say what matters most.
4. **Accessibility tools**: Agents for users with attention limitations or those relying on expensive text-to-speech or speech-to-text systems benefit from concise, purposeful communication.
5. **Real-time collaborative AI**: Co-pilots that assist in time-sensitive tasks (surgery, emergency response, live coding) where verbose explanations waste critical time.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Efficient Agent-as-a-Service platform**: Provide a fine-tuning-as-a-service for enterprises where TACT optimizes LLM agents for low-latency, low-token customer interactions—charge per interaction saved.
2. **Negotiation AI for SMBs**: B2B SaaS tool that automates contract negotiation and supplier discussions with minimal communication rounds; sell to procurement teams or law firms.
3. **Bandwidth-optimized conversational AI for IoT/edge**: Embed communication-efficient agents in resource-constrained environments (e.g., rural healthcare bots over low-bandwidth connections) as a managed service.
4. **Training analytics for agent efficiency**: Offer enterprises a dashboard to audit and optimize agent communication patterns in their deployed systems, identifying where TACT-like training could save costs.
5. **Accessibility-first chatbot SDK**: Provide developers an open-source or low-cost toolkit to build concise, efficient conversational agents specifically for users with accessibility needs, differentiated by communication minimalism.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Dependence on partner model quality**: TACT samples partner responses to evaluate revisions, but if the simulated partner is poor or unrepresentative, the selection criterion becomes unreliable. Real human partners may behave differently.
2. **Scalability of two-specialist system**: Running multiple candidate revisions and sampling partner responses for each adds computational cost. For large-scale deployment, this overhead may be prohibitive.
3. **Goal-token tradeoff tuning**: The balance between goal attainment and communication cost is controlled implicitly through the selection criterion. The paper doesn't fully explore sensitivity to this tradeoff or how to set it for different use cases (e.g., when should an agent prioritize completion over brevity?).
4. **Limited scope of benchmarks**: SOTOPIA and AgentSense are relatively constrained social scenarios. It's unclear whether efficiency gains hold for more open-ended, adversarial, or safety-critical negotiations.
5. **No formal guarantee on independence**: The paper claims the student can act independently at deployment, but the student is trained on teacher-selected revisions. If the teacher's preferences misalign with deployment conditions, performance could degrade.

## Open Questions
1. **How does TACT perform in adversarial settings** where a partner actively misinterprets brief messages or exploits conciseness? Does minimalism reduce robustness?
2. **Can TACT be extended to multi-party negotiation**, where efficiency must balance across multiple agents with conflicting goals?
3. **What is the compute overhead of TACT at scale**, and can the two-specialist design be made more efficient (e.g., by combining specialists or using cheaper partner models)?
4. **How does human evaluation of "goal success" compare to automatic metrics?** Humans may value brevity or tone differently than the benchmarks capture.
5. **Can insights from TACT transfer to non-dialogue tasks**, such as summarization or report generation, where efficiency is also valuable?
6. **Does TACT work with smaller or open-source LLMs**, or is it primarily a technique for large proprietary models?

## Key Takeaway
Agents can achieve social goals with fewer words by training on strategically chosen, concise revisions rather than raw dialogues.
