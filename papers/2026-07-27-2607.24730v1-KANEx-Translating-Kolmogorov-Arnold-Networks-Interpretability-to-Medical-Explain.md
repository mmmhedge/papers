# KANEx: Translating Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks' Interpretability to Medical Explainability

**Authors:** Krithi Shailya, Ananya Lakshmi Ravi, Venkatanathan K. V., Sowmya S. Sundaram, Gokul S. Krishnan et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-27 | **arXiv:** [2607.24730v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24730v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces KANEx, a framework that combines Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) with Vision-Language Models to make chest X-ray classifiers more interpretable and trustworthy for clinicians. Instead of treating a neural network as a black box and only adding natural-language explanations on top, the authors leverage KANs' inherently interpretable spline-based components to ground those explanations in mathematically transparent reasoning. On the MIMIC-CXR dataset, this approach produces more faithful saliency maps and better visual localization than standard methods.

## Theoretical Background
[[Deep learning]] models excel at medical image classification but traditionally lack interpretability — clinicians cannot understand *why* the model made a decision. Vision-Language Models (VLMs) like CLIP or similar architectures can generate natural-language explanations, but these add linguistic fluency without addressing the underlying opacity of the visual backbone.

Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) are a relatively recent architecture inspired by the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem from approximation theory. Unlike standard neural networks with fixed activation functions, KANs use learnable spline functions at each connection, making the learned transformations more explicitly visible and mathematically interpretable. A spline is a piecewise polynomial function — essentially, the network learns smooth curves rather than opaque weight matrices.

The paper builds on two research threads: (1) [[mechanistic-interpretability]] in deep learning — the effort to understand what neural networks compute internally — and (2) explainability in medical AI, where trust and auditability are critical for clinical adoption.

## Core Contributions
1. **First application of KANs to medical explainability**: Demonstrates that KAN's spline-based transparency can be leveraged to improve both visual and textual explanations, not just to build faster or smaller models.

2. **KAN-Map**: A novel saliency/heatmap generation method derived directly from KAN activations and spline functions, rather than relying on gradient-based approximations (like Grad-CAM). This grounds visual attribution in the actual learned functional units.

3. **Empirical gains on MIMIC-CXR**: Shows ~10% improvement in visual localization quality and semantic similarity of generated explanations when KANs replace dense layers in ResNet/ViT backbones. Saliency maps are more faithful (better align with clinician annotations or ground truth).

4. **Concrete framework**: Demonstrates an end-to-end pipeline: KAN-based vision model → KAN-Map (symbolic heatmaps) → VLM reasoning → natural-language explanation, all grounded in interpretable units.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[mechanistic-interpretability]] — understanding what networks compute — with medical AI explainability. It relates to:

- **Gradient-based attribution methods** (Grad-CAM, integrated gradients, etc.): KAN-Map offers a non-gradient alternative, rooted directly in learned spline functions.
- **[[Attention Is All You Need]] and Vision Transformers**: The paper uses ViT as a backbone, but replaces the final dense layers with KANs to improve interpretability.
- **Concept-based explanations**: Rather than learning abstract concepts, KANs expose mathematical operations (splines) that are inherently more interpretable.
- **Multimodal explanations**: Related to work pairing vision models with language models for explanation (VilBERT, BLIP, etc.), but grounding explanations in symbolic transparency rather than just post-hoc text generation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical decision support**: Radiologists reviewing chest X-ray classifications get both a confident prediction *and* a mathematically grounded explanation (heatmap + text), reducing cognitive load and increasing trust in the system.

2. **Regulatory compliance and audit trails**: Medical devices (FDA-regulated) increasingly require interpretability. KAN-based models can generate auditable reasoning paths, easing certification.

3. **Transfer to other medical imaging domains**: The approach can be adapted to CT, MRI, or histopathology image analysis where visual localization and textual reasoning are both important.

4. **Teaching and training**: Medical students could use KAN-based explanations to learn what visual features drive diagnostic decisions in a transparent, reproducible way.

5. **Rare disease diagnosis**: In cases with limited training data, the learned spline functions can be more interpretable and easier to validate by domain experts than opaque weight matrices.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **MedAI Explainability SaaS**: A B2B platform that hospitals/radiologists can plug into existing imaging infrastructure. Converts any chest X-ray classification into a KAN-based explanation (heatmap + natural language) in real time. Revenue: per-scan licensing or per-radiologist seat.

2. **Clinical AI Validation Toolkit**: Sell to hospital IT/compliance teams a toolkit for auditing and certifying AI models used in clinical workflows. KAN-Map visualizations serve as "ground truth" for model behavior, reducing liability and speeding FDA approval.

3. **Insurance Risk Assessments**: Life/health insurance companies deploy KAN-based X-ray classifiers to underwrite policies or flag high-risk applicants. Transparent reasoning reduces disputes and litigation.

4. **AI-Assisted Radiologist Training Platform**: Offer radiologists interactive tutorials showing how KAN models "see" pathology (via spline interpretations and saliency maps), improving diagnostic accuracy and reducing burnout.

5. **Third-party AI Audit Service**: Specialize in validating medical AI models for hospitals and vendors. Use KAN-Map and symbolic transparency to generate audit reports, reducing hospital liability and vendor's certification timelines.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational overhead**: KANs are computationally heavier than standard dense layers (spline evaluation is costlier than matrix multiplication). Real-time deployment in clinical settings may require careful optimization.

2. **Scalability to high-resolution images**: The paper tests on MIMIC-CXR (standard resolution). Ultra-high-resolution imaging (pathology slides, 3D volumes) may hit memory and speed constraints.

3. **Limited to image classification**: The framework is demonstrated on binary/multi-class classification. Segmentation, detection, or 3D reconstruction tasks are not addressed.

4. **VLM dependency**: The final explanation quality still depends on the downstream VLM. A weak or biased VLM can still produce poor explanations, even with perfect visual grounding. The paper doesn't ablate this.

5. **Validation on small datasets**: MIMIC-CXR is large, but the paper doesn't explore behavior with truly small, domain-specific datasets (e.g., rare disease cohorts).

6. **KAN inductive bias**: Spline-based functions assume smooth functional relationships. Pathology that violates this (e.g., sharp discontinuities) may not be well-represented.

7. **Clinical validation lacking**: No user studies with radiologists. It's unclear whether clinicians actually *trust* KAN explanations more or find them useful in practice.

## Open Questions
1. **Radiologist user studies**: Do clinicians find KAN-based explanations more trustworthy or actionable than gradient-based methods? Do they change clinical decisions?

2. **Failure mode analysis**: When do KANs produce unfaithful saliency maps? Are there systematic pathology types that KAN-Map misses?

3. **Adversarial robustness**: Are KAN-based explanations more robust to adversarial perturbations than standard models? Can an attacker fool both the KAN classifier and its explanations simultaneously?

4. **Generalization across domains**: Does a KAN trained on MIMIC-CXR transfer well to other hospital datasets or imaging modalities? How sensitive is KAN-Map to dataset shift?

5. **Spline interpretability in practice**: Can radiologists or cardiologists *directly* interpret the learned spline functions, or do they still need the natural-language layer? Is there a middle ground (e.g., human-readable basis functions)?

6. **Comparison to other interpretable architectures**: How does KANEx compare to other inherently interpretable models (e.g., prototype networks, decision trees, rule-based systems) in a medical setting?

7. **Computational efficiency**: Can KANs be pruned, quantized, or distilled into faster models without losing interpretability gains?

## Key Takeaway
KANs' transparent spline functions can ground medical image explanations in mathematics, improving saliency maps and text quality over black-box models.
