# From Values to Benchmarks: Evaluating Large Language Models for Governmental Use in Dutch

**Authors:** Laurens Samson, Iva Gornishka, Gossa Lô, Yuki M. Asano, Sennay Ghebreab
**Published:** 2026-08-10 | **arXiv:** [2608.09925v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09925v1)

## Summary
This paper develops a systematic evaluation framework for assessing large language models in Dutch governmental settings, identifying six key dimensions (factuality, honesty, bias, energy use, cost, transparency) and benchmarking 30+ models. The work matters because governments need tools to choose LLMs responsibly, yet existing evaluation frameworks ignore public-sector values and non-English linguistic needs.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on the growing literature evaluating LLMs across multiple dimensions beyond pure accuracy. Prior work has examined factuality, bias, and transparency in isolation; this work integrates these into a unified framework designed for public administration contexts. The distinction between factuality (correctness of answers) and honesty (whether a model admits uncertainty) draws from work on calibration and uncertainty quantification in neural networks. The evaluation framework reflects broader concerns in [[alignment]] research — ensuring AI systems behave according to human values — adapted here to governance rather than safety-critical scenarios.

## Core Contributions
- Identifies and operationalizes six evaluation dimensions specific to governmental LLM deployment, grounded in stakeholder consultation rather than researcher intuition
- Demonstrates that factuality and honesty are independently governed properties (high factuality does not ensure high honesty)
- Reveals unavoidable trade-offs: better model quality correlates with higher environmental cost and financial expense, while bias remains largely orthogonal to both
- Provides a multilingual benchmark covering Dutch-specific and general models, with quantitative results for each dimension
- Delivers a non-technical interface designed for policymakers and civil servants, not just engineers

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Attention Is All You Need]] era model evaluation and the broader [[alignment]] movement. It extends prior benchmarking work (e.g., HELM, SuperGLUE) by incorporating environmental and fiscal costs alongside performance metrics — reflecting the "Responsible AI" paradigm. The factuality-honesty distinction parallels work on model calibration and epistemic uncertainty in deep learning. The focus on non-English contexts fills a gap: most LLM evaluation assumes English-language deployment, yet many deployment contexts (especially government) are linguistically local.

## Practical Applications
- **Municipal service chatbots**: Governments can use the benchmark to select models for citizen-facing services (permit applications, benefit inquiries) that balance cost, accuracy, and appropriate uncertainty acknowledgment
- **Policy drafting assistance**: Civil servants writing regulations need models that admit knowledge gaps rather than generate plausible-sounding but false citations
- **Multi-language public administration**: The framework applies directly to any non-English-speaking government facing LLM procurement decisions
- **Regulatory compliance**: Frameworks like the EU AI Act require transparency in training data and environmental footprint — this benchmark provides the measurement infrastructure

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **GovAI Procurement Platform**: A SaaS tool that helps mid-sized municipalities evaluate and compare LLMs on the six dimensions, with automated reporting for procurement committees and CFOs
2. **Bias & Factuality Audit-as-a-Service**: Specialized consulting for government IT departments, using the framework to audit their deployed chatbots quarterly and provide actionable remediation
3. **European Government LLM Benchmark**: Expand the Dutch framework to all EU member states with language-specific and regulatory variants, positioning as a public-sector standard
4. **Carbon-Aware Model Router**: An inference layer that dynamically selects models based on query type, balancing accuracy, cost, and environmental impact in real-time for deployed services
5. **Transparency Dashboard for Citizens**: Public-facing tool showing which models their government uses, their bias scores, training data provenance, and environmental cost — increasing institutional accountability

## Technical Limitations
- **Model selection bias**: Evaluation is limited to models accessible at benchmark time; proprietary or recently released models may not be included
- **Dimension operationalization challenges**: Converting abstract values like "honesty" into quantifiable metrics involves judgment calls that may not transfer across contexts
- **Language scope**: While multilingual, the evaluation is optimized for Dutch; transfer to other low-resource languages is unclear
- **Static snapshot**: LLM capabilities evolve rapidly; benchmark results become stale within months for frontier models
- **Trade-off quantification**: The paper identifies trade-offs (quality vs. cost vs. environmental impact) but doesn't provide a unified metric for governments to optimize jointly — policymakers must still make subjective choices
- **Bias measurement limitations**: Bias evaluation depends on test data composition; systematic biases not represented in benchmarks remain invisible
- **Honesty as proxy**: Acknowledging uncertainty doesn't guarantee the model provides correct information when confident — honesty is necessary but insufficient

## Open Questions
- How stable are the factuality-honesty trade-offs as models scale? Do larger models maintain this independence?
- Can the evaluation framework adapt to real-world deployment scenarios (e.g., specialized Dutch legal documents) beyond general benchmarks?
- How should governments weight the six dimensions? Is there a principled way to set priorities, or must each organization choose subjectively?
- Does fine-tuning on Dutch administrative texts improve all dimensions equally, or does it change the observed trade-offs?
- How do these findings generalize to other non-English-speaking governments with different administrative values (e.g., Germany, France)?
- Can the framework predict which models will maintain performance under distribution shift (e.g., novel policy domains the model hasn't seen)?

## Key Takeaway
Dutch governments can now systematically choose language models using a public-sector evaluation framework, but no single model wins across cost, quality, bias, and environmental impact.
