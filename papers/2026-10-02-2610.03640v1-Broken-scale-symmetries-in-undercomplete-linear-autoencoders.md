# Broken scale symmetries in undercomplete linear autoencoders

**Authors:** Farhad Pashakhanloo, Jacob A. Zavatone-Veth
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03640v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03640v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how finite-stepsize stochastic gradient descent (SGD) breaks scale symmetries in undercomplete linear autoencoders — a toy model where you can scale up decoder weights and down encoder weights without changing the network output. The authors show that SGD has a *directional* preference: it consistently drifts toward large decoder weights on a slow timescale, eventually hitting a stability boundary. This illustrates a deeper principle: gradient noise can be converted into systematic motion along a manifold of equivalent solutions.

## Theoretical Background
Neural networks have many symmetries — redundancies in how you can reparameterize parameters without changing outputs. In homogeneous networks (where scaling one layer and inverse-scaling another preserves function), this creates a continuous manifold of equivalent solutions. Prior work showed SGD breaks symmetries by balancing gradient noise or minimizing loss fluctuations. Linear autoencoders — networks that map input → hidden bottleneck → reconstructed output — are a canonical model for studying implicit regularization, the tendency of SGD to prefer certain solutions over others. This paper leverages the fact that undercomplete autoencoders (where the bottleneck is smaller than input dimension) have a well-characterized solution manifold related to principal component analysis (PCA).

## Core Contributions
1. **Directed scale drift**: Shows SGD doesn't break scale symmetry randomly — it systematically favors large decoder weights, breaking the symmetry in a *preferred direction* tied to solution geometry.
2. **Slow-timescale analysis**: Characterizes this drift as occurring on a slow timescale and derives an analytically-tractable effective dynamical description.
3. **Finite-stepsize constraints**: Demonstrates that scale drift cannot continue indefinitely; it terminates when SGD approaches a stability boundary (loss landscape becomes too sharp for the step size).
4. **Sharpness paradox**: Shows solutions are sharper by some measures (maximum Hessian eigenvalue) but not others — revealing that "sharpness" as an implicit regularizer is measure-dependent.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends the literature on [[implicit regularization]] — how SGD implicitly favors certain solutions — by studying symmetry-breaking in a concrete, analytically-solvable model. It relates to recent work on loss landscape geometry and the role of gradient noise in neural network training. The finite-stepsize stability analysis connects to classical numerical analysis of gradient descent. The result that SGD converts noise into directed motion along solution manifolds complements work on neural network implicit bias and generalization bounds.

## Practical Applications
- **Autoencoder design**: Understanding SGD's preference for certain weight scales informs hyperparameter choices (step size, network width) for dimensionality reduction tasks.
- **Interpretability**: Explains why autoencoders trained with SGD produce decoder weights with particular magnitudes — useful for model inspection and debugging.
- **Training stability**: The analysis of finite-stepsize boundaries helps predict when autoencoders will become unstable during training, informing step-size selection.
- **Implicit regularization**: Provides concrete evidence for how gradient noise induces structured solutions, relevant for theoretical understanding of any SGD-trained model.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Autoencoder optimization toolkit**: A library that predicts the optimal step size and predicts when scale drift will cause instability, preventing training collapse for large-scale autoencoders.
2. **Neural network diagnosis tool**: Software that detects directional symmetry breaking in trained networks to identify when implicit regularization is working as intended vs. causing pathological behavior.
3. **Adaptive learning rate scheduler**: A step-size controller that monitors proximity to finite-stepsize stability boundaries and adjusts learning rates to maintain productive training dynamics.
4. **Synthetic data quality monitor**: Use scale-drift signatures as a metric to assess whether training data has sufficient gradient noise diversity for healthy implicit regularization.
5. **Theoretical ML consulting**: Advisory service for deep learning teams debugging training instabilities by analyzing whether solutions are hitting stability boundaries predicted by this theory.

## Technical Limitations
- **Linear autoencoders only**: Results apply to undercomplete linear autoencoders; nonlinear networks are dramatically more complex and may not exhibit the same directed symmetry breaking.
- **Slow-timescale approximation**: The effective dynamics are valid only on slow timescales; short-term training behavior is not captured.
- **PCA manifold assumption**: Analysis assumes solutions stay near the PCA manifold; solutions leaving this manifold invalidate the model.
- **Homogeneous networks**: Results exploit homogeneity (scaling properties); networks with biases or batch normalization break this structure.
- **Toy model**: Autoencoders are simpler than modern deep networks; whether this principle holds for ResNets, transformers, or other architectures remains unclear.
- **Sharpness is ambiguous**: The measure-dependence of sharpness means this paper doesn't fully explain *which* sharpness measure matters for generalization.

## Open Questions
- Does directed symmetry breaking occur in nonlinear autoencoders or deeper architectures?
- Can the effective dynamics description be extended to networks with biases, batch normalization, or other common components?
- Does the stability boundary prediction hold empirically for real training runs, and can it be used to design better adaptive optimizers?
- How does momentum (in SGD with momentum) alter the symmetry-breaking direction?
- Which sharpness measure is actually predictive of generalization, and does the preference for certain solutions correlate with that measure?
- Does this theory explain the implicit bias of SGD in other canonically undercomplete settings (e.g., matrix factorization, overparameterized regression)?

## Key Takeaway
SGD systematically biases undercomplete autoencoders toward large decoder weights, converting gradient noise into directed drift along a manifold of equivalent solutions.
