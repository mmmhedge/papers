# Adaptive Memory and Reflection Multi-Agent System for Medical Question Answering

**Authors:** Pradeep Murugesan, Luoxiao Yang, Xueli Chen, Xinqi Fan
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.19029v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19029v1)

## Summary
This paper presents AMR, a multi-agent system for medical question answering that uses specialized agents with persistent memory and reflection-based feedback to solve complex medical cases. Unlike traditional single-agent systems, it routes questions through solo, collaborative, or escalated workflows, and includes ethical oversight—achieving strong results on medical QA benchmarks while emphasizing trustworthiness in healthcare settings.

## Theoretical Background
Medical question answering builds on retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) — combining language models with external knowledge retrieval to ground answers in factual information. This paper extends prior work in multi-agent systems (where multiple specialized agents collaborate on tasks) and [[RLHF]]-like reflection mechanisms that use feedback loops to improve reasoning. The key insight borrowed from cognitive science is that persistent memory and structured reflection — reviewing prior cases and decisions — help agents reason more accurately. The system also draws on complexity routing ideas from cascading classifiers, where different pathways handle different problem difficulties.

## Core Contributions
1. **Adaptive Memory Module**: Each agent maintains dedicated memory of prior cases and reasoning paths, retrieved dynamically during inference rather than relying on static retrieval.
2. **Reflection-Based Feedback**: Agents generate structured self-critique and learn from past mistakes, improving subsequent reasoning without explicit retraining.
3. **Complexity-Driven Workflow Routing**: Questions are automatically routed to solo, collaborative (multiple agents), or escalated (human expert) pathways based on assessed difficulty.
4. **Ethical Oversight**: A dedicated overseer module reviews outputs for fairness, safety, and factual accuracy before surfacing answers.
5. **Empirical validation**: Ablation studies isolate the contribution of memory, reflection, and retrieval—showing all three components are necessary for strong performance on MedQA and MedMCQA.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Retrieval-Augmented Generation]] (RAG), multi-agent reasoning frameworks (related to chain-of-thought prompting and agent hierarchies), and trustworthy AI. It relates to recent work on agent planning and tool use (e.g., [[ReAct]]), but extends those ideas with persistent episodic memory and structured reflection. The ethical oversight module echoes safety work in [[Alignment|alignment]] — ensuring model outputs meet human values. Unlike monolithic language model approaches, this embraces decomposition and specialization, similar to mixture-of-experts architectures.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical Decision Support**: Hospital systems using AMR to help junior doctors or nurses triage patient questions, with escalation to specialists for rare or high-stakes cases.
2. **Patient-Facing Health Portals**: Trustworthy chatbots answering patient questions on common conditions, with confidence scores and links to medical literature.
3. **Medical Education**: Training platforms where students ask complex diagnostic questions and receive not just answers but reasoning chains they can learn from.
4. **Insurance/Claims Review**: Automated systems reviewing medical claims and flagging edge cases for human adjudication, maintaining audit trails.
5. **Regulatory Compliance**: Medical AI systems that document their reasoning and safety checks—critical for FDA approval or other healthcare regulations.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **MedAssist Pro**: A SaaS platform for small clinics and urgent care centers—white-label AMR backend that integrates with EMR systems, handles patient Q&A, and flags complex cases for licensed physician review. Revenue: per-query or per-patient-per-month.
2. **Clinical Evidence Assistant**: Browser extension for clinicians doing literature reviews; uses AMR to surface relevant prior cases, treatments, and outcomes from internal case databases and PubMed, with reflection-driven recommendation ranking.
3. **Medical Legal Discovery Tool**: SaaS for medical malpractice defense firms—AMR reasons over past cases and depositions to identify relevant precedents and weak points in expert testimony. Licensing to law firms.
4. **Pharmacy Consultation Engine**: Medication question answering for retail pharmacies—handles drug interactions, contraindications, and patient education with escalation to pharmacist for ambiguous cases. B2B SaaS to pharmacy chains.
5. **Medical Education Tutor**: B2C app for medical students and board exam prep—AMR answers case-based questions with step-by-step reasoning, memory of student mistakes, and adaptive difficulty. Freemium model with subscription for advanced features.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Hallucination Risk**: Even with retrieval and oversight, language models can confabulate plausible-sounding medical facts. The paper doesn't fully solve confidence calibration or detection of false-but-confident answers.
2. **Memory Scalability**: The described memory module's performance on truly large case databases (millions of prior cases) is untested; retrieval latency and storage costs could become prohibitive.
3. **Domain Shift**: Evaluation is on benchmark datasets (MedQA, MedMCQA), which may not represent real clinical diversity. Generalization to rare conditions or non-English medical contexts is unclear.
4. **Ethical Overseer Design**: The paper doesn't detail how the ethical overseer is trained or what criteria it uses. Disagreement between oversight module and clinical ground truth is not analyzed.
5. **Bias & Fairness**: No explicit audit for demographic bias in medical recommendations or disparate performance across patient populations is reported.
6. **Regulatory Path Unclear**: It's unclear whether this architecture meets FDA or other healthcare regulatory requirements for transparency and auditability in clinical decision support.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance degrade as the memory database grows to millions of cases? Is there a retrieval algorithm that scales efficiently?
2. Can the system reliably detect when it *doesn't know* the answer and should escalate, rather than confidently guessing?
3. How should the ethical overseer be trained? Should it be learned from physician feedback, rule-based, or hybrid?
4. Does the system perform equally well on rare diseases and edge-case presentations, or does it overfit to common conditions in training data?
5. How does multi-turn conversation affect memory and reflection—does the system remember earlier questions in a dialogue and adjust reasoning accordingly?
6. Can reflection-based feedback improve performance *within a session* (few-shot learning) without retraining, or only across sessions?

## Key Takeaway
Multi-agent systems with persistent memory and self-reflection improve medical QA reliability and enable safer, more trustworthy clinical AI.
