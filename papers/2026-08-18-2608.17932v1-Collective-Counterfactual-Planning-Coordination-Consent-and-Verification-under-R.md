# Collective Counterfactual Planning: Coordination, Consent, and Verification under Representational Constraints

**Authors:** Chainarong Amornbunchornvej
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.17932v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17932v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Collective Counterfactual Planning (CCP), a formal framework for understanding how teams coordinate to solve problems that no individual member can handle alone. The key insight is that coordination failures often stem not from lack of capability or knowledge, but from *representational constraints*—each agent sees and understands only a limited projection of the shared task space. The paper defines when a team can provably reach a shared goal and verify completion under these geometric limitations.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on multi-agent coordination theory and formal verification, but introduces a novel constraint: representational geometry. Rather than assuming agents have incomplete information (standard in game theory and distributed systems), this framework assumes agents have *incomplete understanding* of the state space itself—like team members working through different windows into the same building.

The paper relates to classical coordination problems in distributed systems and multi-agent planning, but reframes the bottleneck as subspace structure. It invokes concepts from linear algebra (projections onto agent-specific subspaces) and formal logic (soundness and completeness of planning schemes) to characterize what goals remain "verifiable" even when representational windows don't fully overlap.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal model of representational constraints**: Formalizes how agent-specific subspace projections create genuine (not epistemic) limitations on what can be planned, consented to, and verified.

2. **Collective Counterfactual Solvability (CCS) problem**: Separates three layers—geometric feasibility, executable attainment, and validated completion—exposing that a trajectory can succeed accidentally while remaining unverifiable.

3. **Positive-negative duality**: Shows that iterated cross-agent relay can unlock solutions unavailable to one-shot pooling, but goals depending on "dark subspaces" (invisible to the entire team) cannot be validly completed, regardless of accidental success.

4. **Multi-gate framework**: Identifies four gates (implementation coalitions, conception, consent, verification qualification) and shows memoryless vs. audited consent constrain different objects without dominance.

5. **Exhaustive solvability scheme**: Provides a sound and complete four-step algorithm under exact representation, with restricted variants that remain sound.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[multi-agent reinforcement learning]] in its treatment of coordination, but differs by focusing on representational rather than informational barriers. It shares DNA with distributed systems verification (e.g., consensus protocols) but applies geometric constraints instead. The "dark subspace" insight echoes ideas in mechanistic interpretability about unobservable latent structure. The framework also connects to organizational theory in computer science—how teams decompose tasks at expertise boundaries—but formalizes it algebraically.

## Practical Applications
1. **Surgical team coordination**: Surgeons, anesthesiologists, and nurses each see different aspects of the patient state and procedure; the framework models when they can collectively verify a safe outcome.

2. **Autonomous vehicle fleets**: Vehicles with heterogeneous sensor suites (lidar, camera, radar) perceive the road differently; determines what team-level safety properties are verifiable.

3. **Supply chain verification**: Suppliers and logistics partners each track disjoint aspects of a shipment; identifies which end-to-end guarantees can be legitimately audited.

4. **Collaborative robotics**: Robot arms with different sensor coverage and control capabilities must jointly verify task completion in assembly lines.

5. **Distributed financial auditing**: Auditors at different branch offices verify compliance with only local views; determines what fraud-detection goals are achievable.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Representational Coordination Platform**: SaaS tool that maps team member roles to subspaces, automatically identifies coordination bottlenecks, and suggests process redesigns to unlock latent solutions.

2. **Verifiable Multi-Party Computation for Enterprise**: Use the CCP framework to design workflows where teams execute sensitive tasks (e.g., joint financial decision-making) with provable completion guarantees despite incomplete visibility.

3. **Surgical Team Analytics**: Real-time dashboard analyzing whether a surgical team's representational configuration (which surgeon sees what monitor, which anesthesiologist monitors which vitals) can collectively verify patient safety milestones.

4. **Autonomous Fleet Orchestration**: Middleware for heterogeneous robot/vehicle fleets that proves which safety or completion goals are verifiable given their sensor configurations, and re-assigns tasks if necessary.

5. **Organizational Process Auditing**: Consulting tool that models organizational workflows as multi-agent systems, identifies which compliance goals are verifiable, and flags high-risk hidden subspaces in critical processes.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Linearity assumption**: The framework assumes representational subspaces are linear; real agent understanding may be nonlinear and context-dependent.

2. **Static subspaces**: Once a subspace is defined, the model doesn't capture agents learning or expanding their representational windows over time.

3. **Exact relay closure requirement**: The exhaustive algorithm requires exact computation of relay closure; approximation behavior and computational complexity under large agent counts are unstudied.

4. **Memoryless consent model**: Assumes agents cannot reason about cumulative history when deciding consent; real teams use accumulated context to update trust and decisions.

5. **Conjunction-only goals**: The model focuses on conjunctive goals; disjunctive or probabilistic goal specifications are not addressed.

6. **Synchronization**: Assumes coordinated, step-wise execution; asynchronous, concurrent action is not modeled.

## Open Questions
1. **Learning and adaptation**: How do agent subspaces evolve as they execute plans together? Can relay structure be exploited to *grow* representational capability?

2. **Computational complexity**: What is the complexity of computing CCS for large teams? Are there approximation or greedy schemes with guarantees?

3. **Stochastic extensions**: How does the framework extend to probabilistic state transitions or partial observability?

4. **Incentive alignment**: If agents are strategic (not cooperative), how does representational constraint interact with strategic misrepresentation?

5. **Empirical validation**: How well does the geometric model predict failure modes in real team coordination (surgical teams, supply chains, robot swarms)?

6. **Goal negotiation**: When no solution exists, how can agents negotiate to *redefine* goals into verifiable ones?

## Key Takeaway
Teams can solve problems individuals cannot, but only if representational gaps don't hide the proof of success.
