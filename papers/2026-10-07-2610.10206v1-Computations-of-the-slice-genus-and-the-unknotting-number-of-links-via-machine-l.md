# Computations of the slice genus and the unknotting number of links via machine learning

**Authors:** Yutong Dai, Oliver Hayman, András Juhász, Ludovico Morellato
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10206v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10206v1)

## Summary
This paper uses reinforcement learning and Bayesian optimization to compute difficult-to-calculate properties of mathematical knots and links — specifically the slice genus and unknotting number. By training agents to perform knot manipulations, the researchers found new upper bounds on these invariants and, by combining with known lower bounds, determined exact values in many previously unsolved cases. This bridges machine learning and pure topology, showing that neural agents can discover non-obvious solutions to classical geometric problems.

## Theoretical Background
Knot theory studies how circles can be embedded and tangled in three-dimensional space. A "link" is a collection of disjoint circles (called components), and mathematicians care about invariants — properties that don't change under certain transformations. The slice genus measures the minimum complexity of a surface whose boundary is the link; the unknotting number counts the minimum crossing changes needed to untangle a link into trivial circles. These invariants are fundamentally difficult: there's no known algorithm guaranteed to compute them efficiently. The paper treats these as optimization problems where a reinforcement learning agent learns a policy — a strategy for making decisions — to manipulate knot diagrams and minimize (or characterize) these quantities. Bayesian optimization helps explore the space of possible agent configurations efficiently.

## Core Contributions
1. **First systematic application of RL to link invariant computation**: Previous work used ML for knot classification; this paper directly optimizes to find bounds on hard-to-compute invariants.
2. **New exact values**: By combining learned upper bounds with computationally derived lower bounds, the authors determine exact slice genus and unknotting numbers for links where only bounds were previously known.
3. **Discovery of non-additivity**: The trained agents reproduced (and in some cases found new) unknotting trajectories for links where the unknotting number is non-additive — a known pathology that's rare and hard to construct.
4. **Bayesian optimization for agent design**: Used to meta-optimize the RL agent's architecture and hyperparameters rather than relying on manual tuning.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of machine learning and pure mathematics. It parallels recent successes applying deep learning to mathematical discovery (e.g., [[AlphaFold]] for protein structure, neural approaches to knot classification). The core methodology — training [[agents]] via [[reinforcement-learning]] to solve constrained optimization problems — echoes techniques from robotics and game-playing (e.g., AlphaGo). The paper differs from naive supervised learning by framing the problem as sequential decision-making: each unknotting move or crossing change is an action in a Markov decision process. The use of Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning relates to [[AutoML]] and architecture search. Topologically, the work engages with classical results in knot theory (Brittenham–Hermiller counterexamples) and relates to computability theory — these invariants live in a gray zone between tractable and proven-intractable.

## Practical Applications
1. **Knot tabulation and databases**: Mathematicians maintain large tables of knots and links with known invariants. This method could accelerate verification and fill gaps in those tables.
2. **Molecular biology**: Understanding how DNA and proteins fold involves knot-theoretic concepts. Better knot invariant computation could inform how enzymes untangle topological obstructions.
3. **Cryptography**: Knot invariants have been proposed (controversially) as a basis for cryptographic systems; faster or more complete computation of these invariants is relevant for security analysis.
4. **Computer-aided theorem proving**: Automated agents that can explore combinatorial spaces could assist human mathematicians in discovering or verifying proofs.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability to complex links**: The method likely struggles with links having many components or very high crossing numbers; computational cost may grow prohibitively.
2. **Optimality not guaranteed**: RL finds good upper bounds but has no proof that found values are optimal; verification still requires traditional mathematical argument.
3. **Generalization uncertainty**: Agents trained on small knots may not transfer well to larger or topologically distinct families; no theoretical guarantee of out-of-distribution performance.
4. **Problem-specific training**: Each variant (slice genus, unknotting number, etc.) requires retraining; no single universal model is demonstrated.
5. **Lower bounds still require external knowledge**: The approach still relies on known (traditional) invariants to establish lower bounds; on problems where even lower bounds are unknown, progress is limited.
6. **Reproducibility and instability**: RL training is notoriously sensitive to random seeds and hyperparameters; replication may be challenging.

## Open Questions
1. **Theoretical characterization**: Can we prove theoretical bounds on the quality of upper bounds achieved by this method? Is there a fundamental limit on what RL can discover?
2. **Unified model**: Can a single trained agent or model handle multiple types of invariants and link families, rather than requiring retraining per task?
3. **Proof extraction**: Can the RL trajectories be post-processed into rigorous mathematical proofs that a human (or formal verification system) can verify?
4. **Comparison to other heuristics**: How do these RL-discovered bounds compare quantitatively to classical heuristic algorithms (e.g., greedy unknotting, simulated annealing)?
5. **Scaling laws**: What is the relationship between computational budget (RL training time) and solution quality? Are there phase transitions or saturation points?
6. **Algebraic structure**: Can the learned policies exploit deeper algebraic or topological structure (e.g., symmetries, homology) to make more efficient progress?

## Key Takeaway
Reinforcement learning agents can discover unknown bounds on knot invariants that pure mathematics has struggled to compute, bridging machine learning and topology.
