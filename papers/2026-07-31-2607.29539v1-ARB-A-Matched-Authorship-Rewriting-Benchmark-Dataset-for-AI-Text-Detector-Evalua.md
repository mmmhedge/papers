# ARB: A Matched Authorship-Rewriting Benchmark Dataset for AI-Text Detector Evaluation

**Authors:** Gaetano Perrone, Simon Pietro Romano
**Published:** 2026-07-31 | **arXiv:** [2607.29539v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.29539v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces ARB, a benchmark dataset designed to test whether AI-text detectors actually work on the real problem they face: identifying when humans rewrite their own text using LLMs. Existing benchmarks compare pure human text to pure AI text, but the paper shows this doesn't predict performance when an LLM rewrites human content—detection drops 60–78 percentage points. The work exposes a critical gap between how detectors are evaluated and how they fail in practice.

## Theoretical Background
AI-text detection has traditionally relied on comparing two distributions: human-authored text versus text generated directly by large language models (LLMs — neural networks trained to predict and generate text sequentially). Prior work established that rewriting and paraphrasing degrade detector performance, but the exact mechanisms and scenarios remained unmapped. This paper builds on the observation that detector robustness varies depending on *which* text is rewritten by *which* model, suggesting detection exploits statistical signatures that differ based on the rewriting pathway. The benchmark approach follows standard machine learning practice: controlled experimental design with matched variants to isolate causal factors.

## Core Contributions
1. **ARB Dataset**: A systematically constructed benchmark with 1,800 human source texts and four matched variants per source (human-written, direct LLM, human-rewritten-by-LLM, LLM-rewritten-by-LLM), across three diverse writing domains and four open-weight generators.
2. **Asymmetric Vulnerability**: Empirical evidence that detectors are far more vulnerable to human-text-rewritten-by-LLM (H2L) than to LLM-text-rewritten-by-same-LLM (LLM2L), a finding that standard benchmarks obscure.
3. **Detector Evaluation at Strict Operating Point**: Systematic evaluation at 1% false-positive rate (TPR@1%FPR), revealing that detectors perform catastrophically on H2L while remaining comparatively robust on LLM2L, contradicting the assumption that "rewriting always breaks detectors equally."

## Connections to Other Work
This work directly challenges the validity of conventional AI-text detection benchmarks (which pit HUMAN vs. Free-LLM). It relates to broader concerns about [[generative-models|generative model misuse]], the arms race between detection and evasion, and the gap between benchmark performance and real-world robustness—a classic problem in adversarial ML. The finding that detector performance is rewriting-pathway-dependent suggests future work should explore whether detectors exploit artifacts of LLM generation that survive human editing, or whether they rely on statistical signatures humans cannot remove without conscious effort.

## Practical Applications
- **Academic integrity systems**: Universities and journals need detectors that catch rewritten homework/papers, not just direct submissions.
- **Content moderation at scale**: Social media platforms must identify subtle LLM-assisted content (human with LLM polish) that direct detection misses.
- **Detection system hardening**: Vendors can use ARB to stress-test and improve detectors before deployment.
- **Policy and regulation**: Legislators and regulators need evidence of detection failure modes to inform AI governance.
- **Forensic analysis**: Investigators tracing AI-assisted disinformation or fraud need detectors calibrated for mixed human–LLM authorship.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **AI-Text Detector-as-a-Service with Known Limitations**: A SaaS platform that quantifies detection confidence and explicitly warns users when operating in high-risk zones (e.g., human-rewritten text). Monetize via API + transparency as a differentiator.
2. **Academic Integrity Platform with Rewriting Detection**: A turnitin-style tool trained specifically on H2L scenarios, marketed to universities as a "next-generation plagiarism detector that catches LLM rewrites."
3. **Detection Robustness Benchmarking Service**: Offer vendors and researchers a standardized ARB-based evaluation suite with reports showing detector performance across rewriting pathways.
4. **LLM Watermarking + Detection Pair**: Develop detectors tuned to ARB; cross-sell with watermarking services that survive rewriting better.
5. **Content Authenticity Cert for Creators**: A browser extension or CMS plugin that cryptographically certifies "this content is 100% human-written" or "this content was human-written then LLM-rewritten," letting creators signal authenticity.

## Technical Limitations
- **Limited generator diversity**: Only four open-weight LLMs tested; proprietary models (GPT-4, Claude) may exhibit different rewriting signatures.
- **No fine-tuning on ARB**: Detectors are evaluated as-is; the paper does not measure whether retraining on H2L data would recover performance.
- **Shallow rewriting scenarios**: The benchmark uses LLM rewriting only; it does not test human-in-the-loop editing (human rewrites + LLM polish) or adversarial paraphrasing.
- **Domain bias**: Three source domains may not generalize to specialized fields (legal, medical, code).
- **Static snapshot**: LLM and detector capabilities evolve rapidly; results may not transfer to future model versions.
- **Operating point sensitivity**: Strict 1% FPR is realistic but may not reflect all deployment scenarios; results may differ at different thresholds.

## Open Questions
- Can detectors be retrained on ARB data to recover robustness on H2L without sacrificing performance on direct LLM text?
- Do detector failures on H2L stem from human editing removing LLM artifacts, or from detectors exploiting signals humans cannot destroy?
- How do multi-stage rewriting (human → LLM → human → LLM) affect detection, and is there a limit to rewriting iterations?
- Can watermarking schemes survive human rewriting better than statistical detectors, and what would a watermark-aware benchmark look like?
- How do domain-specific detectors (trained on scientific papers, tweets, code) perform on ARB compared to general-purpose detectors?
- Is there a provably robust detection approach, or is the H2L scenario fundamentally undetectable?

## Key Takeaway
LLM detectors fail on human text rewritten by LLMs, even though they catch direct generation—existing benchmarks miss this critical real-world failure mode.
