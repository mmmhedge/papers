# Phylogenetic signal in marine mammal and bird vocalizations captured by audio foundation models: the limited benefit of domain-specific pretraining

**Authors:** Víctor Rincón Yepes
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22458v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22458v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether pretrained audio foundation models automatically learn to encode evolutionary relationships in animal vocalizations, even without explicit training to do so. The authors test four audio models on a phylogenetic distance prediction task using marine mammal and bird recordings, finding that general-purpose models capture strong evolutionary signal—sometimes better than domain-specific ones—revealing that these models learn something about biological structure beyond their original training objectives.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two key ideas: (1) **audio foundation models** — large neural networks trained on massive unlabeled audio datasets to learn general-purpose sound representations (examples include AST, CLAP, and BEATs); and (2) **phylogenetic signal** — the observation that related species tend to have similar traits, including vocalizations, because they inherited them from a common ancestor. The authors use **Mantel tests**, a statistical method that measures correlation between two distance matrices (in this case, acoustic similarity and evolutionary relatedness on a phylogenetic tree). The comparison between hand-crafted acoustic features (MFCCs — Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients, a traditional audio representation) and learned embeddings is central: do neural networks discover something deeper about animal sounds than human-designed features?

## Core Contributions
1. **Quantification of phylogenetic signal in foundation model embeddings**: The paper demonstrates that general pretrained models recover r=0.82 correlation with phylogenetic distance in cetaceans—among the highest acoustic-phylogenetic correlations reported for any animal taxon.
2. **Surprising ineffectiveness of domain-specific pretraining**: The key finding is that BirdNET (trained on 6,000 bird species) and BEATs-bio (bioacoustics-specific) do *not* outperform general-purpose models like AST and CLAP on the bird phylogeny task, contradicting the intuition that matching training domain to target domain should help.
3. **Robustness analysis**: The signal persists after dimensionality reduction to 105D and after controlling for dominant frequency using partial Mantel tests, ruling out simple confounds like model size or pitch alone explaining the result.
4. **Cross-radiation validation**: Results hold independently across two evolutionary radiations (marine mammals and birds), strengthening the generality of the claim.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (probing what neural networks learn without explicit instruction) and bioacoustics. It echoes findings in NLP showing that language models learn linguistic structure beyond their training objective ([[RLHF]], probing studies of transformers). The paper implicitly challenges the "bigger and more domain-specific is better" narrative common in machine learning, aligning with recent work on transfer learning and foundation models that suggests general pretraining can be surprisingly robust.

## Practical Applications
1. **Conservation and species monitoring**: Audio foundation models could enable cost-effective biodiversity surveys by automatically identifying vocalizing species from field recordings, even in understudied taxa.
2. **Evolutionary inference**: These embeddings could serve as a tool for phylogenetics—reconstructing evolutionary trees from acoustic data where morphological or genetic data are unavailable.
3. **Bioacoustic library curation**: Museums and research databases could automatically cluster and organize animal sound recordings by evolutionary relationships without manual annotation.
4. **Cross-species vocal communication analysis**: Understanding which vocalizations are conserved across species could inform studies of convergent evolution in acoustic signaling.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **BioAudio Atlas**: A web platform where researchers upload animal sound recordings; the system automatically identifies species, clusters by phylogeny, and visualizes evolutionary relationships in sound space—no manual labeling required.
2. **Foundation Model as a Service for Bioacoustics**: An API (like Hugging Face for audio) allowing ecologists to embed any animal sound and query evolutionary or taxonomic databases, lowering barriers to using neural models.
3. **Acoustic Biodiversity Monitoring**: A field device (or smartphone app) that continuously records ambient sound, classifies species using foundation models, and sends real-time alerts for endangered or invasive species to conservation organizations.
4. **Evolutionary Hypothesis Generator**: A tool for comparative biologists that takes vocal recordings and automatically suggests which species are likely closely related or share convergent traits, then highlights them for further study.
5. **Taxonomic Validator for Museum Collections**: Software that re-examines archival animal sound recordings (often mislabeled or undocumented) by embedding them and checking consistency with known phylogenies—useful for digitizing natural history collections.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited sample sizes**: Only 32 marine mammal species and 20 bird species; results may not generalize to other vertebrate radiations or invertebrates.
2. **Phylogeny dependence**: Results assume the reference phylogenies are accurate; if the tree is wrong, the correlation is measuring model fit to incorrect biology.
3. **No mechanistic explanation**: The paper shows phylogenetic signal exists in embeddings but doesn't explain *why* or *how* the models learn it—the mechanism remains a black box.
4. **Dataset bias in pretraining**: The foundation models' pretraining datasets are unknown or proprietary; if they contain biased samples of particular animal groups, results may be confounded.
5. **Causality unclear**: The paper doesn't establish whether models learn phylogeny from acoustic similarity (the phylogenetic signal in vocalizations) or from other correlated features (e.g., habitat, body size).
6. **No comparison to traditional acoustic features beyond MFCC**: Hand-crafted features like spectral entropy or cepstral coefficients aren't fully explored; the gap might be narrower with better feature engineering.

## Open Questions
1. **Why do general models outperform domain-specific ones?** Does BirdNET overfit to species classification, losing the phylogenetic structure that AST preserves?
2. **What do the models actually encode?** Can interpretability techniques (attention visualization, activation analysis) reveal which acoustic properties carry phylogenetic information?
3. **Does this generalize beyond birds and marine mammals?** How about insects, amphibians, or deep-sea creatures with very different acoustic niches?
4. **What is learned during pretraining vs. downstream task structure?** A controlled experiment (retraining foundation models with and without phylogenetically related data) could isolate whether the signal comes from the pretraining data itself.
5. **Can this improve actual phylogenetic reconstruction?** Using embeddings as input to phylogenetic inference methods—could they recover accurate trees from sound alone?
6. **How much pretraining data is necessary?** Could a smaller, cheaper model still capture phylogenetic signal, or is scale essential?

## Key Takeaway
General-purpose audio models encode evolutionary relationships in animal vocalizations better than domain-specific ones, suggesting deep structure emerges without explicit biological training.
