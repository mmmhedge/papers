# Langevin-Informed Transfer Learning: Replacing Target Samples by Black-Box Feedback

**Authors:** Vladimir R. Kostic, Karim Lounici, Hélène Halconruy, Timothée Devergne, Michele Parrinello et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.01522v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01522v1)

## Summary
This paper presents Langevin-Informed Transfer Learning (LITL), a method for learning the underlying dynamics of complex systems (like molecular simulations or generative models) when you only have access to biased or incomplete samples and black-box feedback. The key insight is that many scientific systems evolve along low-dimensional manifolds governed by stochastic (randomized) dynamics, and LITL recovers the essential spectral structure of these dynamics without needing direct access to the target trajectories themselves.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on spectral operator learning — a mathematical framework for understanding dynamical systems through the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of infinitesimal generators (operators that describe how systems evolve over infinitesimal time steps). It extends prior work on Dirichlet representation learning (learning representations that preserve spectral properties) to handle distribution shift, where training data comes from a different distribution than the target system. The work is grounded in Langevin dynamics — the standard mathematical model for systems with both deterministic (drift) and random (diffusion) components. Key prior concepts include slow-manifold learning (identifying the low-dimensional structures where systems naturally evolve) and Sobolev norm guarantees (mathematical bounds that ensure learned functions and their derivatives generalize well).

## Core Contributions
1. **LITL framework**: A practical algorithm that recovers spectral properties of target Langevin dynamics from biased source samples using only black-box feedback (scalar rewards), without requiring direct trajectory matching.
2. **Finite-sample theory**: Rigorous statistical guarantees for estimating eigenvalues, eigenfunctions, and projected drift fields in Sobolev norms, ensuring learned representations generalize beyond training data.
3. **Spherical variant**: An adaptation specifically designed for steering normalized latent representations (common in neural networks) toward desired objectives while preserving geometric structure.
4. **Diverse empirical validation**: Demonstrations across molecular dynamics (recovering transition timescales from biased simulations), generative models (building kinetic structure from static samples), and neural network steering (post-hoc control under black-box feedback).

## Connections to Other Work
LITL relates to broader themes in distribution shift and domain adaptation by showing how spectral properties of dynamics are more stable to distributional changes than sample-level properties. It connects to [[Diffusion Models]] by providing a method to reverse-engineer the dynamics that generative models implicitly learn. The work also touches on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] by revealing the underlying spectral structure of learned systems. It generalizes beyond prior spectral operator learning approaches by handling the practically important case where ground-truth target dynamics are inaccessible.

## Practical Applications
1. **Computational chemistry**: Recovering accurate molecular transition rates and timescales from biased molecular dynamics simulations (e.g., enhanced sampling methods that distort natural dynamics).
2. **Generative model analysis**: Understanding the implicit dynamics learned by diffusion models or score-based generative models by reconstructing their kinetic structure from static samples.
3. **Black-box neural network steering**: Post-hoc control of trained neural networks (e.g., steering image generation toward human preferences) using only scalar feedback without retraining.
4. **Climate and complex systems modeling**: Inferring slow manifolds and transition timescales in high-dimensional climate or biological systems where observations are biased by measurement constraints.
5. **Inverse problems in physics**: Reconstructing symmetries and geometric structure of physical systems from incomplete or noisy observational data.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ML interpretability-as-a-service platform**: Offer businesses a tool to audit and understand the implicit dynamics of their proprietary generative models (e.g., recommendation systems, image generators) without white-box access.
2. **Molecular simulation acceleration software**: Sell a package that automatically corrects and enriches biased molecular dynamics data, enabling faster discovery of materials and drugs with higher-accuracy transition timescales.
3. **Black-box generative model control**: Build a SaaS product for fine-tuning generative models using only user feedback signals, enabling companies to steer models toward brand guidelines or safety objectives without expensive retraining.

5. **Latent space navigation tools**: Create developer tools (SDKs, APIs) that allow researchers to steer and control the latent representations of neural networks with minimal data and mathematical expertise.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Manifold structure assumption**: LITL assumes systems evolve on low-dimensional manifolds; failure to identify the true manifold dimension degrades performance.
2. **Spectral gap dependency**: Recovery quality depends on having sufficient spectral separation (eigenvalue gaps); systems with dense spectra may require prohibitive sample sizes.
3. **Black-box feedback limitations**: Scalar feedback provides minimal information; high-variance or delayed feedback could slow convergence significantly.
4. **Computational cost**: Spectral operator learning can be expensive for very high-dimensional latent spaces, and scaling to modern large-scale generative models is unclear.
5. **Bias correction requirements**: The method assumes the source-target bias can be partially corrected through the Dirichlet formulation; unknown or heavy biases may violate underlying assumptions.
6. **Finite-sample bounds looseness**: Stated Sobolev norm guarantees may be loose in practice; actual sample complexity for real applications is empirically determined.

## Open Questions
1. **Scaling to high dimensions**: How does LITL perform on modern large-scale generative models (e.g., billion-parameter diffusion models)? What algorithmic improvements are needed?
2. **Adaptive feedback**: Can LITL be extended to actively select which black-box queries to make, reducing total feedback cost?
3. **Non-stationary dynamics**: How would the framework handle systems whose underlying dynamics change over time (e.g., concept drift in neural networks)?
4. **Partial observability**: What happens when only a subset of the latent space is observable? Can reconstruction still be guaranteed?
5. **Comparison to alternative approaches**: How does LITL compare empirically to other distribution-shift methods (e.g., adversarial domain adaptation, importance weighting) on the same benchmarks?
6. **Interpretability of recovered dynamics**: Can the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions be post-hoc translated into human-interpretable mechanistic descriptions of the system?

## Key Takeaway
Learn system dynamics from biased samples and scalar feedback by recovering the spectral structure of underlying stochastic processes.
