# Validity Without Ground Truth: What Stated-Preference Economics Offers the Evaluation of Language Models

**Authors:** Daniel Robert Kling Alexander, Catherine Louise Kling
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10506v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10506v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes using validity frameworks from economics—a field with decades of experience evaluating surveys without ground truth—to assess language model outputs on subjective questions like policy worth or value trade-offs. The authors demonstrate this approach by running an economic valuation survey through six LLMs and show that economic theory predictions (e.g., demand curves slope downward) can separate model quality, even when no "correct answer" exists.

## Theoretical Background
Stated-preference economics studies how people value goods or policies by asking what they would pay for them, rather than observing real purchases. This creates a core problem: you can't verify answers against reality. Over decades, economists developed validity frameworks to judge survey quality anyway. These include **content validity** (does the survey measure what it claims?), **construct validity** (do results align with theory?), **criterion validity** (do answers predict real behavior?), **reliability** (consistency across similar questions), **incentive compatibility** (do respondents have reason to answer truthfully?), and **consequentiality** (do answers matter?). The paper argues these concepts transfer directly to LLM evaluation—we can test whether model outputs obey economic laws (like negative demand slopes) without needing ground-truth labels.

## Core Contributions
1. **Framework transfer**: Maps economic validity concepts onto LLM evaluation as a general methodology for subjective domains.
2. **Empirical demonstration**: Applies a real water-quality valuation survey to six models (including older and newer variants), showing that economic theory predictions sharply differentiate model performance.
3. **Coherence, not correctness**: Distinguishes between models that produce internally consistent, theory-aligned outputs versus merely plausible ones.
4. **Specific validity tests**: Operationalizes each validity type for LLMs (e.g., checking whether willingness-to-pay responds sensibly to income or scope changes).

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of LLM evaluation methodology and applied economics. It relates to broader efforts to evaluate models on open-ended tasks where traditional benchmarks (like [[MMLU]] or exact-match metrics) fail. It complements [[RLHF]] and [[alignment]] research by offering systematic tests of whether model behavior aligns with rational economic theory—a proxy for "coherent reasoning" without requiring labeled ground truth. The approach echoes quality-assurance methods in behavioral economics and survey design, offering a bridge between social-science methodology and AI evaluation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Policy advisory systems**: Deploying LLMs to estimate public willingness-to-pay for environmental or infrastructure projects; validity tests ensure outputs respect demand theory.
2. **Healthcare valuation**: Assessing quality-of-life trade-offs in medical cost-effectiveness analysis; coherence with economic priors increases stakeholder confidence.
3. **Corporate pricing strategy**: Testing LLMs on customer preference elicitation and value estimation; economic validity tests flag models that produce nonsensical price elasticities.
4. **Regulatory impact assessment**: Evaluating LM-generated estimates of consumer surplus or policy costs; validity framework provides auditable, theory-grounded justification.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Valuation Auditor**: SaaS tool that runs stated-preference surveys through candidate LLMs and scores them on economic validity; sells to consulting firms, government agencies, or corporations vetting models for policy work.
2. **Theory-Aligned Model Certification**: Startup that certifies LMs have passed domain-specific validity tests (economics, psychology, medicine); brands models as "coherence-verified" for regulated industries.
3. **Adaptive Survey Engine**: Platform that administers economic valuation surveys to LLMs dynamically, using validity failures to refine prompts or select better models in real time.
4. **Economic Reasonableness Checker**: API that validates LLM outputs on subjective economic questions against theoretical priors (e.g., income elasticity, scope sensitivity) before deployment.
5. **Benchmark Suite for Preference Learning**: Publish standardized, theory-grounded test suites (water quality, carbon pricing, etc.) that become industry standard for LLM evaluation in policy and business domains.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Assumes theory is universal**: Economic theory (downward-sloping demand) may not apply equally across cultures, contexts, or LM training data distributions.
2. **Coherence ≠ accuracy**: Models can pass all validity tests while still producing values far from true human preferences—the framework measures internal consistency, not ground-truth alignment.
3. **Limited to quantifiable domains**: Works well for willingness-to-pay or scalar trade-offs, but harder to operationalize for open-ended narrative or creative tasks.
4. **Survey-dependent**: Results depend heavily on survey design (incentive structure, question framing, scope definition); poor survey design can fail to separate models meaningfully.
5. **Sparse ground truth**: In some applications, you *do* want to compare to real human behavior; validity alone won't detect systematic model bias relative to actual preferences.
6. **Generalization unclear**: Water-quality valuation may not predict performance on other economic or subjective tasks; transferability of validity tests remains untested.

## Open Questions
1. Do validity tests on one economic domain (e.g., environmental goods) predict coherence on others (e.g., labor-market trade-offs)?
2. Can this framework extend to non-quantitative subjective judgments (e.g., narrative coherence, ethical reasoning) without reducing to scalars?
3. How do prompt engineering, few-shot examples, or in-context learning affect validity scores—can they artificially inflate coherence?
4. Do models that pass all economic validity tests actually converge on human preference estimates, or can they diverge while remaining internally consistent?
5. Can validity frameworks detect adversarial robustness—do models maintain coherence under distribution shift or adversarial inputs?
6. How do you weight different validity dimensions (content vs. criterion vs. convergent) when they conflict?

## Key Takeaway
Economic validity frameworks—developed to evaluate surveys with no ground truth—can rigorously assess whether LLM outputs on subjective questions obey rational-choice theory, separating coherent from incoherent models.
