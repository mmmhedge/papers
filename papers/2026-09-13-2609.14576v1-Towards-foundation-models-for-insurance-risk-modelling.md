# Towards foundation models for insurance risk modelling

**Authors:** Christopher Blier-Wong
**Published:** 2026-09-13 | **arXiv:** [2609.14576v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14576v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes using foundation models — large pre-trained neural networks — to extract predictive signals from unstructured insurance data like claim narratives, images, and sensor readings. Instead of manually engineering features from these rich data sources, foundation models can learn patterns automatically and then be adapted to specific actuarial tasks, potentially helping insurers make better risk assessments and reserve estimates faster and with less domain expertise.

## Theoretical Background
Foundation models are large neural networks trained on diverse, unlabeled datasets (e.g., GPT models trained on text, vision transformers trained on images) that learn general-purpose representations. These models can then be fine-tuned on downstream tasks with modest labeled data. The paper builds on decades of actuarial science — the mathematical framework for assessing insurance risk, pricing policies, and setting loss reserves (funds set aside for expected claims). Traditional actuarial models work with tabular data (age, claims history, location) using statistical methods like generalized linear models. The core insight here is that modern deep learning — neural networks with many layers — can extract actionable variables from unstructured data (text, images, weather simulations) that traditional actuarial methods cannot easily use.

## Core Contributions
- **Systematic review of foundation model types** relevant to insurance: language models for claim narratives, vision models for images, geospatial and time-series models for location/temporal risk, tabular models for structured data, and scientific models for climate/weather simulation.
- **Concrete insurance use cases** showing how each model type could reduce prediction error or enable earlier intervention (e.g., identifying worsening injuries in claim notes before costs spike).
- **A validation framework** for integrating foundation model outputs into actuarial calculations, including measures of predictive contribution, stability over time, and compliance with regulatory rules on information use.
- **Identification of key challenges** unique to insurance: long delay before claim closure (making ground truth collection slow), extreme value problems (large losses are rare, so models trained elsewhere may not generalize), and privacy/fairness risks from finer-grained risk classification.

## Practical Applications
- **Claim triage and reserve setting**: LLMs flag deteriorating injury narratives early, allowing reserving models to adjust loss expectations before actual payments reveal the truth, reducing reserve surprises and improving cash flow forecasting.
- **Underwriting and pricing**: Vision models assess property damage from photos; scientific models link local climate/flood risk to expected losses, enabling more precise premium quotes.
- **Fraud detection**: Language and geospatial models identify inconsistencies or anomalies in claim narratives and locations that traditional models miss.
- **Catastrophe modeling**: Scientific models (physics-based simulations of hurricanes, floods, earthquakes) predict asset damage and insurance losses more accurately than historical loss curves alone, especially as climate patterns shift.
- **Customer retention**: Early injury detection allows insurers to intervene with rehabilitation support, reducing lifetime claim costs and improving policyholder satisfaction.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **InsureAI Claim Analyzer**: A SaaS platform that ingests claim photos, notes, and sensor data, applies fine-tuned foundation models, and outputs structured risk signals (injury severity, fraud risk, reserve recommendation) for insurers to integrate into their workflows.
2. **Climate-Risk Analytics for Commercial Insurers**: Combine scientific climate models with asset-level geospatial data to produce dynamic, location-specific loss projections; sell to P&C insurers and reinsurers as a pricing and capital allocation tool.
3. **Regulatory Compliance Engine for Insurance AI**: A tool that audits foundation model outputs for fairness, explainability, and compliance with insurance regulations; helps insurers deploy models confidently without legal risk.
4. **Federated Foundation Model for Insurance**: A consortium service where multiple insurers pool anonymized data to train shared foundation models (language, vision, tabular) while maintaining data privacy; rent access to fine-tuned versions instead of each insurer training in isolation.
5. **Real-Time Injury Monitoring**: Wearable/sensor data + LLMs detect early signs of claim deterioration (mobility loss, medication changes) and trigger automated interventions (PT referral, cost alerts) before claims escalate.

## Technical Limitations
- **Long feedback loops**: Insurance claim costs are often unknown for years (medical claims, litigation). This makes it hard to validate models and retrain them with recent data, especially for rare, high-severity events.
- **Domain shift and generalization**: A model trained on one insurer's portfolio or region may not predict well on another's. Weather patterns, demographics, and claim culture vary; the paper notes that "patterns learned elsewhere fail to transfer."
- **Data privacy and fairness**: Richer data (detailed injury narratives, images, sensor data) enables finer risk segmentation, which can inadvertently discriminate against protected groups or reveal sensitive health information, creating legal and ethical barriers to model deployment.
- **Computational cost and latency**: Inference on large foundation models is expensive and slow; processing claims in real-time or at scale may be infeasible or uneconomical.
- **Regulatory uncertainty**: Insurance is heavily regulated. Rules on information use, fairness, and explainability vary by jurisdiction and are still evolving for AI/ML; models that pass compliance today may not tomorrow.
- **Model concentration risk**: If many insurers rely on the same foundation model provider or shared predictions, system failures or model drift create systemic risk across the industry.

## Open Questions
- **How do you validate a model when ground truth arrives years later?** What is the best way to backtest foundation models on long-tail claims and adjust for regime shifts (e.g., COVID-era claim patterns)?
- **Can you measure fairness and bias in insurance foundation models without revealing sensitive demographic data?** How do you ensure a model trained on historical data (which reflects past discrimination) doesn't perpetuate it?
- **What is the optimal architecture for pooling data across insurers** to train better foundation models while protecting proprietary information and satisfying antitrust law?
- **How transferable are foundation models across insurance lines** (auto, home, commercial, health) and geographies? What adaptation strategies work best?
- **Can you design an insurance foundation model that is interpretable enough to satisfy regulators and courts** while retaining the predictive power of a black-box deep network?

## Key Takeaway
Foundation models could help insurers turn unstructured data—notes, photos, sensors—into better risk predictions, but long delays, data privacy rules, and regulatory uncertainty remain major obstacles.
