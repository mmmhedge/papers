# Decentralised Consensus Learning Networks: SME Rotation Without Centralised Reward

**Authors:** Florin Neagu
**Published:** 2026-07-27 | **arXiv:** [2607.24416v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24416v1)

## Summary
This paper presents a decentralised learning framework where multiple agents reach consensus on beliefs through peer validation rather than external reward signals. Instead of a central authority defining correctness, agents dynamically earn "subject-matter expert" status by demonstrating consistency with the collective view — a mechanism that proves robust across network sizes from 30 to 10,000 agents but becomes increasingly concentrated in high-dimensional belief spaces.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on multi-agent consensus theory and distributed learning, where agents collectively converge to shared beliefs without centralised control. It contrasts with reinforcement learning approaches that rely on a single reward function (e.g., [[RLHF]], centralised supervision). The framework uses weighted social consensus — agents update their beliefs based on a weighted average of peers' views, with weights reflecting inferred competence rather than external ground truth. Competence is inferred from peer consistency: agents that reliably align with what others believe are trusted more. This echoes ideas from social learning and epistemic networks, though applied here to belief vectors rather than discrete labels.

## Core Contributions
1. **Decentralised expertise emergence**: SME status is dynamically assigned as top-percentile competence rank, with 90–100% of agents achieving it at some point — far broader than traditional fixed-expert models.
2. **Topology and scale invariance**: The framework's core dynamics (SME rotation, convergence) hold across random, grid, and sparse networks at sizes ranging from 30 to 10,000 agents.
3. **Dimensionality-driven phase transitions**: As belief dimensionality increases (D=1–500), the network exhibits five distinct dynamical regimes, culminating in partial consensus and expertise concentration at D=150–200.
4. **Mechanistic insight**: High-dimensional convergence naturally concentrates expertise in the agent most consistently aligned with collective belief — an emergent property rather than imposed rule.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[multi-agent consensus algorithms]], [[decentralised learning]], and [[epistemic trust networks]]. It differs from centralised reward-based learning (standard in deep RL) and from fixed hierarchies in organisational theory. The dimensionality phase transitions echo complexity transitions in [[emergent behavior in multi-agent systems]]. The peer-validation mechanism shares spirit with blockchain consensus (Proof of Stake, Byzantine fault tolerance) but applied to continuous belief alignment. It also connects to social choice theory and collective decision-making, where no single ground truth exists.

## Practical Applications
1. **Organisational knowledge management**: Teams of domain experts (R&D, consulting) self-organise without central authority dictating whose judgment to trust; expertise rankings emerge from consistency.
2. **Federated learning with no ground truth**: Medical or clinical data systems where ground truth is ambiguous (diagnostic uncertainty) — consensus-driven rather than label-driven.
3. **Decentralised sensor networks**: IoT or environmental monitoring where agents (sensors/nodes) must agree on state (e.g., air quality, structural integrity) without a central arbiter.
4. **Open-source community curation**: Code review or library maintenance where trust and decision-making emerge from peer consistency rather than maintainer decree.
5. **Distributed scientific collaboration**: Research teams in early-stage domains (emerging pathogens, novel materials) where consensus on "ground truth" evolves over time.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Decentralised DAO governance platform**: Smart contracts where voting power and expertise rank emerge dynamically from member consistency and peer alignment, replacing static token-weighted voting.
2. **Federated diagnostic AI**: Hospital networks collaboratively refine diagnostic models without sharing raw patient data; trust in model updates emerges from agreement rates rather than central validation.
3. **Trustless supply-chain consensus**: Suppliers and manufacturers reach agreement on product quality, provenance, or compliance through decentralised peer validation; expertise in quality assessment rotates dynamically.
4. **Collaborative research validation service**: Researchers submit claims; peer scientists earn trust and reviewer status by consistency with emerging consensus; a reputation market without editorial gatekeeping.
5. **Decentralised content moderation network**: Social platforms where moderation decisions and moderator trust emerge from user-agent consensus rather than corporate policy; expertise rank fluctuates with collective alignment.

## Technical Limitations
1. **High-dimensionality bottleneck**: At D=150–200+, expertise concentration into single agents suggests the method may not scale well to very high-dimensional belief spaces (e.g., LLM-scale latent representations); centralisation emerges by design.
2. **Lack of ground truth benchmark**: Evaluation is purely on emergent consensus properties (convergence, rotation, rank stability), not on whether the consensus is *correct* — unclear how well this performs when an objective ground truth exists.
3. **Convergence time not characterised**: The paper shows convergence happens but does not analyse time complexity or compare speed to centralised methods.
4. **Limited heterogeneity**: All agents use the same update rule; no analysis of how robust the system is to adversarial, malicious, or fundamentally uninformed agents.
5. **Stochastic noise role underexplored**: ETA sensitivity shows belief dimensionality drives concentration, but the interplay between noise and dimensionality in non-equilibrium regimes is not fully characterised.
6. **Graph topology complexity**: Real networks have temporal edges, heterogeneous degree distributions, and clustering; analysis on synthetic topologies may not capture real-world robustness.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance degrade when a fraction of agents are adversarially biased or uninformed? What is the robustness threshold?
2. Can the high-dimensional concentration problem be mitigated by, e.g., temporal expertise rotation or hierarchical belief decomposition?
3. How does this method perform when ground truth exists and is sparse/noisy? Can we quantify the bias-variance tradeoff vs. centralised reward learning?
4. What happens in non-stationary environments where ground truth or optimal beliefs change over time? Does SME rotation help or hurt adaptation?
5. Can agent heterogeneity (different learning rates, belief priors, or access to side information) be incorporated without breaking the emergent consensus?
6. How does computational cost (message passing, trust inference) scale with agent count and dimensionality compared to centralised alternatives?

## Key Takeaway
Decentralised multi-agent learning achieves robust expert rotation through peer consensus, but expertise naturally concentrates in high dimensions—a design tradeoff rather than a bug.
