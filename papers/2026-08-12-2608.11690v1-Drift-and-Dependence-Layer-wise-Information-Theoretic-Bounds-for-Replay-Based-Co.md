# Drift and Dependence: Layer-wise Information-Theoretic Bounds for Replay-Based Continual Learning

**Authors:** Tieliang Gong, Zhongbo Zhang, Wen Wen, Yong-Jin Liu
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.11690v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11690v1)

## Summary
This paper analyzes why replay buffers—storing and retraining on old examples—help neural networks learn new tasks without forgetting old ones. The authors develop a mathematical framework that separates two coupled problems: the approximation error from using limited past data, and how repeated training on mixed old/new data affects learning. They show which internal network layers should be stabilized to minimize forgetting and provide an online metric to detect when a model is starting to forget.

## Theoretical Background
Continual learning addresses the challenge of sequential task learning: a model must adapt to new data without "catastrophic forgetting"—sudden loss of performance on previously learned tasks. Replay is a simple strategy: maintain a small buffer of old examples and mix them into training batches. Prior work treated this as a single generalization problem, but this paper separates it into two distinct mechanisms. The framework uses information theory—specifically mutual information and divergence measures—to quantify how much information flows through each layer of a deep network. It builds on classical learning theory (Rademacher complexity, uniform convergence bounds) and modern stability analysis (how perturbations in training affect final models). The SGLD (Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics) instantiation connects gradient-based optimization to probabilistic inference, allowing the authors to express learning dynamics in terms of trajectory curvature.

## Core Contributions
1. **Layer-wise decomposition of generalization error**: Separates replay-buffer drift (representation mismatch from finite examples) from optimization-dependence effects (how coupling through training trajectory affects learning), resolved layer-by-layer rather than at hypothesis level.

2. **Drift-sensitivity trade-off with Wasserstein relaxation**: Shows that drift increases with depth, but introducing a Wasserstein distance relaxation (valid when old/new data distributions don't fully overlap) identifies an optimal interior layer to stabilize, creating a practical "interior funnel" effect.

3. **Curvature-aware gradient-alignment diagnostic**: Reduces the optimization-dependence term to a trajectory-level log-determinant budget under SGLD, yielding a computable online statistic (gradient alignment) that predicts task-wise forgetting without ground-truth old-task labels.

4. **Empirical validation of scaling laws**: Controlled experiments confirm predicted memory-sample complexity, the interior-layer stabilization principle, and the alignment signal's link to actual forgetting curves on benchmarks.

## Practical Applications
1. **Adaptive replay-buffer tuning**: Use the gradient-alignment diagnostic online to adjust buffer composition (which old tasks to prioritize) without retraining from scratch.

2. **Early-warning systems for continual-learning systems**: Monitor the curvature statistic during training to flag when a model is about to catastrophically forget, triggering buffer expansion or layer stabilization before performance degrades.

3. **Layer-wise regularization design**: Automatically identify which intermediate layers should be frozen or regularized when learning a new task, reducing hyperparameter search.

4. **Efficient robotics and autonomous systems**: Sequential task learning in embodied agents (e.g., learn grasping, then navigation, then manipulation) where memory and computation are limited—use the theory to minimize buffer size while guaranteeing forgetting bounds.

## Technical Limitations
- **Wasserstein relaxation validity**: The drift bound's support-mismatch assumption may not hold when old and new tasks are highly dissimilar; the framework's guarantees degrade in regime mismatch.
- **SGLD instantiation**: Converting to a practical algorithm requires tuning the temperature/noise level of SGLD; the log-determinant trajectory budget is asymptotic and may not tightly capture finite-batch dynamics.
- **Computational cost of monitoring**: Computing the gradient-alignment statistic and log-determinants requires second-order information (Hessian-related quantities), which can be expensive for very large networks.
- **Benchmark-specific validation**: Experiments are on relatively controlled continual-learning benchmarks (class-incremental, domain-incremental); real-world task sequences with imbalanced, non-stationary, or adversarially ordered tasks may violate theoretical assumptions.
- **No advice on buffer selection**: The theory bounds the cost of replay but does not prescribe *which* examples to replay—coreset selection or active memory management is outside scope.

## Open Questions
1. **Adaptive layer-wise stabilization**: Can the framework guide automatic layer freezing/regularization scheduling without prior knowledge of task similarity? How does the optimal stabilization layer change as more tasks arrive?

2. **Cross-task transfer vs. forgetting trade-off**: The framework separates drift and dependence, but how does improving one affect the other? Is there a fundamental trade-off between positive transfer and forgetting resilience?

3. **Non-replay alternatives**: How do the bounds compare to rehearsal-free methods (e.g., dynamic architectures, parameter isolation)? Can the framework explain why replay sometimes underperforms on certain task sequences?

4. **Scalability to very deep networks**: The layer-wise analysis assumes manageable depth; how do the bounds tighten or degrade for modern very-deep architectures (ResNets, Vision Transformers)? Is there a saturation point?

5. **Task-agnostic forgetting detection**: Can the alignment statistic be made task-agnostic, requiring no access to old-task validation data, for truly online continual learning?

## Key Takeaway
Replay-based continual learning's generalization splits into representation drift and optimization coupling; stabilizing intermediate layers minimizes forgetting detectable via gradient alignment.
