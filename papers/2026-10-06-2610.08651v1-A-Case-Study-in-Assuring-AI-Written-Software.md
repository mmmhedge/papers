# A Case Study in Assuring AI-Written Software

**Authors:** Lindsey Ferris, Sierra Bonilla
**Published:** 2026-10-06 | **arXiv:** [2610.08651v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08651v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how to maintain human control over software built by AI coding agents, using a real healthcare platform as a case study. The authors show that traditional code review fails when agents produce more code than humans can inspect, and that even sophisticated monitoring systems (tests, automated reviewers, repair agents) have hidden failure modes — revealing that effective oversight requires alignment of objectives, evidence, permissions, and human decision-making across the entire system.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of software engineering, AI safety, and multi-agent systems. It builds on decades of software quality assurance (code review, testing, monitoring) but applies these practices to a new regime: systems where autonomous agents generate code faster than humans can review it. The paper implicitly draws on [[alignment]] literature — the challenge of ensuring AI systems pursue intended goals — and extends it from single-agent settings to multi-agent workflows where agents supervise other agents. The healthcare domain adds real stakes: failures directly affect patient safety and regulatory compliance, making this a naturalistic test of alignment in high-consequence environments.

## Core Contributions
1. **Documented failure modes of multi-agent oversight**: Shows concrete examples where monitors measure proxies instead of outcomes, audits fail silently, and automated repairs cause operational disruption — problems not fully anticipated by practitioners.
2. **Empirical characterization of "objective alignment"**: Demonstrates that human control requires simultaneous alignment of four dimensions: the intended outcome, the evidence used to judge success, the agents' action permissions, and the final human decision.
3. **Production case study with lessons learned**: Provides a detailed walkthrough of how a non-expert operator actually learned to govern a live healthcare system with multiple coding agents, revealing practical gaps between theory and practice.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper advances [[alignment]] research by grounding it in a messy, real-world multi-agent setting rather than controlled benchmarks. It relates to work on [[multi-agent-systems]] coordination and emergent behavior, but focuses on safety rather than task performance. The emphasis on monitoring and auditing connects to [[risk-management]] in financial systems (where similar cascading failures occur) and to broader literature on AI safety through oversight (e.g., iterated amplification, debate). The silent-audit-failure pattern echoes known issues in software observability and chaos engineering — validating that AI systems amplify existing engineering blind spots.

## Practical Applications
1. **Healthcare workflow automation**: Building trustworthy clinical decision-support systems where agents handle routine documentation, coding, and routine checks while humans remain in control of critical decisions.
2. **Regulated AI-assisted software development**: Compliance frameworks for regulated industries (healthcare, finance, aviation) that must certify AI-built systems to auditors.
3. **Meta-agent supervision platforms**: Tools that let operators design and configure which agents can supervise which other agents, with configurable audit trails and permission models.
4. **Outcome-tracking dashboards for agent systems**: Monitoring systems that separate what you measure from how you interpret it, preventing silent drift between metrics and reality.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"Agent Governance Engine"** — SaaS for enterprises using coding agents: configure objective alignment (outcome → evidence → permissions → decision), log all agent-to-agent interactions, auto-detect measurement drift. Target: regulated industries (healthcare, fintech, insurance).
2. **"Silent Audit Detector"** — Continuous validation that monitoring and audit systems themselves are working (meta-monitoring). Integrates with existing observability platforms. For any company running multi-agent workflows.
3. **"Outcome Fidelity Framework"** — Consulting + tooling to help teams distinguish proxy metrics from actual outcomes, then wire them through agent permissions. High-touch initial deployment, productize over time.
4. **"Multi-Agent Replay & Counterfactual Analysis"** — Forensic tooling for teams to rewind agent decisions, inspect what different permission models would have done, and validate that repairs actually fix root causes (not just symptoms).
5. **"Agent Escrow for Healthcare"** — Compliance-ready deployment platform for healthcare AI agents, with built-in evidence capture, separation of roles (coder agents vs. auditor agents vs. operators), and audit-ready logs for regulators.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability of objective alignment**: As systems grow to dozens of agents, keeping outcome, evidence, permissions, and decisions aligned becomes a graph problem — the paper doesn't address how to scale this beyond small teams.
2. **Measurement fidelity remains hard**: The paper identifies proxy-vs.-outcome failures but doesn't provide a systematic way to detect them before they cause harm. Domain expertise and iteration seem necessary.
3. **Repair mechanisms lack guarantees**: One automated repair caused operational disruption; the paper doesn't propose formal methods or verification for repair correctness.
4. **Non-expert operators as a constraint**: The case study succeeds partly because the operator was willing to learn and iterate. Generalizability to operators without that capability or time is unclear.
5. **No quantified safety or trust metrics**: The paper is descriptive rather than prescriptive; it doesn't offer metrics to measure how well objective alignment is working or when a system is "safe enough."

## Open Questions
1. **Can you automate objective-alignment verification?** Design an algorithm that, given a system of agents and their mutual oversight rules, detects misalignments before deployment.
2. **What is the minimum viable supervision architecture?** How many human checkpoints, how many auditor agents, how much logging is actually necessary for safety? (Probably domain-specific, but are there principles?)
3. **How do you catch silent failures?** Develop methods to detect when monitoring systems themselves have failed — perhaps via meta-monitoring or adversarial testing of auditor agents.
4. **Can repair agents be verified?** Propose formal methods or testing strategies to ensure that automated repairs don't introduce new failure modes.
5. **Generalization across domains**: Would the objective-alignment framework work in finance, manufacturing, or other regulated domains, or is it specific to healthcare's outcome structure?

## Key Takeaway
Multi-agent software systems need aligned outcomes, evidence, permissions, and human decisions — not just code review and monitoring.
