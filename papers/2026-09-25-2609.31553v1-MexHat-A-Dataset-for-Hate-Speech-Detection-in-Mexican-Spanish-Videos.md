# MexHat: A Dataset for Hate Speech Detection in Mexican Spanish Videos

**Authors:** Itzel Tlelo-Coyotecatl, Hugo Jair Escalante
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31553v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31553v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces MexHat, a dataset of ~1,000 annotated video clips for detecting hate speech in Mexican Spanish content. The dataset addresses a critical gap: while hate speech detection is important for online safety, most resources focus on English, missing the linguistic nuances and cultural context needed to detect harmful content in Spanish-speaking communities. The work provides both coarse-grained labels (no negative content, offensive, hate speech) and fine-grained hate speech subcategories.

## Theoretical Background
Hate speech detection is a content moderation task that requires understanding both explicit language and implicit, contextual meaning. Early approaches relied on keyword matching, but modern systems use deep learning models that capture semantic meaning. The field recognizes that hate speech is culturally and linguistically situated — what constitutes hate speech in one language or community may differ significantly in another. Multimodal hate speech detection combines text and visual information from videos, as tone, imagery, and spoken context all contribute to determining if content is hateful. Most existing datasets (e.g., for English) are text-only or image-only, limiting models' ability to handle video content from non-English-speaking regions.

## Core Contributions
- **First dataset for hate speech detection in Mexican Spanish video content**, filling a specific regional and linguistic gap
- **Multimodal annotation scheme** combining linguistic and visual cues within the same video dataset
- **Dual annotation structure**: three-way classification (benign/offensive/hate speech) alongside fine-grained hate speech subcategories, allowing models to learn both coarse and nuanced distinctions
- **Baseline results** that highlight the difficulty of the task and establish performance benchmarks for future work
- **Culturally grounded resource** that captures Mexican Spanish idioms, slang, and social context relevant to hate speech in that community

## Connections to Other Work
This dataset complements existing multilingual NLP efforts (like [[mBERT]] or multilingual BERT models) by providing a language-specific, multimodal resource. It aligns with growing recognition that content moderation requires local context — similar to work on offensive language detection in other non-English languages. The multimodal angle connects to broader [[computer-vision]] and video understanding research, where models must fuse text, audio, and visual information. The fine-grained annotation approach mirrors recent trends in more nuanced toxicity classification beyond binary toxic/non-toxic splits.

## Practical Applications
- **Platform moderation at scale**: Meta, TikTok, YouTube, and other platforms can use this to improve content filtering for Mexican Spanish users
- **Automated community safety systems**: Deploy models trained on MexHat to flag hateful content in real time for human review
- **Researcher tools**: Enable researchers studying hate speech dynamics, algorithmic bias, and online harassment in Spanish-speaking communities
- **Policy and advocacy work**: Organizations combating online hate can use model predictions to identify emerging hate campaigns and prioritize intervention

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Regional content moderation SaaS** — A moderation platform specifically tuned for Latin American Spanish speakers, offering real-time video filtering with cultural sensitivity
2. **Hate speech trend monitoring dashboard** — Analyze patterns of hate speech in Mexican Spanish videos across platforms to alert NGOs and researchers
3. **Localized safety labeling tool** — A crowdsourced annotation platform for Spanish speakers to label harmful content and improve models for their communities
4. **Multilingual content moderation benchmark** — Build a suite of regional datasets (Mexican Spanish as first; expand to other Spanish dialects, Portuguese, etc.) and sell unified moderation APIs
5. **AI training data service** — Offer MexHat and similar curated datasets to smaller platforms and researchers who lack resources to build regional datasets themselves

## Technical Limitations
- **Limited scale**: 1,000 video clips is modest; larger datasets may be needed for robust deep learning models
- **Annotation subjectivity**: Hate speech judgments are inherently subjective; inter-annotator agreement rates are not detailed
- **Multimodal complexity**: Combining text, audio, and visual features adds engineering complexity; the paper doesn't clarify how much each modality contributes
- **Class imbalance**: Hate speech is typically rare; dataset may be skewed toward benign content, making minority class learning difficult
- **Generalization risk**: Models trained on Mexican Spanish may not transfer to other Spanish dialects (Argentine, Colombian, etc.)
- **Context dependency**: Video-level labels may miss nuance within long videos or require frame-level annotation

## Open Questions
- How do different modalities (text, audio, visuals) contribute to model predictions? Which is most predictive of hate speech?
- How well do models trained on MexHat generalize to other Spanish-speaking regions or dialects?
- Can transfer learning from English hate speech datasets improve Mexican Spanish performance, or does cultural specificity dominate?
- What inter-annotator agreement was achieved, and which subcategories are hardest to label consistently?
- Do models suffer from false positives on reclaimed slurs or in-group speech (e.g., community members using language in non-hateful contexts)?

## Key Takeaway
New video dataset for hate speech detection in Mexican Spanish fills a critical gap in non-English content moderation resources.
