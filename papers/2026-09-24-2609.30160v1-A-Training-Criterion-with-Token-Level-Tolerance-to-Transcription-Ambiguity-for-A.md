# A Training Criterion with Token-Level Tolerance to Transcription Ambiguity for Automatic Speech Recognition

**Authors:** Saurabh Kumar, Diptiman Mohanta, Prasanta Kumar Ghosh
**Published:** 2026-09-24 | **arXiv:** [2609.30160v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30160v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a real problem in speech recognition: reference transcripts often contain ambiguous regions where multiple valid pronunciations or spellings match the same audio, yet standard training assumes only one correct label. The authors propose token-level tolerance in the alignment graph used during training, allowing the model to bypass uncertain tokens while keeping the rest of the word supervised — this achieves 9.45% relative improvement in word error rate across 19 languages.

## Theoretical Background
Speech recognition systems are typically trained using [[Connectionist Temporal Classification]] (CTC), a sequence-to-sequence alignment framework that maps variable-length acoustic frames to discrete token sequences without requiring forced alignments. CTC constructs an alignment graph where paths through the graph represent valid token sequences; the model learns to assign high probability to paths consistent with the reference transcript. Prior work (Omni-temporal Classification, or OTC) introduced wildcards — skip arcs in this graph — to tolerate transcript noise at the word level. The key insight here is that word-level tolerance is too coarse: skipping one token within a word throws away supervision for the entire word, even though other tokens in that word may be unambiguous.

## Core Contributions
1. **Token-level wildcard arcs**: Moving bypass arcs from word to token granularity, allowing the model to skip individual uncertain tokens while retaining supervision for neighboring tokens in the same word.
2. **Hybrid graph design**: Combining token- and word-level arcs as complementary escape paths to handle both very localized and broader transcription ambiguities.
3. **Entropy-indexed relaxation schedule**: Replacing epoch-indexed weighting of wildcard paths with a schedule based on model confidence (predictive entropy), reducing dependence on total training length and improving generalization across datasets.
4. **Large-scale empirical validation**: Demonstrating improvements on 25 tasks across 19 languages and three corpora; independent validator transcriptions confirm that token-level models place bypass probability on genuinely disputed regions.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends [[Omni-temporal Classification]] by moving from word- to token-level granularity — a natural refinement motivated by the observation that transcription noise is localized. It relates to broader work on training with noisy labels and robustness to label noise in sequence models. The entropy-indexed schedule connects to curriculum learning and adaptive weighting schemes in deep learning. The paper also implicitly addresses the mismatch between "reference as ground truth" and linguistic reality, a theme in [[RLHF]] and preference-based training where model outputs may be valid even if they differ from a single reference.

## Practical Applications
1. **Multilingual ASR systems**: Transcription ambiguity is especially common across languages with different spelling conventions or phonetic overlap; token-level OTC improves robustness in diverse linguistic settings.
2. **Real-world noisy transcription**: In industry pipelines, crowd-sourced or auto-corrected transcripts often contain minor inconsistencies; token-level tolerance makes training more robust to these naturally occurring variations.
3. **Low-resource language ASR**: Where high-quality, perfectly consistent transcripts are rare, this method tolerates inconsistency without sacrificing supervision — allowing better use of available data.
4. **Streaming ASR endpoints**: More confident, robust models reduce post-processing and correction pipelines, lowering latency and operational cost.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ASR fine-tuning service**: A cloud API that fine-tunes speech models on customer data using token-level OTC, automatically handling transcription inconsistencies without manual correction — sell to contact centers and media companies.
2. **Noisy transcript cleaning tool**: A pre-processing service that identifies and flags ambiguous regions in existing transcripts, then retrains ASR models to tolerate them, improving quality on legacy datasets.
3. **Multilingual ASR platform**: A commercial ASR product targeting emerging markets and code-switched regions, leveraging token-level OTC to achieve competitive accuracy despite less consistent training data.
4. **Automated QA for transcription teams**: Software that detects which regions in a corpus have genuine ambiguity (vs. transcriber error) by training token-level models and visualizing high-wildcard regions — helps prioritize manual review.
5. **Enterprise speech analytics**: Enhanced ASR for call center and meeting analytics that tolerates minor transcription inconsistencies, improving accuracy on diverse speaker populations and accents without requiring perfect labels.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Requires auxiliary validation data**: The paper validates on independent transcriptions to confirm that wildcards target genuine ambiguity, not noise. In practice, obtaining such data may be costly.
2. **Entropy-indexed schedule tuning**: While reducing epoch-dependence, the entropy threshold and schedule shape are still hyperparameters that may need tuning per corpus.
3. **No analysis of failure modes**: The paper doesn't deeply explore when token-level tolerance might suppress legitimate errors (false negatives in learning) or when word-level tolerance is actually preferable.
4. **Computational overhead not quantified**: Adding hybrid graphs increases the alignment graph size; memory and speed overhead during training are not reported.
5. **Limited to CTC framework**: Benefits are specific to CTC-based systems; applicability to attention-based or RNN-T architectures is unclear.

## Open Questions
1. How does token-level OTC interact with newer training objectives (e.g., lattice-free MMI or transducer loss)? Do similar improvements transfer?
2. Can the model learn which token positions are inherently ambiguous (e.g., consonant clusters in certain phonetic contexts) and automatically adjust wildcard weights by position or linguistic feature?
3. What is the computational cost of the hybrid graph, and can it be optimized for real-time training or on-device fine-tuning?
4. Does this approach help when combined with semi-supervised learning (pseudo-labeling) where reference transcripts for unlabeled data are inherently noisy?
5. Can adversarial or uncertainty estimation techniques be used to automatically identify which tokens should have wildcard arcs, removing manual or entropy-based heuristics?

## Key Takeaway
Token-level flexibility in ASR training tolerates genuinely ambiguous speech-transcript pairs, improving accuracy across languages without needing perfectly consistent labels.
