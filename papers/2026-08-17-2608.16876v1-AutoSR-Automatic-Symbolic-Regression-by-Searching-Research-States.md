# AutoSR: Automatic Symbolic Regression by Searching Research States

**Authors:** Kejia Zhang, Youran Sun, Xinyu Ren, Chugang Yi, Haizhao Yang
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.16876v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16876v1)

## Summary
AutoSR is an automated system that discovers scientific equations from noisy data by treating symbolic regression as a multi-agent investigation rather than a simple curve-fitting problem. Instead of just finding equations that fit the data, it builds up a "research state" — a record of reasoning, evidence, and review — that explains why a particular equation is scientifically credible, and it recovers correct formulas on benchmark problems where previous methods fail.

## Theoretical Background
Symbolic regression — finding compact mathematical expressions that explain data — has traditionally relied on genetic algorithms or tree-based search to minimize numerical error. The core insight here is that numerical fit alone is insufficient: multiple structurally different equations can fit noisy training data identically but diverge wildly outside the observed regime. AutoSR draws on [[reinforcement-learning]] concepts (specifically progressive-widening Monte Carlo tree search — a planning algorithm that allocates exploration budget to the most promising branches) and multi-agent systems (proposer and reviewer agents that debate candidate equations). The paper treats scientific discovery as a structured search problem where accumulated computational evidence and reasoning pathways, not just final scores, guide what to try next.

## Core Contributions
1. **Research State framework**: Couples each candidate equation with its full derivation history — motivations, computational evidence, independent peer review — rather than discarding all information except the final formula and error metric.
2. **Proposer–reviewer agent loop**: Two agents iteratively generate and critique equations; the reviewer's feedback is encoded back into the search state to guide future proposals.
3. **Progressive-widening MCTS for symbolic regression**: Applies a principled tree search algorithm (normally used in games like AlphaGo) to allocate compute across competing mathematical hypotheses.
4. **Empirical recovery of hard problems**: Solves three cp3-bench problems and six LSR-Transform problems that no published baseline recovers, including cases requiring discovery of structurally novel expressions.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (understanding why a learned model makes specific predictions) with automated science. It extends prior symbolic regression systems (e.g., genetic programming, neural-guided search) by incorporating [[multi-agent-systems]] concepts and borrowing the tree-search framework from [[reinforcement-learning]] (particularly AlphaGo-style planning). The emphasis on *accumulated reasoning* as a first-class object parallels recent work in AI that maintains and learns from chain-of-thought traces. It also resonates with [[alignment]] concerns: by making the reasoning transparent and reviewable, AutoSR addresses the "black box" problem in automated discovery.

## Practical Applications
1. **Physics and engineering**: Discovering governing equations for fluid dynamics, material properties, or thermal systems from experimental or simulation data.
2. **Systems biology**: Inferring reaction kinetics or gene-regulatory network equations from high-noise biological measurements.
3. **Climate and geoscience**: Extracting interpretable relationships between climate variables (e.g., temperature, pressure, humidity) that generalize beyond training regions.
4. **Industrial anomaly detection**: Learning compact models of normal system behavior to detect and explain deviations in manufacturing or power grids.
5. **Educational science**: Automating textbook problem solving by generating verifiable, step-by-step derivations.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Scientific knowledge platform**: A SaaS tool where researchers upload experimental datasets and receive candidate equations with full justification reports, formatted for publication or grant proposals.
2. **Automated lab notebook**: Integrate AutoSR into lab software (e.g., Jupyter, LabView interfaces) to continuously generate and validate empirical models as new data arrives, with version control and peer-review workflows built in.
3. **Physics-informed model discovery for industry**: Consult service for manufacturers/energy companies to reverse-engineer unknown processes (e.g., catalyst efficiency, failure rates) and embed the recovered equations into control systems.
4. **Educational software**: Interactive tutoring system that generates step-by-step symbolic derivations and lets students critique the agent's reasoning to learn scientific method alongside mathematics.
5. **Open discovery marketplace**: Crowdsourced platform where researchers post open datasets; AutoSR instances compete to find equations, with provenance and evidence chains stored on-chain for reproducibility and attribution.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability to high-dimensional systems**: The paper focuses on low-dimensional benchmarks (typically <5 variables); how does the search tree explosion affect problems with 20+ input variables?
2. **Termination criteria**: Unclear when to halt the search; the paper doesn't specify principled stopping rules beyond wall-clock time or iteration limits.
3. **Noise robustness**: While "finite, noisy data" are mentioned, there is no systematic ablation on noise levels; true generalization outside observed regimes is validated only on synthetic benchmarks, not real messy data.
4. **Agent design choices**: The proposer and reviewer architectures seem hand-crafted; it's unclear how sensitive results are to these design decisions or how to adapt them to new domains.
5. **Computational cost**: No runtime comparisons; the overhead of maintaining full research states and running MCTS may be prohibitive for large-scale or interactive scenarios.
6. **Equation simplicity**: The paper optimizes for "algebraic equivalence" to ground truth but doesn't explore trade-offs between accuracy, interpretability, and computational simplicity in real applications.

## Open Questions
1. How does AutoSR scale to problems with >5 input variables or >1000 data points? Does the tree search remain tractable?
2. Can the proposer and reviewer agents be learned end-to-end (e.g., via [[large-language-models]]) rather than hand-coded, and does this improve or hurt interpretability?
3. Does the accumulated research state provide genuine scientific insight beyond the final equation — e.g., can domain experts learn something new from the proposer–reviewer dialogue?
4. How does AutoSR perform on **extrapolation** (predicting far outside the training domain) compared to purely numerical methods like neural networks?
5. Can the framework be extended to discover differential equations, inequalities, or conservation laws, rather than just algebraic expressions?
6. What happens when the true generating process is non-polynomial or transcendental (e.g., chaotic, fractal, or involving special functions)? Can the search find these, or does it bias toward simple elementary functions?

## Key Takeaway
Automated equation discovery now builds and preserves a scientific reasoning record instead of just optimizing numerical fit.
