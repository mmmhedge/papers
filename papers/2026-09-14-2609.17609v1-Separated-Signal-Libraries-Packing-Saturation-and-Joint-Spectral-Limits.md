# Separated Signal Libraries: Packing, Saturation, and Joint Spectral Limits

**Authors:** Marc da Costa Nunes
**Published:** 2026-09-14 | **arXiv:** [2609.17609v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.17609v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how to build large libraries of trading signals (forecasts of asset returns) while keeping them sufficiently uncorrelated with each other. The authors frame signal selection as a geometric packing problem—signals are points on a sphere, and a correlation cap means maintaining minimum angular separation—and prove when an equally-weighted portfolio of many signals converges to a theoretically predictable quantity. The work matters because portfolio managers need to understand whether combining many imperfect signals produces reliable results or just dilutes signal strength.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on classical portfolio theory and information theory. A cross-sectional signal is a forecast vector over multiple assets; when normalized, it becomes a point on a high-dimensional sphere. The key mathematical idea is treating a signal's history across $T$ dates as a point on a product of $T$ spheres, and a correlation constraint as a packing problem—the classical problem of fitting as many objects as possible in a space while maintaining minimum separation.

The paper invokes the information coefficient (IC)—a standard finance metric measuring forecast accuracy—and principal component analysis (PCA), which identifies the main sources of variance in a dataset. It also draws on coding theory (Gilbert-Varshamov codes) to show what library sizes are achievable under separation constraints.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formalized the signal library design as a geometric packing problem**, showing that mere separation (low pairwise correlation) does **not** guarantee a library converges to a stable principal component.

2. **Proved that the equally-weighted sum (EWS) of a large, separated library has a limiting distribution that depends critically on the admission rule**, not just separation. Under a positive IC margin $\beta$, the leading eigenvalue tends to $\beta^2$; without it, the second moment becomes isotropic (no distinguished component).

3. **Established finite-sample bounds**: screening $J$ candidates from $T$ dates requires only $\log J = o(T)$ growth—a surprisingly loose constraint.

4. **Demonstrated non-uniqueness via coding theory**: large libraries with identical separation properties can have EWS either aligned with or orthogonal to the principal component, depending on which signals are admitted.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[factor models]] and [[dimensionality reduction]] in quantitative finance—the question of how many independent bets can you reliably combine. It connects to [[portfolio optimization]] and the curse of dimensionality: more signals aren't always better if they're correlated or noise-like.

The packing-problem framing echoes work in information theory and compressed sensing, where sparsity and separation constraints limit what can be recovered. The IC-based screening relates to [[backtesting]] and multiple-comparison problems—how to select predictive models without overfitting.

## Practical Applications
1. **Signal library construction for quantitative hedge funds**: managers can size their signal pools (number of predictive models) without risk of redundancy or dilution, knowing when equally-weighted combinations will work.

2. **Risk management in multi-factor portfolios**: understanding when adding factors helps vs. hurts portfolio stability; critical for allocating capital to strategy teams.

3. **Market-neutral strategy design**: separating uncorrelated trading signals is essential for hedge funds that want true diversification without hidden concentration risk.

4. **Systematic asset allocation**: pension funds and CTAs blend multiple forecasting models; this work predicts when larger libraries improve out-of-sample performance.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Signal audit tool**: Software that analyzes a fund's signal library for true separation vs. hidden correlation, predicting whether adding more signals will improve or dilute performance.

2. **Optimal signal selection engine**: An optimization service that, given a candidate set of signals and an IC margin requirement, selects the maximum-size subset guaranteed to achieve principal-component alignment.

3. **Portfolio rebalancing advisor**: A tool for asset managers that monitors signal correlation drift in real time and recommends when to prune or refresh the signal library.

4. **Synthetic signal generator**: Use Gilbert-Varshamov code theory to algorithmically construct new, maximally separated trading signals from existing ones—expanding library size without redundancy.

5. **Backtesting certification service**: Validate that a quantitative strategy's signal library meets separation and IC criteria, reducing false positives in strategy selection.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Theory assumes perfect spherical geometry**: real trading signals live in markets with fat tails, regime breaks, and non-stationary correlations that violate the steady-state assumptions.

2. **No market data validation**: all results are theoretical/numerical; unclear whether the separation benchmarks and IC margins translate to real alpha.

3. **Equally-weighted assumption**: practitioners rarely weight signals equally; the results don't immediately extend to optimal weighting schemes or risk parity.

4. **Finite-sample effects**: the convergence rates and spectral decay are asymptotic; for practical $T$ (say, 5 years of daily data), small-sample bias could dominate.

5. **IC definition sensitivity**: the paper assumes IC is measured consistently; in practice, IC estimates are noisy and horizon-dependent, breaking the assumptions.

## Open Questions
1. **How do these results change under time-varying correlation?** Real signal correlations drift with market regimes; does the spectrum remain stable?

2. **What happens when signals are not equally weighted?** Can you derive limiting distributions for optimal or risk-parity weighted combinations?

3. **How do transaction costs and implementation noise affect the convergence?** Does the benefit of PC1 alignment survive real execution slippage?

4. **Can the framework extend to hierarchical or adaptive library construction?** Many funds update signal sets dynamically; are there online versions of these results?

5. **How tight are the bounds in practice?** For a typical hedge fund with $T = 1000$ trading days and $J = 100$ signals, how much of the theoretical gain is captured?

## Key Takeaway
Building large signal libraries requires controlling correlation and admission rules, not just separation—and alignment with portfolio performance is not guaranteed.
