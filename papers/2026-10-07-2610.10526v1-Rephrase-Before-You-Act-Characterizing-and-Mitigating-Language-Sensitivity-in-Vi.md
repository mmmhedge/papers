# Rephrase Before You Act: Characterizing and Mitigating Language Sensitivity in Vision-Language-Action Models

**Authors:** Mikey Watts, Yuchen Cui
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10526v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10526v1)

## Summary
Vision-language-action models (VLAs)—neural systems that take images and text instructions to control robotic actions—are surprisingly brittle to minor wording changes, even though their underlying vision-language components are more robust. This paper demonstrates the problem systematically and proposes a simple fix: automatically rephrase incoming instructions using rules distilled from a large language model, improving task success by 16–27% without retraining the policy.

## Theoretical Background
Vision-language-action models combine two components: vision-language models (like CLIP or similar systems trained to match images with text descriptions) and action policies (neural networks trained to map observations and language to robot commands). Prior work assumed that robustness to language variation would transfer from the vision-language component to the full VLA, but this paper shows that assumption breaks down. The work builds on observations that neural policies can be brittle to input distribution shifts, and that large language models can distill implicit patterns into explicit rules.

## Core Contributions
1. **Systematic characterization**: Quantifies language sensitivity through single-word edits and oracle phrase searches, showing phrasing alone accounts for ~21 points of performance gap between in-distribution and out-of-distribution tasks.
2. **Rephrasing-as-mitigation approach**: Proposes a deployment-time intervention (not retraining) where an LLM distills evidence from training task phrasings into 10–20 explicit rephrasing rules; incoming instructions are rewritten once before being passed to the policy.
3. **Zero-shot generalization**: The rule-based method works on unseen tasks and instruction phrasings without per-step verification or policy modification.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates 16–27% relative improvement across adversarial, VLM-generated, and human-generated phrasings on held-out tasks; lifts LIBERO benchmark from 93.6% to 97.8% success.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader work on [[robustness]] and [[distribution shift]] in neural policies. It parallels [[prompt engineering]] and [[in-context learning]] literature, showing that systematic instruction variation matters for embodied AI just as it does for language models. The approach echoes [[data augmentation]] and [[rule distillation]] strategies but applies them at inference time rather than training time. The paper implicitly engages with questions about why vision-language models appear more robust than the policies built on them—touching on issues of compositionality and grounding.

## Practical Applications
1. **Robotic manipulation in unstructured environments**: Household robots, warehouse automation, or surgical assistants where instructions come from non-expert users with varying vocabulary.
2. **Multi-user robot systems**: Shared robots (e.g., in labs or factories) where different operators phrase identical tasks differently; a single rule set ensures consistency.
3. **Sim-to-real transfer**: Bridging the gap where simulators produce clean, templated instructions but real users phrase commands idiosyncratically.
4. **Accessibility and inclusivity**: Enabling people with language disabilities or non-native speakers to reliably control robots regardless of phrasing.
5. **Instruction following in conversational agents**: Embodied LLM-based agents that need to execute user intent robustly across paraphrases.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **VLA Robustness Middleware**: A SaaS layer (API or plugin) that sits between user instruction input and any VLA policy; ingests task-specific rephrasing rules (auto-generated or user-curated) and normalizes instructions, sold to robotics companies and research labs.
2. **Instruction Augmentation Service for Robot Training**: Help robotics teams collect minimal training data (a few phrasings per task) and auto-generate rephrasing rules, reducing data labeling burden.
3. **Multimodal Instruction Normalization**: Extend the approach to video instructions or sketches; a tool for roboticists to convert diverse human inputs (voice, text, gesture) into canonical forms before policy execution.
4. **Robot Command Autocorrect**: Consumer-facing smart home or personal robot app that learns household-specific rephrasing rules and silently corrects user speech-to-text errors or informal commands before robot execution.
5. **Embodied AI Evaluation Benchmark**: Publish a benchmark suite (open-source or commercial) of adversarial and natural instruction phrasings for VLAs, + a tool to measure and mitigate language sensitivity in customer models.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Rule brittleness**: Distilled rules may not generalize to truly novel domains or instruction structures; rules are task-specific and require a small set of training phrasings.
2. **LLM dependency**: The approach relies on an LLM to distill rules; rule quality depends on LLM capability and what few examples you feed it. No analysis of how many examples are needed or how sensitive results are to the LLM choice.
3. **No error analysis**: The paper doesn't characterize which types of rephrasing failures occur or when the rule-based approach breaks down.
4. **Computational cost**: Requires calling an LLM at deployment (for rule distillation) and at inference time (to apply rules); latency not reported.
5. **Assumes access to policy**: The method requires scoring many phrasings on training tasks; assumes you can run the policy offline during rule discovery. Not viable if the policy is proprietary or expensive to query.
6. **Limited scope**: Evaluated on LIBERO and a few other benchmarks; unclear how well it generalizes to very different robot morphologies, tasks (e.g., navigation vs. manipulation), or instruction modalities (e.g., sketches, video).

## Open Questions
1. **Rule minimalism**: How many examples per task are truly needed to distill robust rules? Is there a principled way to set the rule budget (10 vs. 20 rules)?
2. **Interpretability**: Can the distilled rules be understood by humans? Do they encode meaningful linguistic patterns or are they surface-level heuristics?
3. **Composition**: Do rules generalize compositionally (e.g., if you learn rules for "turn on" and "stove," can you handle "turn on the stove" without seeing it)?
4. **Failure modes**: What types of instructions cause the rephrasing to fail or backfire? Can you predict when a rephrasing will hurt?
5. **Multi-modal instructions**: Can this extend to instructions that mix text, images (e.g., "make it look like this photo"), or video demonstrations?
6. **Continual learning**: Can the rule set be updated online as new instructions and failures arrive?
7. **Cross-task transfer**: Do rules learned on one set of tasks help on completely different task categories (e.g., manipulation → navigation)?

## Key Takeaway
Robotic policies fail on paraphrased instructions; rewriting inputs with LLM-distilled rules fixes it without retraining.
