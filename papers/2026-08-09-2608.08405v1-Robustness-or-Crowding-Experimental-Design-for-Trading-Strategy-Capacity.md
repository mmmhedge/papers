# Robustness or Crowding: Experimental Design for Trading Strategy Capacity

**Authors:** Alejandro Rodriguez Dominguez, Miquel Noguer i Alonso
**Published:** 2026-08-09 | **arXiv:** [2608.08405v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08405v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental but overlooked problem in quantitative finance: how much capital can a trading strategy absorb before its edge disappears? The authors show that standard observational approaches (like regression on capital deployed) rest on incompatible assumptions, and they design experiments that can actually answer this question. The work matters because understanding strategy capacity is critical for portfolio managers deciding how much money to allocate to a strategy.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on causal inference in econometrics, particularly the challenge of identifying causal effects from observational data. It engages with two core problems: first, that deploying capital into a strategy gradually erodes its edge (a dynamic effect that fixed-period observation misses); second, that parallel implementations of the same strategy trade overlapping securities and thus violate independence assumptions standard in statistical comparison. The authors use experimental design principles to navigate these constraints—specifically, leveraging same-date comparisons to isolate market-wide shocks, while recognizing that this design choice creates trade-offs in what effects can be measured.

## Core Contributions
1. **Problem formulation**: Explicitly frames strategy capacity as a causal question and identifies why observational proxies fail—they rest on incompatible assumptions about market structure and crowding dynamics.
2. **Design trade-off characterization**: Shows that the same-date design that makes comparisons robust to market shocks (by comparing implementations on identical dates) simultaneously prevents measurement of the crowding effect itself, because aggregate position effects are absorbed by the date fixed effect.
3. **Identification routes**: Proposes two distinct experimental paths: (a) comparing implementations with deliberately different exposure to aggregate positioning, or (b) varying deployment levels over time. Each is characterized by what it identifies and its practical cost.
4. **Finite-sample correction**: Establishes how much a fixed holding period understates the eventual capacity effect and provides a correction formula.
5. **Calibration and design rules**: Builds a purpose-built panel dataset and prices out what a compliant study would cost, making the guidance concrete.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[causal inference]], [[market microstructure]], and [[risk-management]] in quantitative finance. It extends classical portfolio capacity analysis (e.g., work on optimal position sizing) by making the experimental design explicit and rigorous. It relates to broader problems in [[algorithmic-trading]] around strategy decay and edge erosion as capital scales, which practitioners often address via heuristics rather than principled measurement. The paper's emphasis on design as a solution to identification problems echoes methodological work in development economics and industrial organization, but applies it to a finance-specific setting where non-independence of parallel implementations is a novel constraint.

## Practical Applications
1. **Capital allocation decisions**: Portfolio managers can use the proposed designs to test how much capital a newly discovered strategy can safely absorb before expected returns collapse, enabling better go/no-go decisions.
2. **Risk budgeting**: Understanding strategy capacity at different deployment levels helps construct diversified portfolios where capacity constraints are known, reducing tail risk from unexpected edge erosion.
3. **Fund scaling and fundraising**: Managers can credibly communicate to investors the capital constraints of their strategies, improving transparency and reducing disappointment as funds scale.
4. **Strategy evaluation in backtesting**: Quant teams can apply the finite-sample correction to historical backtests to estimate what effects they would actually observe in live trading at planned capital levels.
5. **Regulatory capital planning**: Understanding capacity helps firms size prime broker relationships and manage leverage appropriately.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability to non-linear effects**: The paper assumes capacity erodes smoothly; if edge collapse is non-convex or exhibits thresholds, fixed designs may miss the true capacity boundary.
2. **Market regime dependence**: Capacity estimates are likely regime-dependent (different in high-vol vs. low-vol markets), but the paper does not deeply address how to design studies that generalize across regimes.
3. **Strategy heterogeneity**: The approach assumes a single strategy being scaled. Multi-leg strategies, conditional logic, or adaptive strategies may violate the independence and scalability assumptions.
4. **Implementation differences**: Parallel implementations in practice differ in execution (timing, slippage, broker). The paper assumes these differences are small, which may not hold for complex strategies.
5. **Finite sample power**: The paper notes that finite deployment levels "cannot reveal" certain effects; it does not fully characterize the statistical power requirements for a credible study, leaving practitioners uncertain about sample size.
6. **Time-varying market depth**: The paper does not model changes in market microstructure (decimalization, algo adoption, venue fragmentation), which change how crowding manifests.

## Open Questions
1. **How does capacity interact with market regime?** Can the same experimental design be used to estimate capacity curves conditional on volatility, spread, or liquidity regimes?
2. **What is the optimal experimental design (in a statistical sense)?** The paper characterizes trade-offs; a follow-up could use optimal experimental design theory to minimize cost of the most informative study.
3. **How do correlated strategies behave?** If multiple strategies are crowding the same securities, does capacity degrade superlinearly? Can the framework extend to portfolio-level capacity?
4. **Can machine learning improve capacity estimation?** Could non-parametric methods (e.g., kernel methods or neural nets) fit capacity curves more flexibly than the parametric models implied here?
5. **How does capacity affect strategy correlation?** As a strategy is scaled and its edge erodes, does it become more correlated with other strategies? This matters for diversification.
6. **Can capacity be predicted in advance?** Can pre-deployment characteristics (strategy liquidity needs, overlap with index, factor exposure) predict eventual capacity without running the full experiment?

## Key Takeaway
Strategy capacity—how much capital an edge can absorb—is a causal question that standard observational analysis fails to answer; experimental design can recover it, but designers must choose between isolating private effects or measuring aggregate crowding.
