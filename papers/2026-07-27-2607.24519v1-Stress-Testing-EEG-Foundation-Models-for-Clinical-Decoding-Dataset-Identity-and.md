# Stress-Testing EEG Foundation Models for Clinical Decoding: Dataset Identity and Targeted Negative Controls

**Authors:** Marzieh Zare
**Published:** 2026-07-27 | **arXiv:** [2607.24519v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24519v1)

## Summary
This paper stress-tests six pretrained EEG foundation models on clinical tasks (dementia, Alzheimer's, seizure detection) using rigorous evaluation protocols with negative controls. The authors show that apparent model performance often reflects dataset identity leakage or weak baselines rather than genuine clinical signal—a crucial warning for researchers deploying these models in medical settings.

## Theoretical Background
EEG foundation models are self-supervised neural networks trained on large, unlabeled brain recordings to learn generalizable representations; they can then be fine-tuned or frozen (used without retraining) on downstream clinical tasks. Prior work assumes that pretraining on diverse EEG data improves transfer across patients and hospitals, but this paper questions that assumption. The evaluation methods used here—leave-one-subject-out (ensuring patient-disjoint train/test splits), label permutation (shuffling true diagnoses to check if the model uses real signal), and random feature baselines—are standard rigor checks in machine learning but are often skipped in medical AI papers.

## Core Contributions
1. **Systematic benchmarking** of six major EEG foundation models (LaBraM, EEGMamba, CBraMod, REVE, BENDR, BIOT) on five clinical decoding tasks across four datasets with explicit recording-level and subject-grouped splits.
2. **Dataset identity leakage**: Shows that frozen embeddings from pretrained models encode dataset source nearly perfectly (AUROC 1.000 at 50 principal components), meaning the model may learn dataset artifacts rather than disease markers.
3. **Weak baseline problem**: Randomly initialized encoders outperform pretrained REVE on Korean dementia (0.659 vs. 0.570 AUROC), suggesting the "pretrained" advantage is illusory on some tasks.
4. **Targeted negative controls**: Applies scrambled-label fine-tuning, Gaussian random projection, and label-permuted training to isolate genuine signal from spurious correlation.
5. **Task-dependent results**: Only ictal detection on CHB-MIT shows clear pretrained benefit (0.793 AUROC, 9.2pp above random initialization); other tasks fail to replicate expected transfer.

## Connections to Other Work
This work mirrors validation frameworks in [[RLHF]] and alignment research—stress-testing models with negative controls before deployment. It relates to broader concerns about [[mechanistic-interpretability|mechanistic interpretability]] in neural networks: what are models actually learning? The dataset shift problem echoes challenges in computer vision domain adaptation and cross-silo federated learning. The paper's emphasis on *who* the train/test split is done at (subject vs. recording vs. dataset) connects to causal inference and confounding in observational studies.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical validation pipelines**: Hospitals adopting EEG foundation models should mandate negative-control benchmarks and patient-disjoint held-out sets before clinical deployment.
2. **Dementia and Alzheimer's screening**: Current pretrained models are not yet ready as standalone diagnostic tools; classical EEG features remain competitive.
3. **Seizure detection systems**: REVE shows promise on multi-patient seizure data, potentially useful for ICU monitoring, but requires careful validation in each hospital's dataset.
4. **Multi-center EEG studies**: When pooling data across sites, this paper shows dataset identity dominates learned representations—preprocessing and domain-alignment techniques are essential.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **EEG Model Validation SaaS**: Offer hospitals and device makers automated stress-testing pipelines (negative controls, subject-disjoint splits, classical baselines) before deploying foundation models on their data.
2. **Clinical EEG Foundation Model (with guarantees)**: Develop and openly release an EEG model trained with explicit safeguards against dataset leakage, bundled with validation templates.
3. **EEG Data Harmonization Engine**: A preprocessing service that removes dataset-specific artifacts before feeding to pretrained models, reducing the identity-leakage problem.
4. **Federated EEG Model Training**: Enable hospitals to contribute patient data to a shared foundation model without shipping raw EEG, while certifying generalization across sites with built-in negative controls.
5. **Neurodiagnostics Benchmark Platform**: A hosted benchmark for researchers to compare EEG models on standardized clinical tasks with enforced evaluation rigor (e.g., patient-disjoint splits, pre-registered negative controls).

## Technical Limitations
1. **Small patient cohorts**: Some datasets (e.g., CHB-MIT with n=23) have limited sample size, making AUROC estimates noisy; confidence intervals are not reported.
2. **Frozen-probe-only evaluation**: The paper only tests models with frozen embeddings and linear readouts, not end-to-end fine-tuning, so it doesn't capture the full potential of transfer learning.
3. **No mechanistic analysis**: The paper shows dataset identity leakage but doesn't explain *why* or *which model components* leak it; visualization or layer-wise analysis would be stronger.
4. **Dataset heterogeneity**: Four datasets with different sampling rates, montages, and patient populations may have confounding variation; results on any single dataset may not generalize.
5. **Lack of calibration**: No discussion of model calibration (are predicted probabilities actually trustworthy?) or uncertainty quantification, critical for clinical use.
6. **Limited negative control scope**: The paper tests label permutation and random initialization, but not adversarial or out-of-distribution perturbations (e.g., artifacts, electrode dropout).

## Open Questions
1. **Which architectural components leak dataset identity?** Layer-wise probing or ablation studies could pinpoint whether leakage happens in early encoders, attention heads, or output layers.
2. **Can domain adaptation or multi-task learning reduce dataset leakage** while preserving clinical signal?
3. **Why does REVE fail on Korean dementia but succeed on seizure detection?** Are seizures more "universal" across EEG systems, or does the task complexity matter?
4. **How do these models perform on truly unseen hospital systems** (e.g., a model trained on EEG systems A, B, C tested on system D)?
5. **Can we design EEG foundation models that explicitly decorrelate dataset identity** from task-relevant features during pretraining?
6. **What is the minimal patient cohort size needed to validate a clinical EEG model** with statistical confidence?

## Key Takeaway
Pretrained EEG models often leak dataset identity instead of learning disease—rigorous negative controls and patient-disjoint splits are mandatory before clinical use.
