# Neural Petri flows for chemical reactions

**Authors:** Jose Eduardo Escrig Molina, Daniel Probst
**Published:** 2026-10-06 | **arXiv:** [2610.08750v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08750v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes Neural Petri Flows, a neural architecture that learns chemical reaction dynamics while guaranteeing they obey physical conservation laws (like atomic valence rules). Instead of building neural networks from scratch, the authors hard-wire the mathematical structure of Petri nets—a formal model where places represent bonds, tokens represent bond order, and transitions represent bond formation or breaking—and let the network learn only the reaction rates. The result is a model that predicts reaction products, atom mappings, and enzyme classification with higher accuracy than existing methods, while ensuring every prediction is physically valid.

## Theoretical Background
Petri nets are a formal computational model where a system's state is represented by tokens distributed across places (locations), and transitions fire (occur) based on enabling rules, updating the state via a firing rule. In chemistry, this maps naturally: places represent atomic bonds and valence (bonding capacity), tokens represent bond order, transitions are bond formation or breaking events, and the enabling rule enforces valence conservation. Prior neural approaches treat Petri nets loosely—using them as a scaffolding for message-passing without enforcing their mathematical guarantees. This paper builds on conservation laws (Noether's theorem principle applied to chemistry: quantities like total valence must remain constant) and asks: what minimal architectural constraints guarantee a learned neural network respects these laws by design, not by luck?

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical characterization**: The authors prove that any Petri net respecting valence conservation must use the firing form m′ = m + Cσ (where m is state, C is the stoichiometric matrix, σ is the firing vector), and that non-negativity of reaction rates forces local enabling rules—leaving only the rate law (propensity of transitions) as a learned degree of freedom.

2. **Neural Petri Flow architecture**: A parameter-free, hard-wired Petri net structure where only the rate law (learned via a neural network) is trainable, eliminating invalid predictions by construction.

3. **Empirical validation across three distinct tasks**: The model maps atoms in reactions (88.8% accuracy vs. 85.6% for RXNMapper without training), predicts reaction products (87.7% on USPTO-480K), and classifies enzyme function (EC numbers: 90.2% at third level, 5.6 points ahead of prior work)—all from the same learned firing vector.

4. **Physical validity guarantee**: Every top-1 prediction is a valid molecule; no filter needed. Elementary steps (FlowER: 90.5% accuracy) also remain chemically valid.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (hard-wiring known structure rather than learning black-box models) and [[graph-neural-networks]] (reaction networks are naturally graphs). It contrasts with end-to-end learned models like Transformer-based reaction predictors and [[generative-models]] that learn distributions over molecules without enforcing constraints. The approach echoes physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), which embed differential equations into architectures, but applied to discrete chemical stoichiometry. It also relates to symbolic reasoning in chemistry (e.g., retrosynthesis engines like GraphRetro) that combine learned components with rule-based guarantees, but grounds the rules in formal Petri net theory rather than heuristics.

## Practical Applications
1. **Drug discovery & retrosynthesis**: Predicting synthetic routes for novel molecules with guaranteed chemical validity, reducing costly failed experiments.
2. **Enzyme engineering**: EC number prediction enables automated enzyme classification and substrate prediction for biocatalysis design.
3. **Reaction optimization**: Atom mapping without training accelerates reaction mechanism studies in academic and industrial labs.
4. **Elementary step prediction**: For mechanistic studies in computational chemistry, especially enzyme kinetics (FlowER) and reaction pathway discovery.
5. **Process development**: In chemical manufacturing, ensuring every predicted pathway is chemically feasible reduces simulation overhead and design cycles.

## Product & Startup Ideas

5. **Cheminformatics API for pharma/material science labs**: REST endpoint predicting reaction products with physical validity certificates, enabling high-throughput virtual screening pipelines without filteringand post-hoc validation.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited to valence-based chemistry**: The model hard-wires atomic valence rules; extensions to other conserved quantities (spin states, charge, isotopes in reactions) are unclear.
2. **Requires curated atom-mapped data**: Training leverages the "firing vector" (atom mapping), which must be provided; the model does not learn from unannotated reaction SMILES alone.
3. **Rate law remains a learned bottleneck**: While structure is hard-wired, the rate law (propensity function) is still a neural approximation; it could learn invalid patterns in edge cases or under distribution shift (e.g., reactions with unusual functional groups or non-standard conditions).
4. **Scalability to large molecules unclear**: Experiments focus on USPTO and enzymatic reactions; performance on very large polymers, organometallic systems, or reactions with 50+ atoms not shown.
5. **Single-step prediction**: The model predicts products for one reaction step; it does not learn multi-step synthesis planning or reaction sequences, limiting applicability to complex synthesis workflows.
6. **No uncertainty quantification**: No confidence scores or epistemic uncertainty estimates, making it risky for high-stakes applications without calibration studies.

## Open Questions
1. **Generalization under distribution shift**: How does performance degrade on reaction types, functional groups, or conditions not well represented in training data (e.g., photochemistry, plasma chemistry)?
2. **Extension to electron-transfer reactions**: The paper mentions "electrons as tokens" for elementary steps; can this be unified with traditional valence-based Petri nets, and does it scale to radical mechanisms?
3. **Conditional rate laws**: Can the rate law be learned conditioned on reaction conditions (solvent, temperature, catalyst)? Current work seems condition-agnostic.
4. **Active learning via Petri net structure**: Could uncertainty in the learned rate law guide targeted data collection to improve predictions on under-represented reactions?
5. **Multi-step synthesis planning**: Can this architecture be extended to sequential reaction networks, e.g., retrosynthesis trees with hard-wired conservation across steps?
6. **Interpretability of rate laws**: Can the learned rate law be inverted or inspected to extract chemical insights (e.g., which atoms/bonds drive reactivity), making the model more interpretable than end-to-end black boxes?

## Key Takeaway
Hard-wiring chemistry's conservation laws into neural nets guarantees valid predictions while learning only reaction rates, outperforming unconstrained models on atom mapping and product prediction.
