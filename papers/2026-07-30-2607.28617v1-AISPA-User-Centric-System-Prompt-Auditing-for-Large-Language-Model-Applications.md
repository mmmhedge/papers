# AISPA: User-Centric System Prompt Auditing for Large Language Model Applications

**Authors:** Xiangning Lin, Shenzhe Zhu, Shu Yang, Zhenyu Zhang, Haoqian Zhang et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28617v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28617v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces AISPA, a framework for auditing system prompts — the hidden instructions developers embed in commercial AI applications to control behavior. By analyzing 3,249 instructions across 88 commercial products, the authors reveal that while most AI products include some user-protective instructions, they're often shallow and inconsistent, and many still contain instructions that work against user interests. The work highlights a critical transparency gap in deployed AI systems.

## Theoretical Background
System prompts are a core control mechanism in LLM-based applications — they function as standing instructions that shape model outputs before users interact with the system. The paper builds on prior work in AI safety and alignment, which focuses on ensuring AI systems behave in ways aligned with human values and intentions. It also draws from human-computer interaction (HCI) research, which emphasizes user-centric evaluation. The authors ground their audit framework in eight dimensions that matter to end users: helpfulness, truthfulness, fairness, privacy, security, legality, creativity, and accountability. This connects to [[Alignment]] concerns in AI — the broader challenge of making systems do what humans actually want them to do.

## Core Contributions
1. **AISPA Framework**: A systematic, user-centric methodology for evaluating system prompts across eight distinct dimensions, enabling structured comparison across products.
2. **Large-scale empirical audit**: The first comprehensive analysis of system prompts in commercial AI products (88 products, 3,249 instructions), revealing actual practices rather than claims.
3. **Key empirical findings**: Quantified variation in prompt design (60+ protective instructions in some products vs. fewer than 5 in others), discovered that 98.9% of products have at least one protective instruction but only 24% cover all eight dimensions, and documented that ~40% of products still contain problematic instructions despite overall trends toward user protection.
4. **Transparency argument**: Demonstrated that system prompts are largely opaque to users and regulators, creating an accountability gap in deployed systems.

## Connections to Other Work
This work intersects with multiple research areas:
- **[[Alignment]]**: Directly addresses the challenge of ensuring AI system behavior matches user and societal interests.
- **AI safety and interpretability**: Related to [[Mechanistic Interpretability]] in that system prompts are a human-readable control mechanism, but at a higher level than neuron-level analysis.
- **AI ethics and governance**: Complements work on transparency, fairness audits, and regulatory oversight of AI systems.
- **Prompt engineering**: Extends beyond optimization for capability to include evaluation of safety properties.
- **Human-AI interaction**: Connects to HCI research on user expectations and trust in AI systems.

Unlike mechanistic interpretability work (which examines internal model representations) or [[Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback|RLHF]] (which trains alignment into models), AISPA focuses on auditing the explicit control layer developers use in production.

## Practical Applications
1. **Regulatory compliance**: Auditors, regulators, and compliance teams can use AISPA to assess whether commercial AI products meet policy requirements for user protection and fairness.
2. **Consumer trust certification**: Independent organizations (e.g., consumer advocate groups) could certify AI products based on system prompt audits, similar to energy-efficiency labels.
3. **Developer benchmarking**: AI product teams can use the framework to benchmark their prompt design against peers and identify gaps in coverage across the eight dimensions.
4. **Third-party oversight**: Researchers and advocacy organizations can conduct independent audits of commercial AI systems to surface misalignment between product claims and actual behavior.
5. **Policy design**: Regulators can use empirical findings from audits (e.g., the prevalence of problematic instructions) to inform standards and guidelines for responsible AI deployment.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **AI Prompt Audit SaaS**: A software-as-a-service platform that automatically audits system prompts for commercial AI products using AISPA, generates compliance reports, and tracks changes over time. Target: AI product companies seeking internal quality assurance and regulators needing oversight.
2. **LLM Trust Score / Transparency Label**: A third-party rating service that audits popular AI products and publishes transparency labels (like nutrition labels) showing coverage across the eight AISPA dimensions. Consumers and enterprises could use these ratings to choose tools.
3. **Prompt Governance Platform for Enterprises**: Internal tooling for large organizations deploying custom LLM applications, enabling security/compliance teams to audit, version-control, and approve system prompts before deployment.
4. **Regulatory Intelligence for AI**: A research and advisory firm helping regulators develop evidence-based standards for system prompt design by continuously analyzing and publishing audit findings across products.
5. **Decentralized Prompt Auditing Network**: A blockchain or community-driven platform where independent auditors submit system prompt analyses, creating a public, immutable record of AI product behavior over time.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Static snapshot**: The audit captures a single point in time; system prompts change frequently, so findings may be outdated quickly.
2. **Prompt extraction challenges**: For closed or proprietary systems, extracting system prompts may be difficult or impossible without user interaction, limiting audit coverage.
3. **Limited to text instructions**: The framework doesn't account for other control mechanisms (e.g., in-context examples, retrieval-augmented generation, fine-tuning) that also shape behavior.
4. **Coding subjectivity**: Classification of instructions as "protective" vs. "problematic" involves human judgment; inter-rater reliability and potential biases aren't fully addressed.
5. **No behavioral verification**: The audit examines instructions as written, not their actual effect on model outputs. A protective instruction might fail in practice due to model misalignment or adversarial inputs.
6. **Taxonomy completeness**: The eight dimensions, while user-centric, may not capture all concerns (e.g., environmental impact, labor practices in data collection).
7. **No ground truth**: Without user studies, it's unclear whether the eight dimensions truly reflect what users care about or how much weight to assign each.

## Open Questions
1. **Behavioral validation**: Do system prompts actually produce the intended effects in practice? How often do models violate or circumvent protective instructions?
2. **User awareness and preferences**: What do end users actually want in system prompts? Do the eight dimensions reflect user values, or are they researcher assumptions?
3. **Adversarial robustness**: How vulnerable are system prompts to prompt injection or jailbreak attacks? Does prompt length or complexity correlate with robustness?
4. **Causal design patterns**: Which design patterns (phrasing, length, specificity) are most effective for each dimension? Can developers learn optimal designs from the audit data?
5. **Longitudinal dynamics**: How do system prompts evolve in response to incidents, regulatory pressure, or competitive moves? Do products converge toward common designs?
6. **Cross-lingual and cultural variation**: Do system prompts vary meaningfully across languages, regions, or cultural contexts? Are protective instructions culturally sensitive?
7. **Alignment with fine-tuning and RLHF**: How do system prompts interact with other alignment techniques? Can they compensate for misaligned training, or do they conflict?
8. **Economic incentives**: What organizational, competitive, or financial factors drive variation in prompt design? Are protective instructions costly to implement?

## Key Takeaway
Most commercial AI products lack transparent, consistent system prompts that protect users across multiple dimensions—revealing a critical alignment and accountability gap in deployed AI systems.
