# AdviSD: Learning to Advise Frontier LLMs via Targeted Multi-Turn Self-Distillation

**Authors:** Rishabh Agrawal, Hejie Cui, Shasha Li, Shanchan Wu, Sercan Ö. Arık
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38142v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38142v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces AdviSD, a method for training a small "advisor" model that steers frozen large language models (like Gemini or Claude) using natural-language guidance. The key insight is that not all feedback corrections are equally valuable for learning — some can mislead future decisions — so the method selectively learns from corrections that actually change the executor's behavior. AdviSD outperforms baselines by 4–6 points on standard benchmarks and generalizes to new tasks and different model families.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two main foundations: (1) **reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF)** and reward-based RL for language models, where models learn policies by optimizing task rewards; and (2) **self-distillation**, a technique where a model learns from a filtered or modified copy of itself, often used to improve sample efficiency. The paper also draws on multi-turn dialogue and interactive learning, where systems improve through iterative feedback cycles. A key contribution is a theoretical result showing that in shared-parameter models, learning from corrections that don't actually change executor behavior can *degrade* final performance if those corrections are sampled too frequently — motivating selective learning.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical insight**: Proof that naive learning from all feedback corrections can hurt performance in shared-parameter settings, with conditions for when corrections should be downsampled.
2. **Selective feedback mechanism**: A scoring rule that compares executor output with and without advisor guidance to identify corrections worth learning from — requires no executor likelihoods or extra rollouts.
3. **AdviSD algorithm**: Combines outcome-based RL with selective self-distillation from a feedback-conditioned advisor copy.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates 4.2–6.4 percentage-point gains over advisor-GRPO on BFCL-v3 and strong transfer to out-of-domain tasks and different model families (tested on Qwen3 advisors for Gemini and Claude executors).

## Connections to Other Work
This extends [[RLHF]] and recent work on **advisor models** (lightweight policies that guide frozen executors) by adding a principled learning-from-feedback loop. It relates to [[mechanistic-interpretability]] insofar as it helps understand which feedback signals matter for improving steering. The selective learning principle echoes ideas in **curriculum learning** and **active learning** — focusing on high-signal examples. The multi-turn dialogue setting connects to **interactive RL** and **in-context learning**, though the frozen executor constraint is more restrictive than full model fine-tuning.

## Practical Applications
1. **Cost-effective model steering for deployed LLMs**: Organizations can train lightweight advisors (often <8B parameters) to improve frontier models (Gemini, Claude, GPT-4) without re-training them.
2. **User-specific personalization**: Deploy per-user or per-domain advisors that learn from domain-specific feedback without modifying the base model.
3. **A/B testing and online learning**: Advisors can be rapidly updated in production based on user feedback, while the executor remains stable.
4. **Compliance and safety overlays**: Train advisors to steer models toward compliant outputs in regulated industries (finance, healthcare) without modifying the core model.
5. **Multi-language and cross-model translation**: Train advisors that adapt outputs from one model family to match another's style or constraints.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Advisor-as-a-Service**: SaaS platform where enterprises upload their domain data, train small advisors, and deploy them as middleware between their frontend and a frozen LLM (OpenAI, Anthropic APIs). Charge per API call or advisor update.
2. **Domain-Specific Model Wrappers**: Pre-built advisors for common verticals (legal document review, financial analysis, medical coding) that wrap public APIs, sold to enterprises or resellers.
3. **Feedback Loop Monetization**: Tools that collect user corrections in real time, automatically score them using AdviSD's selection rule, and continuously improve advisors — offered as a data/ML ops product.
4. **Model Arbitrage Platform**: Help companies use cheaper or open-source executors (Llama, Qwen) paired with trained advisors to match the quality of expensive frontier models, with transparent cost savings dashboards.
5. **Compliance & Audit Advisors**: Specialized advisors for regulated industries that continuously learn from human feedback to steer models away from risky outputs, with built-in audit trails and versioning for regulatory compliance.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Frozen executor assumption**: The method requires a frozen LLM; it cannot improve the executor itself. If the executor is fundamentally incapable of producing good outputs, an advisor cannot help.
2. **Reflection overhead**: Requires a separate "reflection" step to propose corrections, adding computational cost and assuming reliable reflection quality — the paper doesn't analyze reflection failures.
3. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The selective learning rule depends on tuning the threshold or sampling ratio for corrections; no principled guidance is provided for setting these in new domains.
4. **Generalization limits**: While the paper shows transfer to out-of-domain tasks, it doesn't test on radically different domains (e.g., advisor trained on math → deployed on creative writing); domain shift may degrade performance.
5. **Scalability to very large advisors**: Experiments use Qwen3-8B advisors; it's unclear if the benefits scale to larger advisors or multi-advisor ensembles, or if computational overhead becomes prohibitive.
6. **Evaluation on adversarial inputs**: No testing against adversarially crafted inputs or distribution shifts that might fool the advisor's selection heuristic.

## Open Questions
1. **What is the optimal correction sampling ratio?** Can we derive it from task properties or adapt it online rather than treating it as a fixed hyperparameter?
2. **How does advisor quality scale with executor size?** Do larger executors need larger advisors, or can tiny advisors still steer 70B+ parameter models?
3. **Can advisors transfer across model families without retraining?** Preliminary results suggest yes, but is there a principled way to predict when transfer will fail?
4. **How sensitive is the selection rule to reflection quality?** What happens if the reflection step itself is noisy or adversarial?
5. **Can this method scale to longer horizons or multi-step reasoning tasks?** Experiments focus on single-turn or short-context corrections.
6. **Is there a connection to reward hacking?** Could selectively learned advisors learn to exploit the selection heuristic rather than genuinely improve executor outputs?

## Key Takeaway
Small trainable advisors can guide frozen LLMs better by selectively learning from corrections that actually change outputs, avoiding the trap of learning from misleading feedback.
