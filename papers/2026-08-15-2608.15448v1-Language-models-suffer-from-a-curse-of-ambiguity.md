# Language models suffer from a curse of ambiguity

**Authors:** Nicolas Zucchet, Hyun Dong Lee, Scott Linderman
**Published:** 2026-08-15 | **arXiv:** [2608.15448v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.15448v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies a fundamental problem in how language models learn probability distributions over tokens: ambiguous distributions (where many tokens are plausible next-words) are much harder for neural networks to learn accurately. The authors trace this "curse of ambiguity" to concrete architectural factors—requiring more model capacity, larger embeddings, longer training, and amplifying sampling noise—and validate their findings on both synthetic tasks and real language models. This matters because modern LLMs increasingly rely on sampling from these learned distributions, so getting them right is critical for both accuracy and reliability.

## Theoretical Background
Language models predict the next token by learning a probability distribution over a fixed vocabulary. The paper builds on foundational work in statistical learning theory and neural network expressivity, particularly how neural networks represent discrete probability distributions. When a distribution is "ambiguous" (high entropy—many tokens have similar probability), representing it precisely requires the network to store more information. The authors connect this to classical results on neural network capacity and generalization, extending ideas from approximation theory to the discrete probability setting. Prior work on distribution learning in neural networks (e.g., via log-loss or cross-entropy minimization) forms the implicit baseline, though the paper's specific focus on *ambiguity* as a learnable challenge appears novel.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formalization of a curse of ambiguity**: The paper provides both theoretical analysis and empirical validation that entropy (or ambiguity) of a target distribution directly increases learning difficulty for neural networks.
2. **Mechanistic decomposition**: Rather than treating this as an abstract statistical problem, the authors trace the curse to four concrete bottlenecks:
   - Model capacity constraints (ambiguous distributions need more latent capacity)
   - Embedding dimensionality requirements (higher-entropy distributions need larger embeddings to separate tokens)
   - Optimization dynamics (more steps needed to converge on ambiguous targets)
   - Sampling noise amplification (high-entropy distributions are more sensitive to sampling variance)
3. **Empirical validation on language models**: Demonstrating that real LLMs exhibit these signatures, not just toy neural networks.
4. **Practical framework**: A method to assess when to trust an LLM's output distribution—e.g., when ambiguity is low, sampling should be more reliable.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several threads:
- **[[Mechanistic interpretability]]** of language models: understanding *why* models fail at specific tasks by examining the distributions they learn.
- **Neural scaling laws** and **loss curves**: extends understanding of what makes certain objectives harder to optimize.
- **Sampling and decoding strategies**: complements work on top-k sampling, beam search, and temperature tuning—all of which are affected by distribution fidelity.
- **Calibration and uncertainty** in neural networks: connects to work on whether models know what they don't know.
- **RLHF** and distribution matching: any approach that relies on matching learned distributions (e.g., policy distillation, reward modeling) is affected by this curse.

## Practical Applications
1. **Safer LLM deployment**: Flag low-confidence predictions when ambiguity is high, or defer to human review rather than sampling blindly.
2. **Curriculum learning for LLMs**: Train on unambiguous examples first, then gradually expose models to harder (higher-entropy) distributions—analogous to easy-to-hard curriculum design.
3. **Model scaling decisions**: Use entropy profiling on downstream tasks to determine whether to allocate more parameters vs. training data.
4. **Decoding strategy selection**: Automatically select between greedy decoding (best for low-ambiguity regions) and sampling (with adaptive temperature) based on measured distribution entropy.
5. **Knowledge distillation**: When distilling a large LLM, prioritize ambiguous regions where the student model will struggle most, allocating more training capacity there.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Ambiguity-aware LLM monitoring tool**: SaaS product that profiles LLM outputs in production, flags high-ambiguity predictions, and alerts teams to regions where the model's sampling may be unreliable. Integrate with observability platforms.
2. **Entropy-optimized fine-tuning service**: Automated service that analyzes a customer's domain data, identifies high-ambiguity regions, and applies targeted fine-tuning with larger models or curriculum strategies to improve reliability.
3. **Smart decoding middleware**: A lightweight inference layer that measures token distribution entropy in real-time and switches decoding strategies (temperature, top-k, beam width) to maximize quality on hard decisions.
4. **LLM quality audit platform**: Help enterprises characterize the statistical reliability of their internal LLMs by profiling which domains/tasks have ambiguous distributions, enabling risk-aware deployment policies.
5. **Synthetic data generation for ambiguous cases**: Tool that identifies where LLMs struggle (high-entropy regions) and automatically synthesizes hard examples to finetune on, reducing the curse through targeted data augmentation.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational cost of profiling**: Measuring entropy on real distributions requires sufficient samples; the cost of characterizing ambiguity may be non-trivial at scale.
2. **Causality vs. correlation**: While the paper shows ambiguity *correlates* with learning difficulty, some of the mechanistic claims (e.g., "capacity is the bottleneck") rely on ablations that may not fully isolate causal factors.
3. **Synthetic vs. real data**: The controlled experiments validate the theory, but real language data has rich structure (context, semantics) that may confound the pure information-theoretic story.
4. **No solution, only diagnosis**: The paper identifies the problem but doesn't propose a general algorithmic fix. Practitioners are left with the insight but limited actionable remedies beyond inference-time strategies.
5. **Token-level focus**: The curse is analyzed at the single-token level; in practice, longer-horizon dependencies and context windows may distribute ambiguity in ways not fully captured here.

## Open Questions
1. **Can we design architectures that are inherently robust to ambiguity?** E.g., using mixture-of-experts or adaptive capacity that scales with entropy?
2. **How does the curse interact with context?** Is ambiguity local (single token) or global (depends on the sequence so far)? Can longer context reduce ambiguity and thus make learning easier?
3. **What is the optimal training strategy for high-ambiguity distributions?** Should we use special losses, weighting schemes, or architecture modifications rather than standard cross-entropy?
4. **Does the curse manifest differently in other modalities** (images, audio) or other discrete prediction tasks (classification, machine translation)?
5. **Can we predict ambiguity before training?** A method to characterize task difficulty (entropy) from the raw data alone would be valuable for curriculum design.
6. **How do scaling laws change in the presence of ambiguity?** Does compute follow different scaling laws for high-vs.-low-entropy objectives?

## Key Takeaway
Language models struggle to learn ambiguous probability distributions, so high-entropy predictions should be treated with skepticism.
