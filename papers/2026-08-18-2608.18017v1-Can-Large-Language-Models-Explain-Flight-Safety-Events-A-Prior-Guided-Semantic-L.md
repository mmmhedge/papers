# Can Large Language Models Explain Flight Safety Events? A Prior-Guided Semantic LLM-based Approach

**Authors:** Lu Xu, Xu Li, Linjiang Zheng, Fan Li, Riquan Zhang et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18017v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18017v1)

## Summary
This paper presents FlightLLM, a system that uses large language models to explain why flight safety incidents occur by converting numerical flight data into natural language descriptions. The key innovation is combining LLMs with domain knowledge and statistical classifiers to generate both accurate predictions and human-understandable explanations of dangerous flight events like hard landings.

## Theoretical Background
The work bridges two distinct areas: **explainable AI** (techniques that make model decisions interpretable to humans) and **domain-specific language processing**. LLMs excel at reasoning over text but struggle with numerical data and specialized knowledge. The paper builds on the idea that converting raw sensor data into qualitative descriptions makes it more amenable to language-based reasoning. It also leverages **few-shot learning** (teaching models with minimal labeled examples) and the principle that auxiliary models (like CatBoost, a gradient boosting classifier) can provide "prior guidance" to steer LLM reasoning toward correct conclusions.

## Core Contributions
1. **Modal bridging**: A semantic discretization module that transforms raw numerical flight metrics into qualitative linguistic descriptions (e.g., "descent rate exceeded normal threshold").
2. **Hybrid classification**: Integration of CatBoost predictions as prompt-injected priors to compensate for LLMs' weak classification abilities.
3. **Contrastive few-shot strategy**: A data-efficient fine-tuning approach designed for domains with scarce labeled examples (704 samples).
4. **Structured prompting with aviation knowledge**: Domain-specific prompt templates that embed pilot behavior concepts and causal relationships directly into inference.
5. **End-to-end evaluation**: Demonstration on real A320 aircraft hard landing data showing competitive classification + human-readable causal explanations.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to several established areas:
- [[Prompt Engineering]] and [[In-Context Learning]]: Uses structured prompts to embed domain knowledge without full fine-tuning.
- [[Explainable AI (XAI)]]: Moves beyond black-box feature importance to generate natural-language explanations.
- [[Few-Shot Learning]]: Addresses data scarcity, a persistent challenge in safety-critical domains.
- [[Hybrid AI Systems]]: Combines symbolic domain knowledge (CatBoost as "expert") with neural language models.
- Domain-specific LLM applications (medical diagnosis, legal reasoning) face similar challenges of modal inconsistency and knowledge scarcity.

## Practical Applications
1. **Airline safety operations**: Flight safety teams can use FlightLLM to rapidly investigate incidents and identify systemic pilot behavior patterns without manual report analysis.
2. **Predictive maintenance and crew training**: Explanations highlight which control behaviors trigger risk events, enabling targeted pilot retraining programs.
3. **Regulatory compliance**: Automated generation of causal narratives for aviation safety databases (e.g., NTSB) reduces manual documentation burden.
4. **Insurance and liability**: Clear explanations support underwriting decisions and accident investigation in aviation insurance claims.
5. **Real-time cockpit alerts**: Embedded systems could provide LLM-generated warnings ("You are approaching hard landing profile; reduce descent rate") during flight operations.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **FlightSafe Analytics**: SaaS platform for airlines to upload flight data and receive AI-generated safety reports with pilot-actionable recommendations and root cause narratives.
2. **Predictive Crew Training Engine**: B2B software that identifies pilots and crews at risk for specific unsafe behaviors and generates personalized training curricula based on LLM explanations.
3. **Autonomous Flight Auditor**: Enterprise tool for regulators (FAA, EASA) to automatically audit thousands of flight records and flag systematic safety violations with natural-language justifications.
4. **Insurance Underwriting Assistant**: Specialized LLM for aviation insurers that auto-generates liability assessment reports and risk profiles from incident data.
5. **Flight Safety Consulting API**: White-label API for MRO (Maintenance, Repair, Overhaul) providers to integrate interpretable safety analysis into their digital twins and predictive maintenance platforms.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Generalization across aircraft types**: Trained on A320 data; unclear whether semantic descriptors and knowledge embeddings transfer to other aircraft with different flight physics.
2. **Data quality dependency**: Assumes reliable onboard flight recording; garbage sensor data or missing fields could produce nonsensical causal narratives without robust error handling.
3. **Explanation validity**: LLM-generated explanations may sound plausible but lack formal verification against true causal mechanisms in complex flight dynamics; no ground truth for "correct" explanation.
4. **Prompt brittleness**: Structured prompts are manually engineered; sensitivity to prompt phrasing not thoroughly explored; may fail on edge cases outside training distribution.
5. **Computational cost**: Real-time inference on streaming flight data may be expensive; latency for safety-critical decisions not discussed.
6. **Limited scope**: Tested only on hard landing detection; unclear whether approach generalizes to other safety events with different causal structures (e.g., stall, wind shear).

## Open Questions
1. How robust are explanations to perturbations in input features? Do small sensor noise changes produce contradictory causal narratives?
2. Can the semantic discretization module be learned end-to-end rather than hand-engineered, and would that improve generalization?
3. How do explanations compare to domain expert interpretations? Is there a systematic way to validate LLM explanations against pilot ground truth?
4. Does the approach scale to real-time streaming data or very large datasets (millions of flights)?
5. How sensitive is performance to the choice of CatBoost prior? What happens if the prior is wrong or misleading?
6. Can the same framework work for rare, catastrophic events where labeled data is even scarcer (e.g., accidents rather than incidents)?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs can explain aviation safety incidents by converting sensor data to text and combining predictions from statistical classifiers with domain knowledge prompts.
