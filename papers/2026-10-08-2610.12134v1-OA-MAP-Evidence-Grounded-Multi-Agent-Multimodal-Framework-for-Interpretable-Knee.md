# OA-MAP: Evidence-Grounded Multi-Agent Multimodal Framework for Interpretable Knee Osteoarthritis Progression

**Authors:** Sixu Chen, Mingrui Yang, Qiang Guan, Xiaojuan Li
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.12134v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12134v1)

## Summary
This paper presents OA-MAP, a multi-agent AI system for predicting knee osteoarthritis progression using medical imaging (MRI, X-ray) and clinical data. The system combines specialized agents with explainability features—showing evidence, uncertainty estimates, and cross-modal conflicts—to help clinicians monitor patient disease progression with transparent, interpretable predictions rather than black-box risk scores.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on several convergent areas: (1) **multimodal fusion** in medical AI — combining different data types (imaging, clinical measurements) to improve predictions; (2) **multi-agent systems** — autonomous agents that collaborate by recruiting specialists and delegating tasks; (3) **interpretability and explainability in healthcare** — the principle that high-stakes medical decisions require transparent reasoning, not just accuracy; and (4) **human-in-the-loop machine learning** — frameworks where humans can inspect, validate, and correct AI decisions at intermediate stages. The paper applies these ideas to osteoarthritis progression, which requires integrating structural changes (visible on imaging) and subjective pain reports.

## Core Contributions
- **Architecture for evidence-grounded prediction**: Unlike standard neural networks, OA-MAP explicitly retrieves and displays supporting literature and intermediate findings alongside predictions, making reasoning traceable.
- **Modality-specific specialist agents**: Separate agents for MRI, X-ray, and clinical assessments that can be selectively invoked based on available data, rather than forcing all modalities through a single model.
- **Uncertainty-informed correction loop**: Clinicians can flag uncertain predictions, triggering automatic recomputation of downstream results — a concrete implementation of human oversight in medical AI.
- **Empirical validation on real cohort**: Evaluation on 600 participants from FNIH Consortium with strong performance on structural progression (AUROC 0.80) but lower on pain (0.68), highlighting the harder problem of subjective outcomes.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper sits at the intersection of:

The gap it addresses: most osteoarthritis prediction work either treats imaging and clinical data separately or fuses them opaquely; OA-MAP adds transparency and human oversight.

## Practical Applications
1. **Rheumatology clinic workflows**: Automated progression monitoring for patients in regular follow-up; alerts clinicians to high-risk cases with supporting evidence for triage decisions.
2. **Clinical trials for osteoarthritis therapeutics**: Automated cohort stratification and progression scoring to reduce manual image reading and enable faster trial execution.
3. **Remote patient monitoring**: Enables non-specialists (nurses, primary-care providers) to review OA status using AI assistance with explanations, reducing need for rheumatology referral.
4. **Longitudinal registry systems**: Hospital networks tracking large osteoarthritis populations can use OA-MAP to flag discordant imaging/clinical findings and prompt clinician review.
5. **Patient-facing apps**: Transparent predictions with evidence help patients understand disease trajectory and engage in shared decision-making about treatment.

## Product & Startup Ideas

## Technical Limitations
- **Imbalanced outcome performance**: Pain progression prediction (AUROC 0.68) substantially lags structural progression (0.80), suggesting pain — a subjective, multifactorial outcome — is harder to predict from imaging alone. The paper doesn't address whether adding patient-reported outcomes improves this.
- **Cohort and generalization**: 600 participants from a single consortium is modest; performance on other populations (different age, ethnicity, imaging protocols) is unknown.
- **Evidence retrieval quality**: The paper doesn't detail how literature is retrieved or whether outdated/contradictory citations are handled. LLM-based retrieval can hallucinate or overfit to training data.
- **Computational cost and latency**: No runtime analysis; multi-agent orchestration + literature retrieval likely slower than end-to-end inference, risking clinical workflow friction.
- **Human-in-the-loop scalability**: The correction loop requires clinician review; unclear how this scales to thousands of cases or whether corrections are consistently applied across similar cases.
- **Multimodal missing data**: Unclear how the system behaves when one modality (e.g., MRI) is unavailable; robustness to partial data not discussed.

## Open Questions
1. **Longitudinal generalization**: Does OA-MAP trained on one time-series predict progression in external cohorts with different follow-up intervals or imaging equipment?
2. **Subgroup performance**: Do prediction accuracies differ by age, BMI, disease stage, or treatment? (Fairness and stratification in medical AI.)
3. **Correction impact**: How often do clinician corrections change predictions for similar future cases? Is there a learning loop, or are corrections siloed?
4. **Literature grounding effectiveness**: Does showing retrieved literature actually improve clinician trust and decision-making vs. standard risk scores? (Requires a prospective clinical trial.)
5. **Pain prediction improvement**: Can the paper achieve higher AUCs for pain by incorporating patient questionnaires (KOOS, WOMAC) rather than relying solely on imaging?
6. **Real-time integration**: What is the latency and cost of deploying OA-MAP in a busy radiology department, and does it fit existing EMR/PACS workflows?

## Key Takeaway
Multi-agent AI system predicts knee arthritis progression with explainable evidence and human oversight, improving transparency over black-box medical predictions.
