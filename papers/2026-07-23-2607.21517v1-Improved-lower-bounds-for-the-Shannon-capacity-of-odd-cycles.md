# Improved lower bounds for the Shannon capacity of odd cycles

**Authors:** Nathaniel Itty, Christopher D. Rosin, Chase Carstensen, Daniel Reichman
**Published:** 2026-07-23 | **arXiv:** [2607.21517v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21517v1)

## Summary
This paper uses large language models to discover improved lower bounds for the Shannon capacity of odd cycles—a fundamental problem in information theory that asks: what's the maximum rate at which we can send information error-free over a noisy channel? By constructing explicit independent sets in graph strong powers through iterative LLM interactions, the authors tighten decades-old bounds for cycles with 7, 11, and 13 vertices, demonstrating that LLMs can help find hard-to-spot combinatorial structures that humans struggle to construct by hand.

## Theoretical Background
The Shannon capacity of a graph—introduced by Claude Shannon—measures information transmission reliability without errors over channels where some symbol confusions occur. Mathematically, it's lower bounded by the independence number (largest set of non-adjacent vertices) of the graph's strong power $G^d$, raised to the power $1/d$. For odd cycles like $C_7$ (a 7-vertex loop), computing exact Shannon capacity is a notoriously difficult open problem in extremal combinatorics; researchers have labored for decades to tighten bounds by finding larger and larger independent sets in these strong powers—essentially finding clever subsets of vertices with special non-interference properties.

## Core Contributions
1. **Explicit constructions** in strong powers of odd cycles: Independent sets of sizes 134753 in $C_7^{10}$, 21909 in $C_{11}^6$, and 62530 in $C_{13}^6$—substantially larger than previously known.
2. **Improved Shannon capacity lower bounds**: $\Theta(C_7) \geq 3.258020$, $\Theta(C_{11}) \geq 5.289773$, $\Theta(C_{13}) \geq 6.300109$.
3. **LLM-assisted discovery methodology**: Demonstrating that iterative human–LLM dialogue can systematically find complex combinatorial structures that evade manual search.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of classical extremal graph theory (the study of Shannon capacity stretches back to Shannon's 1956 paper) and modern AI-assisted mathematics. It relates to broader efforts in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] and [[optimization]] where researchers explore whether language models can serve as reasoning engines for symbolic, discrete problems. Unlike neural network approaches to graph problems ([[graph-neural-networks]]), this uses LLMs not as learners but as guided constructive tools—a novel use case.

## Practical Applications
- **Coding theory & telecommunications**: Designing error-correcting codes for practical communication systems; tighter Shannon capacity bounds inform theoretical limits on real channel coding schemes.
- **Constraint satisfaction problems**: Independent set construction techniques transfer to scheduling, resource allocation, and frequency assignment in wireless networks.
- **Information-theoretic security**: Channel capacity bounds underpin security analysis of communication protocols.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scalability**: The constructions are hand-tuned to specific cycle sizes; unclear how the LLM method generalizes to arbitrary large graphs.
- **Lack of proof of optimality**: The paper finds good independent sets but does not prove they are maximal or near-optimal; the true Shannon capacity may still be unknown.
- **Limited theoretical insight**: While the bounds are improved, the paper does not explain *why* these constructions work or provide transferable principles—it's primarily an engineering result, not a conceptual advance.
- **Computational cost**: No analysis of LLM query cost or computational overhead; feasibility for larger instances unclear.
- **Graph family specificity**: Results are narrowly tailored to odd cycles; applicability to other graph families unstudied.

## Open Questions
1. **Generalization**: Can the LLM construction method yield improvements for other hard graph families (higher cycles, circulant graphs, random graphs)?
2. **Proof guidance**: Can LLMs also generate human-readable *proofs* or invariants that explain why an independent set works, making results more insightful?
3. **Optimality gaps**: How close are these bounds to the true Shannon capacity? New upper bounds would tighten the interval.
4. **Asymptotic scaling**: Do the improved bounds reveal asymptotic formulas (e.g., $\Theta(C_n) \sim f(n)$ for large odd $n$)?
5. **Hybrid methods**: What combination of traditional optimization (integer programming, SAT solvers) and LLM guidance yields the best results?

## Key Takeaway
Large language models can discover intricate combinatorial structures, improving decades-old information-theoretic bounds through guided search—demonstrating AI's utility beyond learning from data.
