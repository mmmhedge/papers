# A Taxonomy of Cognitive Capability Gaps in Generative and Agentic AI

**Authors:** Taye Akinrele, Sindhuja Penchala, Noorbakhsh Amiri Golilarz, Sudip Mittal, Shahram Rahimi
**Published:** 2026-08-03 | **arXiv:** [2608.02553v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02553v1)

## Summary
This paper surveys the gap between current generative and agentic AI systems and what "Cognitive AI" would require — systems that reason over long horizons, adapt their behavior, remember context, and regulate themselves. Rather than proposing a single solution, the authors map out *where* these systems fall short across five key dimensions (memory, goal-autonomy, self-monitoring, environment interaction, learning) and sketch an architectural vision called ACIA to guide future work toward more capable, reliable AI.

## Theoretical Background
Current AI systems excel at pattern matching and token generation (large language models) or task execution in narrow domains (agentic AI), but they lack sustained reasoning and adaptation. The paper builds on prior work distinguishing between narrow task performance and broader "cognitive" abilities — things humans do naturally like maintaining consistent goals over time, noticing when they're failing, and learning from mistakes. It draws on concepts from cognitive science, reinforcement learning (learning from trial and error), and systems architecture. The framing assumes that true AI capability requires not just one skill but an integrated system with persistent memory, self-awareness, and the ability to update strategies.

## Core Contributions
1. **A structured taxonomy** organizing cognitive gaps across five specific dimensions (persistent state, goal-directed autonomy, self-monitoring, environment interaction, learning/adaptation) — moves beyond vague complaints to a systematic framework.
2. **The ACIA conceptual architecture** — a reference design showing how these capabilities might integrate, not just exist in isolation.
3. **A survey of recent advances and recurring limitations** for each dimension — documents what researchers have tried and where they still struggle.
4. **Cognition-centric evaluation framework** — proposes how to measure whether an AI system is actually improving on these dimensions, not just on benchmark accuracy.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Large Language Models]] (which lack persistent reasoning), [[Reinforcement Learning]] (which addresses goal-directed behavior and adaptation), and [[Multi-Agent Systems]] (which face similar challenges in coordination and learning). It relates to broader discussions of [[Alignment]] in that reliably self-regulating systems are safer. The emphasis on persistent memory connects to work on retrieval-augmented generation and continual learning. The ACIA architecture echoes older AI systems research (cognitive architectures like SOAR or CLARION) applied to modern deep learning.

## Practical Applications
- **Autonomous control systems** (robotics, autonomous vehicles) that must adapt to novel situations and learn from failure without retraining.
- **Long-horizon planning agents** in industrial automation that track multiple subgoals, detect drift from objectives, and adjust strategy mid-execution.
- **Financial AI systems** that maintain consistent decision rules, monitor for market regime changes, and avoid catastrophic errors through self-correction.
- **Scientific research assistants** that sustain hypotheses over multi-step reasoning, spot contradictions in their own outputs, and iteratively refine models.
- **Healthcare decision-support** systems that track patient state over time, recognize when confidence is low, and escalate appropriately.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Cognitive AI benchmark suite** — build commercial evaluation tools and datasets that let enterprises measure whether their AI actually learns and adapts over time (licensing to AI labs, large tech companies).
2. **Self-regulating AI middleware** — a software layer that wraps existing LLMs and agentic systems to add persistent memory, goal tracking, and self-monitoring, sold as SaaS to enterprises deploying long-horizon automations.
3. **Continual learning platform for robotics** — commercial system for industrial robots that can adapt to new tasks and environments without full retraining, targeting manufacturing and logistics.
4. **Long-context reasoning IDE for enterprises** — development environment specifically designed to help teams build and debug AI systems that maintain consistent state and goals across long interactions.
5. **Cognitive AI consulting & architecture services** — help organizations apply the ACIA framework to design more reliable autonomous systems, positioning against AI safety consultancies.

## Technical Limitations
- **The taxonomy is descriptive, not prescriptive** — it identifies gaps but doesn't propose concrete solutions or novel algorithms. Implementation details remain unclear.
- **No evidence that integrating all five dimensions is tractable** — the paper doesn't show whether a system can excel in all five simultaneously or if there are inherent trade-offs.
- **Evaluation framework is sketched, not validated** — measuring "self-monitoring" or "adaptive behavior" operationally is hard; the paper identifies this but doesn't solve it.
- **Assumes a single integrated architecture is the right approach** — may be that modular or ensemble methods work better in practice.
- **Limited discussion of computational cost** — systems with persistent memory, self-monitoring, and continual learning are expensive; no analysis of practical scalability trade-offs.
- **Assumes reward/goal stability** — in dynamic environments where objectives themselves shift, the notion of "goal-directed autonomy" may be incomplete.

## Open Questions
- **Can we build a single system excelling in all five dimensions?** Or do we need specialized sub-systems that coordinate? What's the optimal modular design?
- **How do we measure self-monitoring without ground truth?** What metrics or experiments would conclusively show an AI system is actually catching its own errors?
- **What is the computational budget?** How much slower/more expensive does a cognitively capable system need to be compared to narrow models?
- **How do these capabilities degrade in novel environments?** The paper focuses on adaptation, but what happens in truly out-of-distribution scenarios?
- **Can we induce these capabilities through scaling alone** (bigger models, more data) or do we need architectural changes?
- **How does ACIA compare empirically to other proposed architectures** (e.g., neuro-symbolic systems, world models)?

## Key Takeaway
Generative and agentic AI lack integrated reasoning, memory, and self-correction needed for reliable long-horizon autonomy.
