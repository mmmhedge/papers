# Effective Dense Retrieval using Only In-Context Examples

**Authors:** Nour Jedidi, Abdul Basit Ali, Hang Li, Jimmy Lin
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38099v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38099v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that large language models can be turned into effective dense retrievers—systems that find relevant documents by comparing numerical representations—without any training, using only a few example queries and documents as context. The method, called RICE, works by prompting the LLM with in-context examples that teach it how to encode both queries and documents consistently, making it practical for teams that can't afford to fine-tune models.

## Theoretical Background
Dense retrieval is the problem of finding relevant documents for a query by converting both into high-dimensional numerical vectors ("embeddings") and measuring their similarity. Traditional approaches required training specialized retriever models on labeled query-document pairs. Recent work has explored using [[Large Language Models]] directly as retrievers by extracting their internal representations, but these "prompt-based" embeddings often underperform trained retrievers. In-context learning—the ability of LLMs to adapt behavior based on examples in the input prompt—has emerged as a powerful alternative to fine-tuning, enabling task adaptation without gradient updates.

## Core Contributions
1. **RICE framework**: A training-free method that extracts dense representations from decoder-only LLMs by conditioning on in-context examples that provide shared encoding context for queries and documents.
2. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates that RICE embeddings substantially outperform naive prompt-based LLM embeddings on retrieval benchmarks.
3. **Practical accessibility**: Shows that effective dense retrieval is possible without training, lowering the barrier for practitioners without GPU resources or labeled data.

## Connections to Other Work
RICE builds on recent trends in prompt-based retrieval and in-context learning (e.g., [[In-Context Learning in Large Language Models]]). It contrasts with supervised dense retrieval approaches ([[DPR]], [[ColBERT]]) that require labeled training data and gradient-based optimization. The work also relates to broader research on using LLMs as zero-shot or few-shot tools, and touches on the question of what linguistic or conceptual knowledge LLMs implicitly encode in their representations.

## Practical Applications
- **Small-team RAG systems**: Teams building retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) applications without labeled data can use RICE immediately without fine-tuning infrastructure.
- **Cross-lingual retrieval**: In-context examples could help LLMs adapt to languages with less training data without retraining.
- **Domain-specific search**: Quickly deploying retrievers for niche domains (e.g., legal documents, scientific papers) by providing domain examples in context.
- **Cost reduction**: Companies running inference-only systems avoid the computational and engineering overhead of training custom retrievers.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **No-code retrieval service**: SaaS platform where users upload documents and provide 5–10 examples of good query-document pairs; RICE automatically indexes and serves a retrieval API without training.
2. **Multi-domain RAG agent**: AI assistant that switches between different retrieval behaviors (customer support vs. technical docs) by swapping in-context examples at inference time.
3. **Dynamic knowledge base adapter**: Enterprise tool that lets domain experts improve retrieval on internal documents by simply adding exemplar pairs, no ML team required.
4. **Retrieval analytics dashboard**: Monitoring product that detects when retrieval quality degrades and recommends new in-context examples to re-prompt the LLM.
5. **Lightweight embedding API**: Inference service that returns dense vectors from any LLM without training, targeting teams building semantic search features on a budget.

## Technical Limitations
- **LLM-dependent quality**: Representation quality is bounded by the base LLM's capabilities; weak models won't produce useful embeddings regardless of examples.
- **Example sensitivity**: Performance likely depends on choosing representative in-context examples; no systematic method for selecting or ranking them is discussed.
- **Scalability unclear**: Computational cost of encoding millions of documents (inference through an LLM for each) compared to small, trained retrievers is not addressed.
- **Limited evaluation scope**: Results are on standard benchmarks; generalization to very different domains or languages is unknown.
- **Stability**: In-context learning is known to be sensitive to prompt wording and example order; robustness analysis is absent.

## Open Questions
- How many in-context examples are truly needed? Is there a diminishing returns curve, and can it be predicted?
- Can in-context examples be automatically selected or optimized, rather than manually chosen?
- How does RICE scale to retrieving from billions of documents in latency-critical settings?
- Does performance degrade when the in-context examples are misaligned with the test distribution?
- Can RICE handle multi-modal retrieval (images + text) or graph-structured documents?
- How do different base LLMs (open vs. closed, different sizes) affect the quality of RICE embeddings?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs can become effective document retrievers through in-context learning alone, eliminating the need for expensive training and making retrieval accessible to resource-constrained teams.
