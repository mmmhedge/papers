# Herding, Momentum, and Reversal in China's A-Share Market: An Agent-Based Network Model with Information Diffusion

**Authors:** Jiahao Weng
**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **arXiv:** [2607.27063v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27063v1)

## Summary
This paper builds an agent-based model of stock markets where individual investors influence each other locally (through social networks) and learn about prices at different speeds (information travels slowly). The model explains why stocks show momentum (prices keep moving in one direction) followed by reversals (sudden direction changes), and validates these patterns in China's A-share market using statistical measures of investor herding.

## Theoretical Background
Agent-based models (ABMs) simulate markets by modeling many individual traders with heterogeneous beliefs and local interactions, rather than assuming all traders are rational and have instant information. This paper combines two key mechanisms: (1) **herding** — the tendency of investors to imitate neighbors' trading decisions through social networks (modeled using lattices and complex network topologies like Watts-Strogatz); (2) **information diffusion** — the realistic assumption that news spreads gradually through markets rather than instantly. The paper draws on behavioral finance (which studies how psychology drives trading) and network theory (which models how connections between agents create emergent patterns). Traditional financial models assume prices reflect all available information instantly; this work relaxes that assumption to explain momentum and reversal — patterns where prices overshoot then correct.

## Core Contributions
1. **Disentangles two sources of price movement**: separates behavioral imitation (herding) from delayed information arrival, showing each contributes differently to momentum and reversal.
2. **Network topology matters**: demonstrates that the structure of investor connections (von Neumann lattices vs. small-world networks) produces distinct trading patterns and price statistics (kurtosis, clustering).
3. **Novel herding measurement**: introduces a rolling tail-based herding indicator using Johnson transformation, validated against standard cross-sectional absolute deviation (CSAD) measures on real Chinese market data.
4. **Empirical grounding in A-shares**: applies the model to China's A-share market, a less-studied but highly liquid market with distinct microstructure.

## Connections to Other Work
This extends the agent-based modeling tradition (e.g., [[Lux-Marchesi models]], [[Brock-Hommes heterogeneous beliefs]]) by explicitly modeling information speed alongside behavioral imitation. It relates to [[market-microstructure]] research on how trading volume and order flow create momentum (e.g., momentum from delayed price discovery) and to [[behavioral finance]] work on herding as an explanation for bubbles and crashes. The use of complex networks (Watts-Strogatz, Erdős-Rényi) connects to [[graph neural networks]] approaches in finance, though applied here analytically rather than as a learning architecture. The empirical validation using cross-sectional dispersion (CSAD/LSV measures) bridges classical econometric herding detection with agent-based simulation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Market surveillance**: Regulators and exchanges can use the rolling tail-based herding indicator to detect regime shifts and potential instability during stress periods (validated here on major Chinese market disruptions).
2. **Risk management for market-makers**: Understanding how local clustering of trades builds up helps market-makers model liquidity provision and inventory risk in decentralized trading venues.
3. **Trading strategy calibration**: Momentum and mean-reversion strategies can be tuned using real-time herding measures — stronger herding signals stronger momentum, weaker herding signals earlier reversals.
4. **Central bank communication**: Identifying when information diffusion is slow allows policymakers to time announcements to avoid herding-driven overshoots.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Herding Dashboard**: Real-time visualization of network-based herding metrics across stocks/sectors, with alerts when clustering suggests imminent reversals — sold to quant hedge funds and prop traders.
2. **Information Diffusion Optimizer**: A service that predicts how fast news will propagate through a specific market (calibrated by network topology), helping institutional investors time execution to minimize market impact from delayed discovery.
3. **Agent-Based Backtesting Engine**: Backtesting platform that simulates trading strategies under different herding/information regimes, allowing traders to stress-test performance across market microstructure scenarios.
4. **Behavioral Risk Meter**: SaaS tool for asset managers measuring tail-based herding on their portfolio holdings, with early-warning signals for when momentum will reverse (reducing drawdowns).
5. **Market Topology Analyzer**: Consulting service that reconstructs investor network graphs from order flow and transaction data, identifying systemic clustering and contagion risk in emerging markets.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Stylized beliefs**: Investor beliefs are Gaussian with exogenous variance — real beliefs may be multi-modal, self-reinforcing, or driven by sentiment that persists beyond information arrival.
2. **Fixed network topology**: The lattices and random networks are static; real market structure evolves (new traders enter, exit; relationships change with regime). Dynamic network learning is not addressed.
3. **No endogenous information**: The "signal" that information diffuses is exogenous; the model doesn't explain what creates true price signals or how they emerge from order flow (a core chicken-egg problem in microstructure).
4. **Parameter sensitivity**: Results depend heavily on herding strength, diffusion speed, and network structure — the paper shows robustness across network topologies but doesn't fully characterize sensitivity to behavioral parameters.
5. **A-shares specificity**: Validation is only on one market; generalization to US/European markets (with different trading venues, regulatory regimes, retail participation) is untested.
6. **Aggregation mismatch**: Simulations model discrete investor choice (buy/sell/hold); empirical tests use continuous return measures — the mapping between micro-level choices and macro price formation is assumed, not derived.

## Open Questions
1. **Can the model predict reversals in real time?** Validation here is ex-post correlation; can the herding indicator forecast the timing and magnitude of reversals out-of-sample?
2. **How sensitive are results to belief heterogeneity?** All investors form Gaussian beliefs; what if beliefs cluster into a few modes (e.g., bull vs. bear camps), or if belief variance is state-dependent?
3. **Does the network structure adapt endogenously?** In reality, traders cluster around shared information sources (analysts, news feeds). Can the model incorporate feedback from trading success to network formation?
4. **What is the role of high-frequency trading and order-flow toxicity?** The model has no distinction between retail and algorithmic traders; how does latency-driven order flow change herding patterns?
5. **Quantitative calibration to real networks**: How can the "true" investor network topology be inferred from market data, and how much does estimation error in network structure affect predictions?

## Key Takeaway
Local investor clustering and slow information spread together explain why prices overshoot then reverse; herding strength determines magnitude, diffusion speed determines timing.
