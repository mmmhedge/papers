# RECAST: Learning to Compute the Right Context through Adaptive Evidence Routing

**Authors:** Yilun Hao, Krishna Sayana, Isabella Ye, James S Ren, Sukhdeep Sodhi et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10507v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10507v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces RECAST, a framework that teaches language models to actively construct evidence by deciding *how* to process information rather than just retrieving it. Instead of passively finding relevant documents, a learned router iteratively selects operations (filtering, aggregation, computation) to derive the answer, working over heterogeneous data sources. The system outperforms standard retrieval-augmented generation by 16% on average and generalizes well to unseen tasks.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on [[Retrieval-Augmented Generation]] (RAG), which augments language models with external knowledge to reduce hallucination. Conventional RAG uses fixed similarity-based retrieval — you query a database and get the most similar items. Agentic variants add tool-use and query adaptation, but remain fundamentally retrieval-centric: they still assume the evidence exists as-is in some source.

RECAST reframes the problem as a sequential decision process — a Markov Decision Process where the router must choose actions (retrieve, filter, aggregate, compute) to build evidence step-by-step. This draws on reinforcement learning (specifically [[policy gradient methods]] like group relative policy optimization, or GRPO) to train the router to make these decisions without explicit supervision for every intermediate step.

The three-component architecture (RouterLM, CompilerLM, AnswerLM) is a form of modular reasoning: a small trainable model (the router) orchestrates fixed, larger models to decompose a hard problem.

## Core Contributions
1. **Problem formulation**: Reframes evidence construction from retrieval-as-lookup to evidence-as-computation — the key insight that some answers require deriving facts by combining, filtering, or computing over multiple sources.

2. **RECAST architecture**: A learned routing framework where a lightweight RouterLM iteratively selects primitive operations (e.g., "filter documents by date") or requests a CompilerLM to execute custom operations (e.g., "compute the average of column X"). The router stops when it deems evidence sufficient.

3. **Training methodology**: Combines supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with group relative policy optimization (GRPO) to train the router end-to-end without requiring oracle intermediate steps.

4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates 75.6% success across six heterogeneous benchmarks (a 15.9% improvement over baselines) and strong zero-shot generalization to held-out tasks. A 9B fine-tuned model outperforms a larger frozen 35B baseline.

## Connections to Other Work
- **[[Retrieval-Augmented Generation]]**: RECAST is a generalization — RAG is one special case where "retrieve the most similar item" is the only operation.
- **[[ReAct]] / Tool-Use Agents**: Related to agentic frameworks that decide which tools to call, but RECAST focuses specifically on evidence construction over heterogeneous sources rather than general task planning.
- **[[In-Context Learning]]**: The frozen AnswerLM uses in-context learning with constructed evidence instead of relying on parametric memory alone.
- **[[Prompt Engineering]] / Program Synthesis**: Requesting the CompilerLM to write executable code is a lightweight form of in-context program synthesis.
- **[[Policy Gradient Methods]]**: GRPO is a variant of RL used to optimize the router without full trajectory supervision.

## Practical Applications
1. **Financial analysis**: Given reports, regulatory filings, and time-series data, automatically construct evidence by filtering by date range, aggregating metrics, and computing ratios to answer analyst questions.

2. **Medical diagnosis**: Over patient records, lab results, and clinical guidelines, route through operations like "filter for recent symptoms," "aggregate comorbidities," "compute risk scores" to support diagnostic reasoning.

3. **Legal document review**: Process contracts, case law, and regulatory documents by filtering by clause type, cross-referencing precedents, and aggregating risk factors to construct evidence for legal opinions.

4. **Enterprise data analytics**: Answer questions over heterogeneous databases (SQL, document stores, time-series) by routing through filtering, joining, and aggregation operations without requiring users to write queries.

5. **Customer support**: Route through order history, product documentation, and support tickets to construct context (e.g., "filter orders from last 30 days, aggregate refund patterns, check warranty status") before generating responses.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Enterprise Evidence Platform**: A SaaS tool where companies upload their data (docs, spreadsheets, databases) and ask natural-language questions; the system learns to route through filtering, joining, and computation operations to answer questions that require multi-source reasoning. Monetize per query or subscription.

2. **Analyst-in-a-Box for Finance**: Automate equity research by routing through financial statements, news archives, and market data to construct evidence for earnings calls, sector reports, or investment theses. Target hedge funds and asset managers.

3. **Compliance Automation**: Audit logs, policies, and transaction data are routed through filtering (by time, actor, type) and aggregation to construct evidence for compliance questions (e.g., "Who accessed sensitive data in Q4?"). Sell to regulated industries.

4. **Medical Evidence Assistant**: Doctors upload patient records; the system learns to route through patient history, lab results, and guidelines to construct diagnostic or treatment evidence. Position as a clinician copilot.

5. **Knowledge Graph Inference Engine**: Instead of pre-building knowledge graphs, let a router dynamically decide what to retrieve and compute over raw data to answer structured queries. Target enterprise AI teams building custom reasoning systems.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational overhead**: RECAST requires multiple sequential decisions and potentially multiple calls to a CompilerLM. Latency may exceed direct RAG in practice, especially for simple queries that don't need multi-step reasoning.

2. **Dependency on CompilerLM quality**: If the frozen CompilerLM cannot execute a requested operation, the router has no fallback. The framework assumes a sufficiently capable frozen model.

3. **Limited to structured operations**: The primitive operations (filter, aggregate, compute) are designed for semi-structured or tabular data. Reasoning over fully unstructured text (e.g., prose essays) may require different operation sets.

4. **Training data requirements**: RECAST was trained on six benchmarks; it's unclear how much labeled data is needed to train a router for a new domain or task.

5. **Explainability**: While the routing decisions are interpretable (users see which operations were chosen), the reasoning for *why* the router chose those operations remains opaque.

6. **Scalability to very large source collections**: Performance on datasets with millions of items, or very long documents, is not evaluated.

## Open Questions
1. **Online learning**: Can the router adapt during deployment based on user feedback without full retraining?

2. **Operation discovery**: Instead of a fixed set of primitives, can the framework learn what operations are useful for a given domain?

3. **Compositional generalization**: How does performance degrade when test tasks combine operations in ways unseen during training?

4. **Cost-benefit trade-offs**: Can we teach the router to balance accuracy vs. latency — i.e., to stop early if it's confident enough, or spend more steps if uncertain?

5. **Scaling to larger routers**: All experiments use a 9B router. Does scaling up the router (e.g., to 34B or 70B) improve performance further, or is the bottleneck the fixed CompilerLM?

6. **Adversarial robustness**: How does RECAST perform on deliberately misleading or noisy source data?

## Key Takeaway
Language models can learn to actively derive answers by routing through computation and filtering, not just passively retrieving information.
