# Agreement Is Not Validity: Cross-Model LLM Consensus in Diagnosing Student Failure Modes in K-12 Math Tutoring Dialogue

**Authors:** Clayton Cohn, Joyce Fonteles, Kirk Vanacore, Gianni Mazza, Candida Crawford et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-06 | **arXiv:** [2610.08703v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08703v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether agreement among multiple LLMs on educational diagnoses actually reflects correct interpretations. The researchers found that while different LLMs agree with each other 76–78% of the time when classifying student math errors, they only agree with human experts 52–60% of the time—suggesting that LLM consensus can mask systematic errors. For educators and learning analytics researchers, this is a critical warning: using multiple models to "validate" automated diagnoses of student reasoning may give false confidence.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of learning analytics and LLM evaluation. Learning analytics applies computational methods to extract insights from educational data—like student dialogue transcripts—to improve teaching and personalize instruction. Prior work has increasingly relied on language models to automate this extraction, leveraging their ability to understand context and nuance in student reasoning. However, LLM reliability in high-stakes educational contexts has not been rigorously validated. The paper builds on coding schema theory, in which human experts create explicit definitions (codebooks) for educational constructs like "conceptual gap" (misunderstanding a core principle vs. careless error). The key tension is between scalability (LLMs can analyze thousands of dialogues cheaply) and validity (does the model actually measure what it claims to measure?).

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical demonstration of the consensus illusion**: The paper shows quantitatively that cross-model agreement (kappa = .755–.781) is substantially higher than human-LLM agreement (kappa = .524–.597), directly contradicting the assumption that LLM consensus validates diagnoses.
2. **Operationalized failure mode taxonomy**: A diagnostic codebook for five specific student error types in math tutoring (uncertainty, misattribution, operator selection, conceptual gap, procedural slip) with human ground truth labels.
3. **Critical methodological warning**: The paper challenges a widespread practice in educational AI—using model agreement as a proxy for correctness—and argues for independent validity evidence instead.

## Practical Applications
1. **Educational diagnostic systems**: Schools building automated systems to flag at-risk students must now validate against human expert review before deployment, not rely on model agreement.
2. **K-12 tutoring platforms**: Intelligent tutoring systems (like Carnegie Learning or ALEKS) that use LLMs for real-time feedback must add a validation layer to ensure diagnoses are sound.
3. **Teacher dashboards**: Rather than surfacing raw LLM classifications, platforms should combine model outputs with flagging uncertainty and requiring human review for pedagogically sensitive decisions.
4. **Curriculum design**: Learning scientists analyzing tutoring dialogues to improve problem sequences or instructional design can use LLMs for initial coding, but must spot-check and calibrate against human experts.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Validated LLM Diagnostic Middleware**: A service that wraps multiple LLMs for educational classification tasks, runs calibration studies against human experts per school/district, and reports confidence bounds. Schools pay per validated deployment.
2. **Learning Analytics Auditing Platform**: Software that continuously monitors LLM-based diagnostic systems in production, flags when model consensus diverges from human validation samples, and alerts administrators.
3. **Interactive Codebook Builder for Educators**: A tool that helps teachers and researchers create rigorous diagnostic taxonomies and automatically benchmarks any model (LLM or traditional ML) against human ground truth before scaling.
4. **Hybrid Tutoring Intelligence**: A K-12 tutoring chatbot that pairs LLM reasoning-analysis with low-cost human review loops—flagging high-confidence diagnoses to teachers automatically and requesting expert input only on edge cases.
5. **EdTech Compliance/Validation Service**: Consulting + software that helps edtech companies document and validate their AI systems for evidence of learning impact, addressing skeptical school districts and regulators.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Small scope of failure modes**: Only five student error types examined; unclear if findings generalize to richer or more ambiguous classification tasks.
2. **Single subject domain**: Restricted to K-12 math; results may not transfer to language arts, science, or adult learners.
3. **Model selection unclear**: The paper doesn't specify which LLMs were compared or whether they were fine-tuned; conclusions depend on model choice.
4. **Ground truth quality**: Human expert agreement not reported; if human coders themselves disagree, the "validity" baseline is noisy.
5. **Dialogue context limitations**: Real tutoring dialogue may be longer, more complex, or ambiguous than the samples analyzed; model performance could degrade with scale.
6. **No causal mechanism**: The paper identifies the consensus problem but doesn't explain *why* models agree with each other but not humans (e.g., shared pretraining bias, shortcut learning).

## Open Questions
1. **Why do LLMs agree with each other?** Are they learning the same shortcuts or biases from pretraining, or are they converging on a stable-but-wrong interpretation? Probing or ablation studies could help.
2. **Does fine-tuning on human-labeled data improve validity?** If models are retrained on expert codebook examples, does consensus then track human judgment?
3. **What is the cost-benefit tradeoff?** How much human review is needed to validate a scaled LLM system? Is it cheaper than manual coding?
4. **Can ensemble methods improve validity?** Rather than majority vote, can uncertainty estimates or model diversity help weight disagreement?
5. **Do task-specific prompts help?** Does carefully engineering the prompt with examples from the codebook reduce the consensus-validity gap?
6. **How do model size and training data affect the problem?** Do smaller, domain-specific models differ from large general-purpose LLMs?

## Key Takeaway
LLM consensus on student errors can mask systematic mistakes; independent validation against human experts is essential before deploying automated diagnosis at scale.
