# Why Large Language Models and Humans Converge and Diverge in Evaluating Creativity

**Authors:** Pengzhao Lyu, Yeun Joon Kim, Hanlin Xiao, Yingyue Luna Luan
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22218v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22218v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates when and why large language models (LLMs) agree or disagree with humans when evaluating creative ideas. Across three studies with six different LLMs and over 2,000 ideas, the researchers find that LLMs converge with humans on novelty but diverge sharply on contextual factors like market viability and social impact—and that different LLM models apply fundamentally different evaluation standards. This matters because LLMs are increasingly used to assess creativity in real applications, yet their judgments are unpredictably misaligned with human values.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of computational creativity assessment and AI alignment. Creativity evaluation is inherently multidimensional: humans typically judge ideas along novelty (originality), usefulness (practical value), and contextual appropriateness (fit to social, market, or reputational circumstances). Prior research in organizational psychology and creativity science has established that human evaluations integrate both intrinsic properties of ideas and extrinsic contextual signals.

The paper builds on emerging work in [[LLM evaluation]] and human-AI alignment, which examines whether language models can faithfully replicate human judgment distributions. Key prior findings show that LLMs often exhibit systematic biases in preference tasks, and that alignment is task- and domain-dependent rather than universal.

## Core Contributions
1. **Quantified divergence across creativity dimensions**: The paper operationalizes which aspects of creativity LLMs capture well (novelty ≈ 0.70 alignment) versus poorly (contextual dimension ≈ 0.42 alignment), moving beyond aggregate alignment metrics.

2. **Model-specific evaluation standards**: Six different LLMs applied visibly different subsets of evaluation criteria—some broad, others narrow. This is not just noise; it's systematic variation in what each model "cares about" when assessing ideas.

3. **Causal evidence for context-blindness**: Study 3 demonstrated that contextual information (e.g., market conditions, reputational stakes) significantly moved human ratings but left LLM ratings nearly flat, showing LLMs lack robust integration of external signals.

4. **Actionable guidance on model selection**: The paper demonstrates that LLM choice itself is consequential—picking different models produces different creativity rankings of the same ideas.

## Connections to Other Work
This research directly informs broader questions about [[AI alignment]], particularly whether language models can serve as reliable proxy evaluators for human preferences. It complements work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] by showing that different models internalize different evaluation heuristics, not just apply the same standards with different noise levels.

The findings resonate with research on LLM biases and limitations: models struggle with grounding in social and market contexts (a known weakness), and they appear to overweight statistical patterns in training data (novelty, linguistic originality) while underweighting pragmatic constraints.

It also relates to debates about [[RLHF]] (reinforcement learning from human feedback) and how to train LLMs to align with multidimensional human values—if models diverge systematically on contextual reasoning, RLHF training for creativity evaluation may require explicit supervision on contextual dimensions.

## Practical Applications
1. **Automated innovation pipeline filtering**: Companies can use this research to decide which LLM (if any) to deploy when screening novel product ideas, knowing it will over-value novelty and miss market-fit signals.

2. **Hybrid human-LLM workflows**: Design systems where LLMs handle high-volume screening for novelty, then route to human specialists for contextual judgment (market, feasibility, stakeholder impact).

3. **Creativity training and feedback**: Educational platforms can use LLMs to give instant feedback on creativity's intrinsic dimension (originality) but warn students that external factors (applicability, social context) require human input.

4. **R&D portfolio management**: Research teams can use this to understand which LLM evaluator to consult for different stages (early brainstorming vs. go/no-go decisions requiring context sensitivity).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"CreativeScore API"**: A managed service that runs submissions through multiple LLMs with known evaluation profiles, returns a multi-dimensional score card (novelty via GPT, contextual fit via Claude, etc.), and visualizes where models agree vs. diverge—helping users make informed decisions about idea quality.

2. **"Context-Augmented LLM Evaluator"**: A fine-tuned model or prompt-engineering framework that embeds contextual data (market reports, competitor analysis, regulatory landscape) into the evaluation prompt, reducing LLM context-blindness for organizational innovation.

3. **"Model Selection Dashboard"**: A SaaS tool for companies that lets them benchmark their own creativity evaluations against the six LLMs studied, identifies which model's standards best match their brand/values, and auto-routes submissions accordingly.

4. **"Creativity Arbitrage Platform"**: A crowdsourced platform where human experts rate ideas, then algorithms identify gaps between human judgment and LLM judgment—selling those insights as a "hidden value detection" service to investors or accelerators.

5. **"Interpretability-as-a-Service for Evaluation"**: Offer organizations a diagnostic tool that automatically extracts which evaluation criteria each LLM is actually using on their specific ideas (via attention probing or embedding analysis), making black-box evaluations transparent and auditable.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Small model sample (N=6)**: Findings may not generalize to newer or future LLMs with different architectures (e.g., mixture-of-experts models) or training approaches.

2. **Domain specificity unclear**: The study uses idea/creativity domains but doesn't isolate whether findings hold equally across scientific innovation, artistic creativity, business ideas, etc.

3. **Context representation is loose**: "Contextual information" was provided as text within the prompt—the study doesn't isolate whether LLMs are fundamentally incapable of integrating context or just do so poorly when context is narrative rather than structured data.

4. **No mechanistic explanation**: The paper describes *what* diverges but not *why*—it's unclear whether the divergence stems from training data imbalance, architectural design, or fundamental limits of attention mechanisms in capturing pragmatic reasoning.

5. **Alignment measured via correlation, not calibration**: Moderate correlation (0.50–0.70) doesn't mean LLM confidence is well-calibrated; an LLM could rank ideas similarly to humans but assign wildly different confidence levels, which matters in deployment.

## Open Questions
1. Can explicit in-context prompt engineering or few-shot examples improve LLM sensitivity to contextual information, or is the deficit deeper?

2. Do multimodal LLMs (with visual access to prototypes, market images, etc.) show better contextual alignment than text-only models?

3. Can a single LLM be fine-tuned to dynamically adopt different evaluation standards depending on the stated *purpose* of evaluation (e.g., "evaluate for artistic merit" vs. "evaluate for commercial viability")?

4. How do these findings scale to evaluation of more complex artifacts (e.g., business plans, software systems) versus simple ideas? Does contextual blindness grow or shrink?

5. What role does retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) over external market/social data play in reducing context-divergence?

6. Can interpretability techniques like attention visualization or logit probing identify which neurons/weights encode contextual vs. intrinsic creativity reasoning?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs evaluate creativity by novelty well but miss market and social context that humans value—model choice matters.
