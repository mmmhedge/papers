# Verification with Transfer: Exact Information Frontiers and Their Price in Calls

**Authors:** Hazar Yueksel
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.12211v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12211v1)

## Summary
This paper studies the information-theoretic cost of verifying answers through transfer learning—using related source tasks to reduce the number of direct verifications needed. The authors derive exact "information frontiers" (optimal trade-offs between source calls and verification budget) and show these frontiers can predict which bits a transformer actually uses during training, suggesting the theory meaningfully characterizes learning efficiency.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on rate-distortion theory (a classical information-theoretic framework that quantifies how much compression is possible at a given error level) and applies it to the verification problem. A verifier is a function that accepts or rejects candidate answers; under a uniform prior over k-bit answers, checking each answer independently requires exponentially many verifications (2^k - 1 for zero error). The authors extend this to settings where you can call related "source" tasks to narrow the answer space before verification. The framework formalizes the trade-off: fewer verifications require more source calls, and vice versa. The paper proves that the optimal information cost is a list rate-distortion function and derives closed-form solutions for linear codes over finite fields.

## Core Contributions
- **Exact information frontiers**: A characterization of the minimal information (in bits) needed for any interleaving of source calls and n verifications to succeed with probability s; proved to equal a list rate-distortion function and achieved by observing sources before any verification.
- **Call efficiency bounds**: For unique answers, designed sources meet the information lower bound within 1 + log₂(5) calls; for general cases, within a logarithmic additive term (no constant suffices).
- **Source-first vs. interleaved protocols**: Under exact verification, moving all source calls before verification preserves worst-case call counts, yet interleaving can save unbounded expected calls; under noisy verification, source-first can lose unbounded factors in both information and error.
- **Closed-form solution for linear codes**: For linear banks over 𝔽₂, optimal accuracy has a computable closed form; the budget profile is computable in time 2^O(h²)poly(J, k+h).
- **Mechanistic prediction of transformer behavior**: The frontier predicts which latent dimensions a small transformer uses; in experiments, low XOR degree did not suffice for bit usage, suggesting the theory captures real learning structure.

## Practical Applications
- **Active learning & label efficiency**: Determining optimal strategies for querying related labeled datasets (source tasks) before requesting expensive expert verification on the final task.
- **Multi-task learning pipeline design**: Deciding which auxiliary tasks to solve and in what order to minimize the cost of verifying the main task.
- **Curriculum design in safety-critical domains**: Constructing ordered task sequences (e.g., medical diagnosis via progressively harder cases) where verification is expensive.
- **Federated or distributed learning**: Coordinating which agents solve source tasks vs. which verify the main model, under communication constraints.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Verification-as-a-service platform**: Use information frontiers to dynamically recommend which intermediate tasks a client should label/source before expensive verification, optimizing label budget allocation.
2. **Curriculum learning advisor**: SaaS tool that ingests a main task and candidate source datasets, computes the frontier, and suggests optimal task orderings and budget splits to minimize labeling cost.
3. **Noisy verification optimization engine**: For domains where verification is unreliable (e.g., crowdsourced labels, sensor data), apply the theory to recommend redundancy and source-first vs. interleaved protocols.
4. **Transfer learning cost estimator**: A library that predicts the minimal number of source tasks and calls needed for a given target accuracy on a new task, helping practitioners allocate compute/data budgets.
5. **Adaptive model training system**: Real-time monitoring of latent dimension usage (as in the transformer experiment) to prune unused capacity and optimize training budget allocation across architectures.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scalability to high-dimensional, real-world problems**: The closed-form results and computability guarantees hold for linear codes over 𝔽₂; extension to nonlinear, real-valued, or unstructured problems is unclear.
- **Exact vs. approximate verifiers**: The theory assumes exact verifiers (which are rare in practice). The extension to noisy verifiers shows losses can be unbounded, but practical guidance for real noisy settings is limited.
- **Answer space structure**: The framework assumes answers are discrete and finite (k bits). Continuous or infinite-dimensional answers require fundamentally different analysis.
- **Source availability and relatedness**: The theory does not address how to select or design source tasks when they are not given; it assumes sources exist and can be quantified.
- **Transformer experiment scope**: The validation on a small transformer with specific XOR-based synthetic tasks does not demonstrate scalability to modern large models or real applications.

## Open Questions
- How do the frontiers generalize to nonlinear, continuous-valued, or infinite-dimensional answer spaces?
- Can the theory predict optimal source selection (which tasks to solve) rather than just the order and budget split?
- How tight are the bounds when sources are noisy, incomplete, or adversarially chosen?
- What is the empirical frontier for realistic ML tasks (vision, NLP, control) and how closely do practitioners' choices match the theory?
- Can the machine-checked results be extended to cover the two clauses currently unverified, and what additional structure would that reveal?
- How does the framework extend to multi-verifier or hierarchical verification settings?

## Key Takeaway
Information theory reveals the exact cost of learning efficiently via related tasks: fewer verifications require logarithmically more source calls, and this frontier predicts which parts of a network actually learn.
