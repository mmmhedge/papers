# A Spread-Gated Hawkes-Flocking Model for Best Bid and Ask Dynamics, with an Application to Limit Order Placement

**Authors:** Hyoeun Lee, Kiseop Lee
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.36631v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36631v1)

## Summary
This paper models how best bid and ask prices move together in financial markets using a spread-gated Hawkes-flocking model—a statistical framework that captures how quote updates on one side of the market influence the other. The authors apply this to real stock data and derive optimal strategies for placing limit orders, showing that understanding these joint dynamics improves trading decisions.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on Hawkes processes (self-exciting point processes where past events make future events more likely) and flocking models (frameworks from physics that capture coordinated motion). The "spread-gated" element refers to a mechanism that disables certain dynamics when the bid-ask spread is minimal (one tick). Prior work in market microstructure (the study of how prices form and orders execute) has focused on individual sides of the order book; this paper extends that by modeling bid and ask as a coupled system. The authors use maximum likelihood estimation to fit the model to limit order book data—the record of all pending buy and sell orders at different price levels.

## Core Contributions
1. **Spread-gated Hawkes-flocking model**: A novel dynamic system where spread-narrowing moves switch off at minimum spread, and cross-side excitation (ask updates triggering bid updates, or vice versa) depends on the current spread width.
2. **Non-explosiveness proof**: Shows the process doesn't blow up in finite time, a necessary mathematical property.
3. **Efficient likelihood computation**: Develops an O(N) recursive algorithm for computing likelihood (vs. slower alternatives), enabling practical estimation.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates on INTC and MSFT that the cross-side term is statistically significant and improves model fit (AIC/BIC).
5. **Closed-form limit order sizing**: Derives optimal position size for a single-period limit order at the best or second-best quote, conditioning on the model's predicted next events.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends classical [[Hawkes processes]] and microstructure models by:
- Introducing state-dependent gating (spread-dependent behavior), similar to regime-switching models in finance.
- Linking to the broader algorithmic trading literature on optimal order placement and execution (e.g., [[VWAP]], [[TWAP]], optimal liquidation).
- Relating to [[limit order book]] dynamics research, which often treats sides separately; this unifies them.
- Connecting to [[market-making]] strategies, which hinge on understanding bid-ask correlations.

## Practical Applications
1. **Limit order placement algorithms**: Trading firms can use the optimal sizing formula to decide how many shares to offer at the best bid/ask or one level deeper, adapting to current spread and volatility.
2. **Intraday market-making**: Market makers can use the cross-side excitation term to anticipate moves on the opposite side of the book and adjust their inventory faster.
3. **Execution algorithms**: Large orders can be split using the model's predictions of when spreads will narrow, improving fill quality.
4. **Risk management in dark pools or alternative venues**: Understanding how quotes are correlated helps estimate adverse selection risk when filling large orders.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Smart limit order router**: A tool that automatically places and reprices limit orders on behalf of brokers, using the spread-gated model to maximize fill probability while minimizing market impact. License to buy-side firms.
2. **Microstructure analytics SaaS**: A dashboard ingesting real-time LOB data, fitting the model on-the-fly, and alerting traders when cross-side excitation spikes—signaling potential repricing on the opposite side.
3. **Execution optimization engine**: A white-label service for brokers that suggests optimal order size and venue (exchange, dark pool) given client constraints, using the model's closed-form solutions.
4. **Quantitative research data product**: Sell pre-fitted model parameters and next-event probabilities for major stocks to quant traders, updated daily; compete with traditional market microstructure data vendors.
5. **Regulatory surveillance tool**: Use the model to detect anomalous cross-side coupling in LOB data, flagging potential spoofing or layering (spoofing = fake orders to create illusions of demand). Sell to exchanges or regulators.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Single-period horizon**: The limit order sizing formula assumes a one-period lookahead; real traders need multi-period or continuous strategies.
2. **Model misspecification risk**: If the true process deviates from the assumed functional form (e.g., nonlinear spread dependence), estimates and predictions fail.
3. **Multimodality**: The likelihood is multimodal on single days, requiring multi-start optimization, which is computationally expensive and may trap in local maxima.
4. **Limited data**: Only two large-cap stocks (INTC, MSFT) tested; results may not generalize to mid-cap, small-cap, or micro-cap stocks with different microstructure.
5. **Execution probability externally supplied**: The model requires external estimates of execution probabilities; errors here propagate to the final trading recommendation.
6. **No regime shifts**: The model does not explicitly handle market regimes (e.g., high-vol vs. low-vol periods); a single day's parameters may not apply across market conditions.

## Open Questions
1. **Multi-period and continuous strategies**: Can the closed-form optimal sizing be extended to multiple orders over a trading session or to a continuous-time framework?
2. **Generalization across market conditions**: How do model parameters vary with volatility, trading volume, and time of day? Can a single adaptive model be fitted?
3. **Other asset classes**: Does the model work for FX pairs, futures, or cryptocurrencies, which have different microstructures?
4. **Asymmetry**: Do the dynamics differ between widening and narrowing spreads, or between bid and ask initiated moves? Can this be formally tested?
5. **Latency and queue position**: Real traders care about queue position at each level; can the model incorporate depth and order queue dynamics?
6. **Causality and information**: Does the cross-side excitation represent informed trading, or just mechanical inventory rebalancing? Can information be extracted?

## Key Takeaway
A statistical model of coupled bid-ask dynamics improves limit order placement by predicting when quotes will move together.
