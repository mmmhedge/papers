# Stop Shipping AI Agents on Faith: Capability Is Not Production Readiness

**Authors:** Fouad Bousetouane
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.27677v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27677v1)

## Summary
This paper argues that demonstrating AI agent capability—through tests and demos—is insufficient to determine whether an agent is safe to deploy in production. Instead, the authors introduce the ProofAgent Index (PAI), a governance readiness framework that evaluates agents across four dimensions: Evaluation (observed behavior), Context (operating environment), Compliance (regulatory alignment), and Governance (organizational control and auditability). Testing PAI in healthcare and finance shows it successfully separates high-risk from low-risk agent configurations and reveals that context engineering and governance visibility matter far more than capability alone.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of AI safety, operational governance, and regulatory compliance. It builds on earlier frameworks in [[RLHF]] and alignment that recognize the gap between capability (what a system can do) and safety (whether it should and will behave correctly in practice). The paper also draws from enterprise risk management and audit traditions, which emphasize that organizational controls—not just technical performance—determine whether a system can operate responsibly. The core insight is that AI agents operate in sociotechnical systems where environmental context, regulatory constraints, and human oversight structures determine real-world outcomes as much as the agent's underlying intelligence.

## Core Contributions
1. **Reframes deployment readiness**: Shifts agent release decisions from binary capability signals to multi-dimensional evidence of organizational and operational readiness.
2. **ProofAgent Index (PAI)**: A concrete four-pillar governance readiness metric (Evaluation, Context, Compliance, Governance) with empirical validation in regulated domains.
3. **ProofAgent Harness**: Open-source infrastructure enabling systematic, auditable agent evaluation and governance—not just capability testing.
4. **Empirical separation of risk profiles**: Demonstrates across healthcare and finance that PAI successfully identifies which agent configurations are genuinely production-safe, independent of capability improvements.
5. **Context-aware reliability insight**: Shows that context engineering (how you configure the agent's operating environment) is a stronger predictor of reliability than raw capability.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper extends ideas in [[alignment]] and AI safety by moving beyond capability benchmarks. It aligns with work on [[risk-management]] and compliance in financial systems, where regulators require organizations to demonstrate not just system performance but governance, auditability, and control. It also connects to broader discussions around responsible AI deployment, echoing concerns raised in work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (understanding what agents actually do) and [[RLHF]]-style approaches to ensuring systems behave as intended. The emphasis on organizational readiness parallels governance frameworks in MLOps and model risk management.

## Practical Applications
1. **Healthcare deployment**: Agents that retrieve patient records, suggest diagnostics, or coordinate care must pass PAI before production—ensuring hospitals can audit decisions, comply with HIPAA, and maintain physician oversight.
2. **Financial services**: Trading agents, compliance chatbots, or loan underwriting agents require PAI sign-off to satisfy regulatory audits and demonstrate risk controls to regulators and internal audit teams.
3. **Enterprise automation**: Organizations deploying agents for internal workflow automation (HR, procurement, customer service) can use PAI to justify agent release to legal and compliance teams and to customers.
4. **Regulated B2B SaaS**: AI-native products (document processing, claims handling, legal research) can bundle PAI readiness as a competitive differentiator in markets where compliance is a purchase criterion.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ProofAgent as a compliance SaaS**: Expand the open-source Harness into a managed platform where enterprises submit agents for automated PAI evaluation and receive a "readiness certificate" for regulatory reviews and board presentations.
2. **Vertical-specific agent auditors**: Build consulting or SaaS services that specialize in PAI evaluation for specific regulated industries (healthcare, fintech, insurance) and bundle readiness certification into contracts.
3. **Agent governance insurance**: Create a new insurance product for organizations deploying AI agents—underwritten only for agents that pass PAI—reducing liability and enabling faster adoption.
4. **Internal audit automation**: Offer large enterprises tooling to run continuous PAI monitoring on production agents, generating compliance reports and alerts when governance evidence degrades.
5. **Agent vetting marketplace**: Build a platform where third-party PAI auditors certify agents for enterprise use, creating a trust market similar to code security scanning or ISO certification for AI systems.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Domain-specific calibration**: PAI was validated only in healthcare and finance; generalization to other regulated domains (aviation, autonomous vehicles, critical infrastructure) remains unproven.
2. **Governance dimension subjectivity**: The "Governance" pillar relies heavily on organizational self-assessment and audit processes, which vary widely and may not be uniformly rigorous or honest across contexts.
3. **Context dependency**: PAI's effectiveness depends on accurately modeling the operating environment; if context is misspecified or changes post-deployment, readiness predictions degrade.
4. **No adversarial robustness testing**: The paper does not address whether agents pass PAI while remaining vulnerable to adversarial inputs, prompt injection, or distribution shift.
5. **Scalability to multi-agent systems**: PAI was tested on individual agents; extension to [[multi-agent-systems]] where agents interact with each other or external APIs introduces coordination and emergent-behavior risks not yet measured.
6. **Temporal validity**: Readiness is a snapshot; the paper does not establish how long PAI approval remains valid or how to detect readiness decay over time.

## Open Questions
1. **How does PAI generalize beyond healthcare and finance?** What do domains like transportation, energy systems, or national security require differently from the PAI framework?
2. **Can governance readiness be quantified more objectively?** How can organizations standardize the Governance pillar to reduce auditor subjectivity and enable automated scoring?
3. **What is the false-negative rate?** How many agents pass PAI but later fail in production? Conversely, how many fail PAI but would have succeeded?
4. **How do you detect readiness drift?** Once deployed, how frequently should agents be re-evaluated, and what changes trigger a new PAI assessment?
5. **Does PAI apply to emergent behavior in multi-agent systems?** If agents interact with other agents or systems, does PAI still predict safety?
6. **What is the cost-benefit trade-off?** How much does PAI governance overhead slow deployment, and is the risk reduction worth the compliance burden?

## Key Takeaway
Capability alone does not make agents safe for production; governance, context, and compliance readiness must be audited and demonstrated before deployment.
