# Positional choice and robust collective behavior in fish schools: biohybrid experiments and modeling

**Authors:** Vahagn Grigoryan, Donato Romano, Cesare Stefanini, Giulia De Masi
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35554v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35554v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how fish schools maintain collective behavior through experiments combining live fish with robotic fish, then builds mathematical models to understand the underlying rules. The key finding is that realistic schooling models need to include both social interaction rules and individual exploratory behavior—standard models are too fragile to small changes in their parameters and miss what actually happens in nature.

## Theoretical Background
Collective behavior in animal groups has traditionally been modeled using boid-like rules: each individual adjusts its position and velocity based on neighbors' positions and velocities (attraction, repulsion, alignment). These agent-based models reproduce many observed patterns like flocking and schooling. However, they typically assume fixed interaction rules learned from bulk observations rather than direct measurement of individual decision-making. The paper tests whether these classical models match what individual fish actually do when placed in controlled settings with known stimuli (robotic fish).

## Core Contributions
1. **Experimental validation of individual fish behavior**: Used biohybrid experiments (live fish + robot fish) to directly measure positional preferences and time spent in different relative positions, rather than inferring rules from school-level patterns.

2. **Parameter sensitivity analysis**: Demonstrated that classical schooling models require extremely specific parameter tuning to match experiments—minor perturbations cause complete failure, raising questions about biological realism.

3. **Robustness through exploration**: Proposed a new model adding an exploratory term that captures moment-to-moment positional preference. This version matches experiments while being robust to parameter variation.

4. **Dual-scale behavior**: Showed that the new model exhibits both cohesive schooling at the collective level and persistent exploratory behavior at individual and school levels simultaneously.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[agent-based models]] of collective behavior and empirical animal behavior studies. It relates to the broader challenge of building [[multi-agent systems]] that are both realistic and robust—a problem also central to swarm robotics and distributed AI. The emphasis on robustness to parameter uncertainty echoes concerns in [[reinforcement-learning]] about policy brittleness and domain adaptation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Bioinspired swarm robotics**: Designing robot swarms for search-and-rescue or environmental monitoring that combine social cohesion with adaptive exploration.
2. **Aquaculture optimization**: Improving fish farm designs by understanding how tank geometry and stocking density affect welfare and stress based on natural schooling behavior.
3. **Autonomous vehicle flocking**: Traffic flow management using schooling principles that include both coordination and individual flexibility.
4. **Ecological prediction**: Better forecasting of fish school dynamics under changing environmental conditions (temperature, predators, food availability).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Swarm robotics platform for underwater exploration**: Low-cost modular drones that use the robust schooling algorithm for autonomous coral reef mapping or pipeline inspection without centralized control.
2. **Fish farm management software**: Real-time monitoring system using computer vision to track fish positions, detect stress via schooling breakdown, and optimize feeding/aeration using the model's predictions.
3. **Distributed sensor networks**: Deploy nodes that self-organize into schools for environmental monitoring (water quality, pollution) with built-in robustness to node failure.
4. **Bio-hybrid research toolkit**: Commercial biohybrid setup combining off-the-shelf robot fish with tracking software and modeling tools for researchers studying collective behavior in different species.
5. **Autonomous delivery swarms**: Small underwater drones for last-mile delivery in harbors or restricted water areas, using robust schooling algorithms to maintain formation while adapting to obstacles.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scale**: Experiments use only four robot fish; unclear whether results hold with larger schools where dynamics become more complex.
2. **Simplified environment**: Tank experiments don't include predators, food sources, or environmental variability (currents, visibility) that shape real schooling.
3. **Species specificity**: Results are from a specific fish species; generalization to other schooling species is untested.
4. **Model interpretability**: The "exploratory term" is somewhat ad-hoc; the paper doesn't fully explain *why* this particular formulation works biologically or whether other formulations would also be robust.
5. **Parameter identifiability**: Even the new model requires fitting to experiments; the paper doesn't address how well the approach works with limited or noisy observational data.

## Open Questions
1. How does the exploratory component scale with school size? Does individual exploration suppress or amplify in larger groups?
2. Can the model predict schooling behavior under novel conditions (different tank geometry, lighting, temperature) without retraining?
3. What is the evolutionary or behavioral basis for the exploratory term? Is it related to information-seeking, predator avoidance, or metabolic constraints?
4. How robust is the model to heterogeneity (different fish types or personalities) rather than identical agents?
5. Can similar principles explain collective behavior in other taxa (birds, insects, mammals)?

## Key Takeaway
Fish schools need both social rules and individual exploration; realistic models must include both to remain robust.
