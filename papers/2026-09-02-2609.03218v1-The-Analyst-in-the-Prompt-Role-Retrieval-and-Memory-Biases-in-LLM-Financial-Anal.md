# The Analyst in the Prompt: Role, Retrieval, and Memory Biases in LLM Financial Analysis

**Authors:** Ahmed Asaad, Amr Mohamed, Yang Zhang, Omneya Abdelsalam
**Published:** 2026-09-02 | **arXiv:** [2609.03218v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.03218v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates how LLMs' financial analysis changes based on user context (roles, memory, profiles)—and whether they retrieve different evidence or interpret the same evidence differently. Testing 3,575 SEC filings across twelve models, the authors find that most bias comes from *interpretation*, not evidence selection, and propose two mitigation strategies with mixed results. This matters because LLMs are increasingly used for high-stakes financial decisions where consistency and objectivity are critical.

## Theoretical Background
Large language models (LLMs)—transformer-based neural networks trained on vast text corpora—are now deployed in financial advisory, equity research, and investment analysis tasks. Prior work has shown that LLMs are sensitive to prompt design and "persona conditioning" (instructing the model to adopt a specific viewpoint or role). In finance, this sensitivity is especially dangerous because decisions depend on interpreting long, complex documents (like SEC filings) where subtle interpretive biases can shift conclusions. The paper builds on two existing concerns: (1) *retrieval bias*—models might select different evidence under different prompts—and (2) *interpretation bias*—models might read the same evidence differently depending on context. The authors separate these mechanisms to understand which drives financial analysis errors.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical isolation of interpretation vs. retrieval bias**: Using a controlled design (same evidence, different roles), the authors show that ~80% of context spillover comes from how LLMs *interpret* evidence, not from which evidence they retrieve—a finding that flips the intuitive hypothesis.
2. **Large-scale financial benchmark**: Testing 3,575 SEC filings across twelve LLMs (including GPT-4, Claude, Llama, Gemini variants) is the first systematic quantification of this problem at scale in finance.
3. **Two practical mitigation strategies** with transparent effectiveness: (a) expressing investor mindset as a user profile rather than assistant role, and (b) separating evidence-based reasoning from personalized outputs. Both reduce spillover but incompletely, with model-dependent variance.
4. **Documentation of model variance**: Different LLMs show drastically different susceptibilities to context bias, suggesting no one-size-fits-all fix.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper extends recent work on [[prompt injection]] and [[adversarial prompting]] into the financial domain, where stakes are higher. It relates to broader [[alignment]] concerns—especially the tension between personalization (making LLMs adaptive to user needs) and objectivity (making them give the same answer to the same financial question regardless of who asks). The work also connects to [[mechanistic-interpretability]] efforts to understand why LLMs weight context the way they do, though the authors focus on behavioral measurement rather than internal representations. It complements [[RLHF]] and instruction-tuning research by showing that even well-trained models leak context into supposedly factual outputs.

## Practical Applications
- **Equity research platforms** (Bloomberg, FactSet, etc.): detecting when analyst personas are distorting recommendations and alerting researchers.
- **Robo-advisor and automated portfolio management systems**: ensuring that algorithmic recommendations for SEC filings and earnings reports don't drift based on implicit user bias.
- **Compliance and audit workflows**: flagging when LLM-generated analyst reports diverge from the same filings under different prompt conditions, reducing regulatory risk.
- **Financial education software**: tuning LLM responses to remove unintended bias when teaching students how to analyze earnings documents.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Bias auditor for financial LLMs**: A SaaS tool that tests institutional LLM deployments against a battery of role/persona prompts to detect interpretation drift and quantify risk exposure to model bias.
2. **Neutral-mode financial analysis API**: A wrapper around LLMs that strips user context during evidence extraction and fact-checking, then reapplies personalization only for recommendations—marketed as "bias-resistant equity research."
3. **SEC filing "consensus oracle"**: Run the same 10-K through multiple LLMs and roles; aggregate outputs to highlight which conclusions are robust vs. role-dependent, sold to hedge funds as a hedge against LLM model risk.
4. **Prompt engineering platform for finance**: A specialized tool for building and testing persona-aware prompts with built-in A/B testing against control (neutral) runs, helping quants and analysts diagnose whether their LLM is answering *their question* or *a different question under their persona*.
5. **Regulatory-grade LLM certification service**: Audit and certify that financial institutions' LLM systems meet consistency/objectivity thresholds for fiduciary or compliance use, with regular re-testing.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scope narrow to interpretive tasks**: The paper tests financial *analysis* (reading and reasoning about filings), not tasks like price prediction or feature engineering where interpretation bias may matter less.
- **SEC filings are text-heavy but structured**: Results may not generalize to less formal or noisier evidence (e.g., earnings call transcripts, news articles, social media).
- **Mitigation strategies are crude**: Expressing investor mindset as a profile instead of role is a heuristic fix; no principled mechanism ensures interpretation neutrality.
- **Model variance not explained**: The paper documents that different LLMs have different susceptibilities but does not identify what architectural or training properties cause this—limiting transferability of fixes.
- **No causal mechanism identified**: The paper measures bias but does not open the model to explain *why* context shifts interpretation (mechanistic black box remains intact).
- **Static filings, single-turn prompts**: Real-world financial analysis is multi-turn (follow-up questions, clarifications); the paper does not test memory or conversation drift over time.

## Open Questions
- **Can we identify and remove the internal representations that cause context spillover?** Do attention heads or particular layers encode "persona" vs. "evidence"? Can pruning or fine-tuning surgically remove bias while preserving personalization?
- **How does context bias compound in multi-turn financial workflows?** If an analyst asks ten questions about a filing with an implicit persona, does bias accumulate or stabilize?
- **Do the mitigation strategies generalize to other high-stakes domains** (medicine, law, policy analysis)? Or are finance-specific fixes needed?
- **What is the optimal trade-off between personalization and objectivity?** Is there a theoretical bound on bias reduction without losing useful user-adaptation?
- **Can ensemble methods or model chaining reduce spillover?** E.g., using one LLM to extract evidence neutrally and a second to interpret it under persona?
- **How do newer LLMs (GPT-5, etc.) and reasoning-focused models compare?** Do chain-of-thought or intermediate reasoning steps reduce interpretation bias?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs bias financial analysis more through *how they interpret* evidence than through which evidence they retrieve, and current fixes are incomplete across models.
