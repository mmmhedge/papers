# RNADyn: A Benchmark for Generating and Understanding RNA Dynamics

**Authors:** Yiming Huang, Lennart Bastian, Hanqun Cao, Luis Vollmers, Tolga Birdal
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03712v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03712v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces RNADynBench, a large-scale benchmark dataset of RNA molecular dynamics simulations, and RNADynNet, a unified neural network model that generates realistic RNA trajectories and extracts dynamics information from static structures. RNA molecules constantly change shape to perform their biological functions, but datasets and methods to understand these dynamics at scale have been scarce—this work fills that gap with quality-controlled data and a model that treats trajectory generation and dynamics understanding as complementary tasks.

## Theoretical Background
RNA dynamics involve conformational changes (changes in 3D shape) that are critical to function but difficult to observe directly. Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations use physics-based equations to predict atomic trajectories over time, producing all-atom trajectories showing how each atom moves. Traditional approaches either generate trajectories (forward simulation) or extract features like RMSF (root-mean-square fluctuation, a measure of atomic motion) separately. This paper builds on generative modeling frameworks—particularly coordinate denoising (a technique where neural networks learn to denoise corrupted molecular coordinates iteratively)—and representation learning, which aims to extract meaningful patterns from raw data. The work connects deep learning with structural biology, where understanding RNA shape is fundamental to drug design and synthetic biology.

## Core Contributions
1. **RNADynBench**: A curated dataset of 2,585 all-atom RNA MD trajectories (100 nanoseconds each), with rigorous quality control and leakage-controlled train/test splits designed to prevent data leakage (using structural similarity measures to ensure test molecules are genuinely novel).
2. **RNADynNet**: A unified architecture with a shared backbone that simultaneously: (a) generates plausible multi-frame trajectories from a single input conformation, and (b) extracts dynamics fingerprints (learned representations capturing motion patterns) from that same static structure.
3. **Physical grounding**: Integration of physics constraints during training to ensure generated trajectories and learned representations respect known molecular dynamics principles, improving correlation with real MD simulations (RMSF r=0.875 on test set, r=0.766 on high-flexibility molecules).
4. **Single-conformer prediction**: Demonstrating that dynamics can be predicted from a single static structure with comparable accuracy to full MD simulations, a significant simplification.

## Practical Applications
1. **Drug discovery**: Predict how RNA structures move in cells without expensive simulation, accelerating computational screening of small molecules that bind to dynamic RNA targets.
2. **Synthetic biology**: Design stable or unstable RNA constructs by predicting their conformational flexibility from sequence alone, useful for biosensors and genetic circuits.
3. **Structure-based RNA therapeutics**: Optimize anti-sense oligonucleotides and siRNA designs by understanding target RNA dynamics without running full MD.
4. **COVID-19 and viral research**: Rapidly assess how viral RNA structures (e.g., SARS-CoV-2) move and expose conserved epitopes for vaccine design.
5. **Metabolic engineering**: Predict ribozyme (catalytic RNA) dynamics to optimize enzyme function in synthetic pathways.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Validation scope**: Correlations are strong (r=0.875) but not perfect; high-flexibility molecules drop to r=0.766, suggesting the model struggles with large conformational transitions.
2. **All-atom detail**: Scaling to longer RNAs or RNA-protein complexes is unclear; computational cost and data requirements may grow prohibitively.
3. **Static-to-dynamics extrapolation**: Predicting dynamics from a single static structure assumes sufficient information is encoded in one conformation—may fail for rare or transient states.
4. **Dataset composition**: 2,585 trajectories, while substantial, may not represent the full diversity of RNA types (viral, catalytic, regulatory) or cellular conditions (temperature, pH, salt concentration).
5. **Physical realism**: "Physical grounding" is empirically driven; the model does not solve the underlying physics equations and may violate subtle thermodynamic constraints in edge cases.
6. **Generalization**: Unclear how well RNADynNet transfers to RNA structures or sequences very different from training data.

## Open Questions
1. Can this framework extend to RNA-protein complexes or larger ribonucleoprotein assemblies, where dynamics are often critical to function?
2. How does the model perform on non-canonical RNA structures (pseudoknots, G-quadruplexes) or engineered synthetic RNAs?
3. Can dynamics fingerprints be used for inverse design—i.e., specify desired dynamics and generate RNA sequences that exhibit them?
4. What is the minimal amount of training data (or pre-training strategy) needed to adapt RNADynNet to new RNA families?
5. Can uncertainty quantification be added to trajectory generation, so users know when predictions are unreliable?
6. Does this scale to millisecond-timescale dynamics, or is it fundamentally limited to nanosecond regimes captured in the training data?

## Key Takeaway
A unified neural network learns to generate realistic RNA motion trajectories and extract dynamics patterns from static structures, enabling fast prediction of molecular flexibility without expensive simulation.
