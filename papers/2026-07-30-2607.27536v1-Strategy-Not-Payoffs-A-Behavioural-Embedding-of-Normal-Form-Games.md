# Strategy, Not Payoffs: A Behavioural Embedding of Normal-Form Games

**Authors:** Joshua Caiata, Sreepriya Pulyassary, Xiang Li, Kate Larson
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.27536v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27536v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates how large language models transfer strategic reasoning skills across different games when fine-tuned on one game versus another. The authors propose a lightweight embedding based on two behavioral features—Nash equilibrium entropy and response sensitivity—that successfully predicts whether learning one game will help or hurt performance on another, contrary to approaches that rely on payoff structures. The work matters because it reveals that strategic transfer in LLMs depends on the *type of decision-making required* rather than the numerical payoff matrices.

## Theoretical Background
Normal-form games are simultaneous-move strategic interactions where each player has a fixed set of actions and payoffs depend jointly on all players' choices. A Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player can unilaterally improve by deviating. Existing game embeddings attempt to represent games as vectors in a learned space, usually by capturing payoff structure (what outcomes are worth). This paper builds on transfer learning—the observation that knowledge from one task improves (or sometimes worsens) performance on another—and applies it to strategic reasoning. The behavioral embedding approach diverges from structural embeddings by focusing on the *decision-making demands* (e.g., how uncertain the equilibrium is, how much your optimal move depends on reading your opponent) rather than the numbers in the payoff matrix.

## Core Contributions
1. **Demonstration that transfer is behavior-driven, not payoff-driven**: Fine-tuning effects on LLM strategic capabilities are explained by the strategic structure LLMs must learn (entropy, response sensitivity) rather than payoff magnitudes or geometry.
2. **A lightweight two-feature behavioral embedding**: Entropy of Nash equilibrium + sensitivity of optimal responses to opponent action. Minimal yet interpretable.
3. **Empirical validation on held-out games**: The behavioral embedding reliably predicts transfer success on unseen games, while existing structural embeddings fail to generalize and merely memorize identities.
4. **Contrast with prior work**: Shows that published structural embeddings overfit to training games and offer no predictive power.

## Practical Applications
1. **Strategic AI agent design**: Predicting which games to train an LLM-based negotiator or poker-playing agent on first to maximize downstream performance on unknown strategic tasks.
2. **Curriculum learning for game-playing systems**: Automatically order training games to maximize transfer and avoid negative transfer (where training on one game makes another harder).
3. **Corporate/organizational game playing**: Fine-tuning LLMs for competitive scenarios (auction bidding, pricing dynamics, contract negotiation) by selecting training games whose behavioral demands match the target task.
4. **Robustness testing**: Identifying which games, if memorized poorly during training, will most damage performance on unseen strategic interactions.
5. **Game design for AI training**: When training agents for a specific strategic domain, select games with matching behavioral signatures rather than similar payoff matrices.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Strategic Transfer Optimizer**: SaaS tool that predicts which game-like training scenarios to use for LLMs entering a specific competitive domain (negotiation, pricing, auction). Input: target game signature. Output: recommended training games and expected performance gains.
2. **Behavioral Game Recommender**: Platform that ingests a novel game or strategic scenario and recommends pre-trained LLM checkpoints (trained on games with matching behavioral embeddings) for immediate fine-tuning, reducing compute cost.
3. **Curriculum-as-a-Service for Multi-Agent RL**: Automatically sequence multi-agent games for corporate training simulations (sales teams, trading floors, supply chain) based on behavioral compatibility to maximize transfer.
4. **Game Similarity Index for AI Audit**: Tool for risk managers and AI safety teams to measure whether two games have similar behavioral demands—useful for certifying that a game-playing model trained in simulation will transfer to a real competitive scenario without catastrophic negative transfer.
5. **Strategic Capability Profiler**: Product that characterizes an LLM's strategic strengths (e.g., "this model excels at high-entropy equilibria but struggles with strict best-response games") and matches it to target applications.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited to normal-form games**: Results are demonstrated on simultaneous-move games with explicit payoffs. No evidence the approach scales to extensive-form games (sequential moves), imperfect information, or repeated interactions.
2. **Two-feature model may oversimplify**: Entropy and response sensitivity are minimal; there may be other behavioral factors (e.g., dominance structure, symmetry, number of players) that matter in practice.
3. **LLM-specific**: Unclear whether results generalize to other architectures (RL agents, symbolic solvers) or whether behavioral embeddings are an artifact of how transformers process game descriptions.
4. **No explanation for *why* behavioral features transfer**: The paper predicts transfer but doesn't mechanistically explain what the LLM learns about entropy or sensitivity that then ports to new games.
5. **Potential data leakage**: If test games are similar in distribution to training games, good generalization might still reflect memorization rather than true compositional understanding.
6. **Scalability unclear**: Computational cost and performance on games with very large action spaces not discussed.

## Open Questions
1. **Extensive-form games**: Do behavioral embeddings generalize to sequential games with history-dependent moves and imperfect information?
2. **Mechanistic explanation**: What internal representations in the LLM correspond to entropy and response sensitivity? Can we intervene on these representations?
3. **Other behavioral features**: What other decision-making properties (dominance structure, player symmetry, payoff correlations) should be included in a richer behavioral embedding?
4. **Negative transfer prevention**: Can we predict *when* fine-tuning on game A will hurt performance on game B, and how to mitigate it?
5. **Non-LLM agents**: Do behavioral embeddings work for RL agents, symbolic game-theoretic solvers, or human-agent teams?
6. **Real-world validation**: Do these predictions hold for actual strategic domains (negotiation, auction, pricing) with human or hybrid human-AI participants?
7. **Embedding robustness**: How sensitive are predictions to the specific choice of the two features? Should this be a learned embedding rather than hand-crafted?

## Key Takeaway
LLM strategic transfer depends on behavioral task structure, not payoff numbers—and this can be predicted with just two features: equilibrium entropy and response sensitivity.
