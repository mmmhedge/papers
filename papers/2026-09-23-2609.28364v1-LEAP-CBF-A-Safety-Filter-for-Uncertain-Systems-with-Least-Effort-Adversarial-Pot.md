# LEAP-CBF: A Safety Filter for Uncertain Systems with Least-Effort Adversarial Potentials

**Authors:** Oswin So, Eric Yu, Chuchu Fan
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28364v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28364v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Least-Effort Adversarial Potentials (LEAP), a new safety verification method for uncertain robotic systems. Instead of designing overly conservative safety filters, LEAP measures how much effort a disturbance must exert to break safety guarantees—then uses deep reinforcement learning to compute these robustness certificates efficiently. The approach is validated on physical robots (quadrupeds and drones), showing it handles real-world uncertainties better than existing methods.

## Theoretical Background
Control barrier functions (CBFs) are certificates that guarantee a system stays in a safe region by constraining its control inputs. Standard CBFs work well for perfectly known systems, but fail when disturbances are present—leading to "robust CBF" variants that add safety margins. These robust variants are overly conservative (especially in high dimensions) because they must guard against worst-case disturbances without knowing their true magnitude or direction. This paper builds on control theory foundations (Lyapunov stability, barrier functions) and combines them with deep reinforcement learning—a method where agents learn policies by trial-and-error to maximize cumulative rewards. The key insight: instead of worst-case safety analysis, compute the minimum effort required for a disturbance to cause failure; this gives a more honest robustness measure.

## Core Contributions
1. **LEAP formulation**: A new robustness certificate defined as the minimum cumulative cost (or "effort") needed for a disturbance to violate a safety constraint. LEAP is both a valid CBF for nominal systems and extends to disturbance-robust settings.
2. **RL-based construction**: An on-policy deep reinforcement learning algorithm to compute LEAPs by having an "adversary" agent learn the most efficient attack strategy against a system.
3. **Hardware validation**: Demonstrations on real quadrupeds and quadrotors, not just simulation—showing the method handles actual sensor noise, model mismatch, and actuator delays.
4. **Comparison with robust CBF**: Empirical evidence that LEAP is less conservative than traditional robust variants while maintaining safety guarantees.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Control Barrier Functions]], robust control theory, and adversarial reinforcement learning. It relates to [[Adversarial Training]] (learning against worst-case perturbations) but differs by quantifying perturbation cost rather than treating all disturbances equally. The RL construction echoes methods in [[Reinforcement Learning|safe RL]] and [[Robust Control]] that use game-theoretic or min-max optimization. Unlike [[RLHF]] or alignment work, this targets safety in physical systems rather than language models, but shares the philosophy of learning robustness properties rather than hand-designing them.

## Practical Applications
1. **Autonomous vehicles**: Guaranteeing safety under sensor noise, wind, and actuator delays without overly restrictive speed/maneuver constraints.
2. **Collaborative robotics**: Robots working near humans in factories can certify safety margins proportional to actual uncertainty, allowing tighter, more productive spacing.
3. **Drone swarm coordination**: Multi-agent systems with communication delays; LEAP quantifies how much disturbance the swarm can tolerate before collision.
4. **Legged robot locomotion**: Quadrupeds navigating rough terrain; LEAP adapts safety margins to terrain uncertainty (soft ground, inclines) rather than one global bound.
5. **Surgical robotics**: Physicians operating robots remotely; LEAP bounds the effort an adversarial perturbation (hand tremor, network lag) needs to cause harm.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RobotOS Safety Module**: A plug-and-play software layer that robots integrate at startup; it learns LEAP certificates for their specific hardware/environment, reducing deployment time and enabling tighter control policies.
2. **Insurance & Certification for Autonomous Systems**: Compute LEAP scores for robots before deployment; use them as an objective safety metric for insurance underwriting and regulatory approval.
3. **Simulation-to-Real Bootcamp Service**: Cloud-based service that trains LEAP for customer robots in simulation, then validates on shared hardware testbeds before shipping to clients.
4. **Disturbance-Aware Motion Planning**: Commercial middleware for robot planners that requests real-time LEAP estimates and adjusts task speed/trajectory to stay within safety margins given measured sensor noise.
5. **Digital Twin Certification**: Sell digital twin software that co-trains with robots in the field, continuously refining LEAP estimates to match actual disturbance distributions and alert operators when conditions exceed learned bounds.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational cost of RL training**: On-policy RL requires many environment interactions; for new robots/environments, this can be slow. No guidance on when learned LEAP generalizes to unseen systems.
2. **Bounded effort assumption**: LEAP assumes disturbances have bounded cumulative cost. It will fail silently if real disturbances exceed this bound (e.g., if adversary has much higher power than expected).
3. **State-space dimensionality**: Experiments are on relatively low-dimensional systems (quadrupeds, drones). Scalability to high-dimensional state spaces (e.g., humanoid robots with many joints) is unclear.
4. **No formal guarantees from learned certificates**: The RL-trained LEAP is approximate, not analytically proven. If training fails or exploration is incomplete, the safety certificate may be wrong.
5. **Distribution shift**: LEAP learned in one environment (lab) may not transfer to different conditions (outdoors, different floor friction, etc.).

## Open Questions
1. How does LEAP performance degrade as system dimensionality increases? Can the method scale to 20+ DOF systems?
2. Can LEAP be transferred across similar robot morphologies without retraining, or must it be learned per-instance?
3. What is the relationship between the effort bound and real-world disturbance distributions? How would you set effort thresholds in practice?
4. Can LEAP be composed for hierarchical systems (e.g., a fleet of drones)? How does robustness propagate up the hierarchy?
5. How sensitive is LEAP to the choice of effort metric (L2 norm, energy, time)? Should the metric be task-dependent?
6. Can formal analysis prove that the learned LEAP satisfies the safety certificate definition (or characterize the approximation error)?

## Key Takeaway
Robots can learn robustness certificates that measure how hard disturbances must work to break safety, enabling tighter real-world control than worst-case methods.
