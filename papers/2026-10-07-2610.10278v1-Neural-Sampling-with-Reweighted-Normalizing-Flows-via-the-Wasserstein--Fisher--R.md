# Neural Sampling with Reweighted Normalizing Flows via the Wasserstein--Fisher--Rao JKO Scheme

**Authors:** Chenguang Duan, Johannes Hertrich, Gabriele Steidl
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10278v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10278v1)

## Summary
This paper presents a neural algorithm for sampling from complex probability distributions (specified by unnormalized Boltzmann densities) using a geometric approach called the Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao JKO scheme. The key innovation is combining theoretical convergence guarantees—which work without restrictive assumptions like log-concavity—with a practical neural implementation using normalizing flows. This matters because sampling from complicated, multimodal distributions is a fundamental problem across machine learning, statistics, and physics simulations.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on several classical ideas. The **Jordan–Kinderlehrer–Otto (JKO) scheme** is a time-discretization method for gradient flows in probability space—it's a way to iteratively move a distribution toward a target by taking small, geometry-aware steps. Traditionally, JKO uses **Wasserstein geometry** (optimal transport distances). Here, the authors switch to **Wasserstein–Fisher–Rao (WFR) geometry**, which combines optimal transport with a mass-balance term, making the scheme more flexible. **Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence** measures how different two probability distributions are. **Normalizing flows** are neural networks that transform simple distributions (like Gaussians) into complex ones via invertible maps. The paper's theoretical contribution proves that iterating this scheme exponentially fast approaches the target distribution without needing strong assumptions like **log-concavity** (which many real distributions violate).

## Core Contributions
1. **Convergence guarantee**: Proves that the exact WFR JKO iterates converge exponentially fast to the target for any fixed step size, with no log-concavity or spectral gap assumptions—a genuine generalization over prior results that required such structural assumptions.

2. **Neural implementation**: Develops a practical algorithm that implements WFR JKO by parametrizing both transport and reaction components as **reweighted normalizing flows**—enabling end-to-end differentiable sampling without expensive density ratio estimation.

3. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates on challenging multimodal target distributions that the approach outperforms or matches existing sampling methods.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to:
- **[[Normalizing Flows]]** and **[[Variational Inference]]**: both use invertible neural networks to approximate complex distributions.
- **[[Score-Based Diffusion Models]]**: alternative neural sampling methods that also use iterative refinement, though with different geometry.
- **[[Optimal Transport]]**: the Wasserstein geometry here is grounded in optimal transport theory.
- **[[Metropolis–Hastings]] and MCMC**: classical sampling methods this aims to improve upon for high-dimensional problems.
- **[[Neural Density Estimation]]**: the broader category of learning distributions with neural networks.

The WFR geometry is less common than pure Wasserstein; this paper argues it's better for sampling because the reaction term naturally handles probability mass changes.

## Practical Applications
- **Bayesian inference**: Sampling from posterior distributions in probabilistic modeling (e.g., variational Bayes, approximate inference in graphical models).
- **Molecular dynamics & physics simulations**: Sampling conformations of biomolecules or particles from energy potentials (Boltzmann distributions).
- **Generative modeling**: Training generative models that must sample from complex, multimodal targets (e.g., image generation, molecule design).
- **Variational autoencoders (VAEs) and structured generative models**: initializing or improving the inference network.
- **Anomaly detection**: sampling from learned density models to compute likelihood or sample plausible alternatives.

## Technical Limitations
- **Curse of dimensionality**: No explicit analysis of how the number of iterations or flow complexity scales with dimension—high-D problems may still be intractable.
- **Hyperparameter tuning**: Step size, normalizing flow architecture, and reweighting scheme all require tuning; no principled guidance given.
- **Unnormalized densities only**: Assumes you can evaluate the log-density (up to a constant); if only samples or black-box queries available, inapplicable.
- **Neural overhead**: Requires training normalizing flows; for low-dimensional or simple targets, classical MCMC may be faster in practice.
- **Convergence proof is asymptotic**: Guarantees exponential convergence as iterations → ∞, but finite-sample convergence rates and bias-variance tradeoffs are not characterized.
- **Limited to continuous distributions**: Discrete or mixed-type distributions not addressed.

## Open Questions
- How does performance degrade when the normalizing flow is misspecified or under-parameterized?
- Can you provide finite-iteration convergence rates and dimension-dependent complexity bounds?
- How does WFR geometry compare to standard Wasserstein JKO or other divergences (e.g., Fisher, Hellinger) in high dimensions?
- Can the method be extended to sample from Gibbs measures with non-convex potentials or time-varying targets?
- Is there a principled way to choose step size adaptively during iterations?
- How does reweighting affect gradient flow through the normalizing flow, and can you reduce reweighting variance?

## Key Takeaway
Neural algorithm for sampling from complex distributions by combining geometric optimization theory with learned normalizing flows—no log-concavity required.
