# The Cost of Classical Multi-Agent Path Finding

**Authors:** Alvin Combrink, Sabino Francesco Roselli, Martin Fabian
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10100v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10100v1)

## Summary
This paper quantifies the performance gap between classical Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF)—which assumes discrete time steps and point agents on graphs—and continuous-time MAPF, which relaxes these simplifications. The work shows that classical MAPF's restrictive assumptions cost 5–20% solution quality depending on environment structure, and that simply increasing map resolution recovers almost none of this loss, revealing fundamental limits rather than computational trade-offs.

## Theoretical Background
Multi-Agent Path Finding addresses the problem of computing collision-free routes for multiple agents moving simultaneously through shared spaces. Classical MAPF simplifies the problem by discretizing time into steps and treating agents as points moving on graph nodes, assumptions that make algorithmic search tractable but may misrepresent real physical motion. Continuous-time MAPF (MAPF_R) relaxes these constraints—allowing agents to have physical extent (radius) and move continuously through space. The paper builds on decades of MAPF research (including methods like conflict-based search and prioritized planning) and compares outcomes empirically across problem instances.

## Core Contributions
1. **Quantification of the classical MAPF penalty**: Systematic empirical measurement of solution quality loss across different map types, agent counts, and sizes—the first rigorous characterization of this gap.
2. **Isolation of cost drivers**: Shows that continuous time and agent geometry *individually* contribute little value; their power comes from enabling richer motion primitives (e.g., diagonal or diagonal-like moves at arbitrary times).
3. **Resolution scaling analysis**: Demonstrates that increasing classical MAPF's map resolution recovers <3% of forfeited quality, proving the gap is structural rather than computational.
4. **Context-dependent guidance**: Identifies when classical MAPF is acceptable (narrow, constrained environments) versus when continuous-time methods are essential (open spaces).

## Connections to Other Work
This work contrasts with the classical MAPF literature (which focuses on algorithmic speedups for the discrete formulation) by questioning whether the formulation itself is the bottleneck. It relates to broader [[optimization]] work on problem relaxations and computational complexity. The empirical comparison structure mirrors benchmark-driven work in [[agents]] and [[multi-agent-systems]], and the insight that motion primitives matter echoes research in robotics and physical simulation.

## Practical Applications
- **Autonomous warehouse systems**: Determining whether to upgrade from discrete-time controllers to continuous motion planning, and expected performance gain.
- **Drone swarm coordination**: Understanding cost of simplifying to grid-based assumptions for real aerial agents with size and continuous dynamics.
- **Traffic simulation and urban planning**: Assessing when simplified traffic models are sufficient versus when fine-grained continuous modeling is mandatory.
- **Robot manufacturing floors**: Deciding whether discrete scheduling algorithms meet quality thresholds or require richer motion planning.

## Technical Limitations
- **Experimental scope**: Comparison limited to specific map topologies and agent configurations; results may not generalize to highly irregular or 3D environments.
- **No algorithmic innovation**: The paper compares problem formulations but does not propose new solvers for MAPF_R; continuous-time methods themselves may be computationally harder or slower in practice.
- **Discrete agent sizes**: Study focuses on circular agents; irregular shapes or orientation-dependent collisions are not addressed.
- **Scalability questions**: Unclear how large the agent counts or map sizes can grow before continuous-time methods become intractable, limiting practical deployment guidance.
- **Assumption on motion**: Assumes agents can execute any feasible continuous path; does not model actuator constraints, acceleration limits, or other robot dynamics.

## Open Questions
- How do results scale to 3D environments or highly non-Euclidean spaces (e.g., multi-level warehouses)?
- Can hybrid approaches (mixing discrete and continuous reasoning) recover most continuous gains at lower computational cost?
- How sensitive are the findings to agent speed, size distribution, and density?
- What is the actual wall-clock time cost of solving MAPF_R compared to classical MAPF, and at what problem scale does it become prohibitive?
- Do modern learning-based MAPF methods (neural networks) exhibit similar gaps, or do they implicitly learn to overcome classical formulation limits?

## Key Takeaway
Classical multi-agent path finding forfeits 5–20% solution quality due to discretization, a gap that more resolution cannot close—justifying continuous models for open spaces.
