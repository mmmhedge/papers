# Loss Choice or Model Choice? The Role of Forecast Level in Cryptocurrency Volatility Forecasting

**Authors:** Andrzej Tokajuk, Jarosław A. Chudziak
**Published:** 2026-09-22 | **arXiv:** [2609.27024v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27024v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether loss function choice or model architecture matters more for cryptocurrency volatility forecasting. The authors show that when you adjust for the overall forecast level (a hidden factor that makes losses appear more important than they are), model selection becomes the primary driver of forecasting quality, while loss choice has less influence than raw comparisons suggest.

## Theoretical Background
Volatility forecasting is central to financial risk management — it estimates how much an asset's price will fluctuate, which drives decisions on hedging, position sizing, and capital allocation. Traditional studies compare forecasting models (neural networks, GARCH variants, etc.) by training them with different loss functions (like mean squared error or quantile loss) and comparing their test-set performance. However, different loss functions inherently push models toward different overall forecast levels — some naturally produce higher or lower volatility estimates on average. This creates confounding: when one loss-and-model combination outperforms another, you cannot easily tell whether the win came from the loss function itself or merely from its target forecast level.

## Core Contributions
1. **Decomposition of forecast-level effects**: The paper introduces validation-based alignment, a technique that adjusts the average forecast level before comparison, isolating the true differences in day-to-day forecast quality.
2. **Empirical re-ranking under alignment**: Seven loss functions and five models are compared across major cryptocurrencies. Raw comparisons show large score variation across losses; after level adjustment, model choice emerges as the dominant source of variation.
3. **Risk-relevant evaluation**: The analysis includes Value-at-Risk (VaR) breach rates, showing that loss-driven differences in raw breaches largely vanish after alignment, suggesting forecast level is the key confound for downstream risk decisions.

## Practical Applications
- **Portfolio risk management**: Hedge funds and asset managers can use this insight to prioritize architectural improvements over loss tuning when building volatility models, saving development time.
- **Cryptocurrency exchanges**: Margin lending and liquidation thresholds depend on volatility forecasts; understanding the true driver of forecast quality (model vs. loss) helps set more stable risk parameters.
- **Risk capital allocation**: Banks and trading firms can allocate engineering resources more efficiently by focusing on model selection and validation-based calibration rather than exhaustive loss function tuning.
- **Backtesting frameworks**: This work provides a methodology for fairly comparing forecasting systems in production, reducing spurious conclusions from raw metric comparisons.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Volatility Forecast Benchmarking SaaS**: A platform that ingests user-trained volatility models, applies validation-based alignment, and produces fair, risk-adjusted performance comparisons to guide model selection.
2. **Cryptocurrency Risk Dashboard**: Real-time VaR and volatility forecasts for exchanges and traders, with transparent calibration checks to ensure forecast levels match realized volatility.

## Technical Limitations
- **Crypto-specific findings**: Results are drawn from cryptocurrencies only; volatility dynamics may differ in equities, FX, or commodities, limiting generalizability.
- **Limited model scope**: Only five models are compared; the generality of the finding (model > loss in importance) may depend on the models chosen.
- **Validation-based alignment assumes stability**: The alignment procedure assumes that forecast-level biases remain constant over time; in regime shifts or market dislocations, this may break.
- **VaR as risk metric**: VaR is a point estimate and does not capture tail risk beyond the breach threshold; expected shortfall or other tail metrics might tell a different story.
- **No discussion of computational cost**: The paper does not weigh model performance against training time or inference latency, which matters for production deployment.

## Open Questions
- Does the dominance of model choice over loss choice hold in less liquid markets (smaller cryptocurrencies, less frequent data)?
- How do these findings change with different validation splits or time-series cross-validation schemes?
- Can a single, universally optimal loss function be designed that obviates the need for loss tuning, or are losses inherently task-specific?
- How much of the remaining model-driven variation is explainable by architectural choices (e.g., depth, attention mechanisms) versus hyperparameter tuning?
- Does this framework apply to other financial forecasting tasks (price direction, realized variance, volume)?

## Key Takeaway
Model architecture, not loss function choice, drives volatility forecast quality once forecast levels are properly aligned.
