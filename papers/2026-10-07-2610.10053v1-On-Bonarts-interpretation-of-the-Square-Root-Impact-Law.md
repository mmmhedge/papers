# On Bonart's interpretation of the Square-Root Impact Law

**Authors:** J. -P. Bouchaud, I. Mastromatteo, B. Toth
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10053v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10053v1)

## Summary

This paper examines the square-root impact law (SRIL) — an empirical regularity stating that large trades move prices by an amount proportional to the square root of volume traded — and evaluates Bonart's recent theoretical explanation for why this relationship holds. The authors show that while Bonart's mechanism (assuming market prices whiten incoming order streams) is elegant, it requires unrealistic assumptions about how markets identify and attribute anonymous trades, and the theory fails to match observed behavior for isolated, time-separated orders.

## Theoretical Background

Market impact is the price movement caused by executing a trade. The square-root impact law emerged as an empirical stylized fact: when a trader executes order quantity $Q$ in a market with daily volume $V$ and volatility $\sigma$, the typical price impact scales as $I \propto \sigma\sqrt{Q/V}$. 

Bonart proposed that this follows if: (1) both realized and hypothetical counterfactual prices evolve diffusively (as random walks), and (2) for information-neutral market impact, the increments must be "white" (uncorrelated over time). Under these assumptions, the SRIL emerges naturally. The paper builds on the [[Lillo-Mike-Farmer model]], a foundational multi-agent framework for understanding price formation and market impact, and invokes [[volume conservation]] arguments from statistical mechanics to constrain possible impact functional forms.

## Core Contributions

1. **Reformulation and simplification** of Bonart's argument, making explicit the key assumption: markets must whiten the *stream* of each participant's orders over time, not just isolated trades.

2. **Identification of a critical failure mode**: The LMF model satisfies Bonart's diffusivity premises but predicts super-square-root scaling ($\varphi^{(1-\gamma)/2}Q^{(1+\gamma)/2}$) for isolated metaorders because single orders are statistically invisible to the market.

3. **Construction of an explicit multi-agent propagator** that realizes Bonart's whitening mechanism and reproduces the clean SRIL — but only when the market perfectly re-attributes anonymous trades to their originators.

4. **Demonstration that perfect trade attribution is implausible**: The signal-processing task required to identify which trader placed which order is computationally and informationally infeasible in practice.

5. **Sharp, falsifiable prediction from the stream hypothesis**: Isolated metaorders well-separated in time should violate the SRIL, but empirical evidence does not support this prediction.

6. **Volume-conservation argument** that uniquely singles out the square-root form and highlights a conceptual gap: diffusivity pins down the *variance* of impact (additive and age-blind), but SRIL describes the *mean* impact (which decays with age as $1/\sqrt{\text{age}}$), requiring an explicit time origin.

## Practical Applications

1. **High-frequency trading algorithm design**: Traders can refine execution algorithms knowing that the SRIL may not hold for isolated orders or markets with poor attribution mechanisms, allowing better impact prediction on short timescales.

2. **Market microstructure forensics**: Regulators and compliance teams can use the falsifiable predictions (e.g., isolated orders violating SRIL) to detect and investigate suspicious trading patterns.

3. **Liquidity provision optimization**: Market makers can adjust pricing for metaorder streams vs. isolated orders differently if the stream hypothesis correctly captures market behavior, improving bid-ask spreads.

4. **Risk management for large trades**: Portfolio managers executing large positions can more accurately forecast execution costs by understanding whether their order is part of a detectable stream or effectively anonymous.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **Smart execution analytics platform**: Real-time impact prediction engine that distinguishes between streaming vs. isolated orders and adjusts execution recommendations accordingly, particularly for institutional clients.

2. **Market microstructure intelligence service**: Subscription tool that estimates trade attribution probabilities and reveals how well exchanges/venues actually identify traders, helping quantitative firms exploit markets with poor signal processing.

3. **Regulatory surveillance SaaS**: Deploy the falsifiable SRIL predictions (isolated orders violating square-root scaling) as an automated marker for suspicious trading, sold to exchanges and compliance teams.

4. **Liquidity cost benchmarking tool**: SaaS that measures whether a specific venue's impact law deviates from SRIL (suggesting poor trade attribution or unusual market structure) and helps clients choose optimal execution venues.

5. **Agent-based market simulator**: Software that lets traders test execution strategies under different assumptions about market attribution capability, revealing which venues have Bonart-like properties and which don't.

## Technical Limitations

1. **Unrealistic attribution assumption**: The model requires near-perfect re-identification of anonymous trades to their originators — markets almost certainly cannot achieve this, severely limiting the theory's empirical scope.

2. **Stream vs. isolated distinction is blurry in practice**: The paper draws a sharp line between metaorders that are part of a detectable stream and those that are isolated, but real trading exhibits a continuum and depends heavily on time horizons and market information sets.

3. **Diffusivity assumption may not hold**: Prices may not follow true diffusive processes, especially at high frequencies or during stress periods; the SRIL derivation becomes fragile if this breaks.

4. **Empirical falsifiability unclear**: The prediction that isolated orders violate SRIL lacks clear statistical tests — what constitutes "well-separated in time"? How much departure from square-root scaling counts as evidence?

5. **Multi-agent complexity underspecified**: The explicit propagator construction is presented but the paper does not fully characterize which multi-agent models satisfy Bonart's premises, leaving the space of consistent theories large.

6. **Age-dependence of impact decays**: The observation that mean impact decays as $1/\sqrt{\text{age}}$ is noted but not deeply integrated into a unified theory; the gap between variance and mean remains conceptually.

## Open Questions

1. **Empirical test design**: How should we rigorously test whether isolated, time-separated metaorders violate SRIL? What time separation threshold and sample size are required for statistical power?

2. **Attribution in real markets**: Can we measure how well real exchanges actually attribute trades to participants? How does this vary across venues and asset classes?

3. **Stream detection and learning**: If markets do learn to identify participants' trading patterns, what is the learning timescale? Can this be measured empirically?

4. **Age-dependent impact theory**: Can a unified framework explain both the variance (constant/additive) and mean (decaying $\sim 1/\sqrt{\text{age}}$) of impact in a natural way?

5. **Robustness to realistic noise**: How does the theory change if we relax diffusivity, allow partial attribution, or introduce information asymmetries? Which results are robust?

6. **Comparison across asset classes**: Does SRIL hold equally well in equities, FX, and fixed income? Do attribution mechanisms vary sufficiently to explain cross-asset differences?

## Key Takeaway

The square-root impact law is elegant but requires markets to perfectly identify traders—a task empirically implausible and theoretically unnecessary to explain observed price movements.
