# SciExam for ENSO: Can AI Agents Build Climate Models?

**Authors:** Yinling Zhang, Langchen Liu, Dongbin Xiu, Xueyan Zou, Xu Kuang et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10513v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10513v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces SciExam for ENSO, a benchmark that tests whether AI agents can autonomously build climate models from real observational data—without a predetermined correct answer. Twelve agent systems attempted the task, and six produced models that outperformed a published baseline, suggesting AI can conduct meaningful open-ended scientific research with verifiable, real-world constraints rather than subjective grading.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two emerging areas: (1) **LLM-based agents** — AI systems that break down complex tasks into steps, write code, and iterate based on feedback — and (2) **climate modeling**, particularly the El Niño-Southern Oscillation (ENSO), which is Earth's dominant year-to-year climate pattern driven by ocean-atmosphere interactions. Traditional agent evaluation relies on language-model reviewers or fixed rubrics, which cannot validate whether a newly discovered scientific model is genuinely correct. ENSO modeling itself sits in a long-standing scientific debate: whether the warm-cold asymmetry in El Niño behavior stems from deterministic nonlinear dynamics or stochastic forcing — two competing theories with no settled answer.

## Core Contributions
1. **Novel evaluation framework**: SciExam is the first benchmark that grades agent-built scientific models against hidden test criteria (reproduction of observed statistics, recovery of unobserved variables, out-of-sample forecasting accuracy) rather than against a known ground truth or human rubric. This sidesteps the "who judges the judge?" problem in open-ended science.

2. **Agent-discovered models with scientific relevance**: Six of twelve agents produced models that exceeded a published baseline. Crucially, the simplified forms of top-performing models align with competing theories in the ENSO literature that the task never explicitly mentioned — suggesting agents inferred scientifically meaningful structures.

3. **Ablation and control experiments**: Controlled runs show that top agents do not simply memorize the observational record; their model structures respond to the information they receive, indicating genuine modeling behavior rather than retrieval.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[agents]] (autonomous systems decomposing and solving problems), [[large-language-models]] (foundation models like GPT-4 used to drive reasoning), and climate science. It relates to broader questions about:
- **AI for science**: Can LLMs discover new physical laws or just optimize known ones?
- **Agent benchmarking**: How do we evaluate agents on tasks with no ground truth? (cf. open-ended research tasks versus constrained benchmarks like SQuAD or MMLU)
- **Scientific reproducibility and validation**: The hidden-grader design echoes peer-review workflows but at machine speed.

The ENSO asymmetry debate it touches is rooted in decades of climate literature; the paper shows AI agents can implicitly discover structure aligned with competing human theories.

## Practical Applications
1. **Accelerated climate model discovery**: Agents could rapidly prototype low-order models for other climate phenomena (monsoons, Atlantic Multidecadal Oscillation, sea-ice dynamics), speeding hypothesis generation for climate scientists.

2. **Hybrid human-AI science teams**: Scientists could set observational constraints and let agents propose candidate models, then curate and test the best ones, reducing engineering overhead.

3. **Educational climate science**: Universities could use SciExam-style tasks to teach students how to build and validate models from data, with AI agents as exemplars or sparring partners.

4. **Early-warning systems for extreme events**: Improved ENSO models (especially if agents find better forecasting forms) could refine El Niño and La Niña predictions, informing agriculture, fisheries, and disaster preparedness in vulnerable regions.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **SciExam-as-a-Service platform**: A web service where researchers submit observational datasets and define hidden test criteria, and autonomous agents compete to build models. Pricing per task; scientists access agent-generated models and code.

2. **Climate Model Autopilot**: A tool for climate labs that ingests raw observational data, automatically preprocesses it, runs multiple agent systems in parallel, and produces ranked candidate models with explainability reports — reducing months of manual modeling to days.

3. **Scientific Hypothesis Marketplace**: A platform where agents run open-ended science tasks (like SciExam), publish their findings, and human experts peer-review and rate them. Top agents earn reputation; labs license their code.

4. **Domain-specific Agent Training**: A consultancy that fine-tunes and specializes LLM agents for specific scientific domains (biology, materials science, atmospheric physics), using SciExam-style benchmarks to measure progress and sell custom agents to research institutes.

5. **Forecasting-as-a-Service for climate**: Deploy agent-discovered ENSO models and similar climate models as APIs for insurance companies, agricultural traders, and supply-chain managers to consume real-time climate forecasts.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Simplification vs. realism**: Agents built low-order stochastic models, not full GCMs (general circulation models). Real climate forecasting often needs higher-dimensional physics; generalization to complex systems is unclear.

2. **Observational data sufficiency**: The task assumes six hours of computation and quality observations are enough to identify causal structure. In domains with sparse or noisy data (e.g., exoplanet atmospheres, deep-sea biology), agent performance may collapse.

3. **Frozen diagnostics constraint**: Once agents chose their diagnostics, they were locked in. Real science is iterative — better diagnostics might emerge. This artificial constraint may limit agent flexibility.

4. **Baseline comparison**: The published model is not an ensemble or expert-consensus baseline; we don't know if human teams with similar time and data would outperform or underperform the agents.

5. **Interpretability of agent reasoning**: The paper doesn't deeply expose *why* agents chose certain model structures — much of the decision-making is opaque, limiting how much scientists can learn from the process.

6. **Generalization to other domains**: Success on ENSO doesn't guarantee agents can build models in domains with fewer observational constraints or longer timescales (e.g., slow climate feedbacks over centuries).

## Open Questions
1. **Can agents discover truly novel physics?** The best models align with existing theories; did agents invent new causal mechanisms, or just rediscover known ones?

2. **How sensitive is agent performance to computational budget and data quality?** What happens with less training time or noisier observations?

3. **Can we make agent reasoning interpretable?** What prompts or architectural changes would expose the causal reasoning agents use to build models?

4. **Scaling to high-dimensional systems**: How do agents perform on climate phenomena with spatial structure (monsoons, storm formation), not just temporal modes like ENSO?

5. **Real-time forecasting**: Do agent-discovered models outperform operational forecasting systems (e.g., NOAA's statistical and dynamical models) when tested on recent, withheld years?

6. **Multi-agent collaboration**: Would teams of agents debating model structure outperform single agents? Can they implicitly discover competing theories and reconcile them?

7. **Transfer learning**: Can agents trained on ENSO quickly adapt to other oscillatory climate modes with minimal retraining?

## Key Takeaway
AI agents can build climate models from real data that rival expert baselines and align with unmentioned scientific theories, suggesting genuine autonomous research capability.
