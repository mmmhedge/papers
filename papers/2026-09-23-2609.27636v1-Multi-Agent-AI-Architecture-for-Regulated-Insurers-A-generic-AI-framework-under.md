# Multi-Agent AI Architecture for Regulated Insurers: A generic AI framework under Solvency II and the AI Act in Austria and Germany

**Authors:** Walter Kurz
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.27636v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27636v1)

## Summary
This paper designs a formal multi-agent AI system for insurance companies operating under strict European regulations (Solvency II, AI Act). It breaks down an insurer into specialized agents—underwriting, claims, compliance, fraud detection—coordinated by a central orchestrator to ensure all decisions meet regulatory requirements and risk limits. The contribution is a concrete institutional blueprint showing how to deploy AI safely in highly regulated financial firms.

## Theoretical Background
The framework rests on three classical economic theories. **Arrow's risk pooling theory** explains how insurers transform individual risks into collective pools through diversification. **Nash equilibrium** (from game theory) models strategic interactions: when multiple agents must coordinate, Nash equilibrium predicts stable outcomes where no agent benefits from unilateral deviation. **Principal-Agent theory** addresses a core problem in organizations: how to align incentives when one party (principal) delegates tasks to another (agent) who has private information or misaligned goals. The paper applies these to model insurers as constrained optimization problems operating within solvency buffers, legal boundaries, ESG commitments, and operational limits. Solvency II is the EU regulatory framework requiring insurers to hold sufficient capital reserves; the AI Act imposes transparency and risk-management rules on high-risk algorithmic systems.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal decomposition**: Breaks an insurer into distinct functional agents (capital management, underwriting, claims, compliance, fraud detection, client interaction) with explicit decision boundaries and information flows.
2. **Regulatory architecture**: Embeds Solvency II, AI Act, and Insurance Distribution Directive constraints directly into agent protocols and an orchestrator layer, making compliance verifiable and auditable.
3. **Human-in-the-loop governance**: Designs tiered access control so different roles (underwriters, compliance officers, executives) see and influence decisions proportionally to their authority.
4. **Technical protocol stack**: Specifies asynchronous communication via Model Context Protocol (MCP) and Agent-to-Agent (A2A) messaging to enable scalable inter-agent coordination.
5. **Institutional coherence**: Demonstrates how economic theory (incentive alignment, risk pooling, strategic equilibrium) translates into operational multi-agent design for Austria and Germany.

## Practical Applications
1. **Automated underwriting**: Route insurance applications through underwriting agents that propose decisions within risk appetite and regulatory capital thresholds; human agents review flagged cases.
2. **Claims triage and fraud detection**: Specialized agents screen claims for fraud, estimate reserves, and escalate to human investigators, ensuring Solvency II reserve adequacy.
3. **Compliance monitoring**: Continuous agents audit transactions, model decisions, and data flows against AI Act transparency rules; generate audit logs.
4. **Capital allocation**: Optimize reinsurance and capital deployment across business lines subject to solvency constraints, modeled as a constrained optimization agent.
5. **ESG scoring**: Agents assess policyholder portfolios or investments for Environmental, Social, Governance alignment, reporting to regulators.
6. **Client interaction**: Chatbot/LLM agents handle inquiries with guardrails to avoid giving unauthorized financial advice.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Compliance-as-a-Service for Insurers**: Packaged multi-agent reference architecture (templates, orchestrator code, audit logging) that insurers license and customize for their own AI systems. Revenue: subscription per insurer size.
2. **Regulatory AI Audit Platform**: SaaS tool that ingests logs from insurers' multi-agent systems, checks for Solvency II and AI Act violations, generates compliance reports and alerts. Target: compliance departments and external auditors.
3. **Insurance Underwriting Copilot**: Pre-built agents for underwriting and claims that plug into existing insurer systems via APIs; insurers pay per decision or per premium volume processed.
4. **AI Governance Software for Financial Firms**: Extend beyond insurance—generalize the orchestrator and governance layer to banks, asset managers, reinsurers. White-label and sell to FinTech platforms.
5. **Institutional Multi-Agent Simulation**: Consulting/software tool: help insurers model and stress-test their proposed multi-agent architecture under regulatory scenarios (e.g., solvency crises, AI Act audits) before deployment.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Absence of learning**: The paper specifies static agent architectures and rules; it does not address how agents adapt over time. Real-world insurers need to learn from claims data, market changes, and regulatory feedback.
2. **No empirical validation**: No case study, dataset, or simulation results demonstrating that the architecture actually maintains Solvency II compliance or passes AI Act audits in practice.
3. **Scalability unclear**: Coordination overhead for dozens of agents communicating via MCP/A2A is not analyzed. Could become a bottleneck.
4. **Human-in-the-loop burden**: Tiered access control assumes humans review decisions, but underwriting and claims volumes in large insurers (millions per year) may overwhelm human capacity.
5. **Regulatory specificity**: Tailored to Austria and Germany; generalization to other EU countries or jurisdictions with different regimes (e.g., UK PRA, US state insurance commissioners) requires rework.
6. **Black-box models**: The paper does not address how LLMs or deep learning models (if used by component agents) meet AI Act explainability requirements. No mechanistic interpretability approach.
7. **Incentive alignment theory vs. practice**: Nash equilibrium and Principal-Agent theory assume rational actors; real human and AI agents exhibit bounded rationality, corruption, or unexpected emergent behavior.

## Open Questions
1. **Learning and adaptation**: How should the architecture evolve as markets, regulations, and claims data change? Should agents use reinforcement learning, and if so, how to ensure learned policies remain compliant?
2. **Empirical validation**: Can this architecture be prototyped on a real or synthetic insurance dataset and shown to maintain regulatory ratios and audit trails?
3. **Scalability benchmarks**: What is the latency and throughput of the MCP/A2A stack? At what agent count does coordination fail?
4. **Emergent risks**: Can two or more agents collude or miscommunicate in ways that violate constraints? Formal verification or adversarial testing needed.
5. **LLM integration**: If agents are powered by large language models, how do you ensure their outputs meet AI Act documentation and traceability standards? Are prompt-based guardrails sufficient?
6. **Solvency stress tests**: Run multi-agent simulations under solvency stress (e.g., pandemic, market crash) to confirm that orchestrator constraints hold and agent decisions remain prudent.
7. **Generalization**: Apply the framework to banks (Basel III) or asset managers (AIFMD) and validate that the approach transfers.

## Key Takeaway
A formal blueprint for building compliant, auditable AI systems in insurance firms through multi-agent decomposition and regulatory orchestration.
