# Agentic Detection of Online Conspiracies

**Authors:** Lior Biton, Oren Tsur
**Published:** 2026-09-24 | **arXiv:** [2609.30250v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30250v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles the problem of detecting conspiracy theories in social media by recognizing not just what people say, but *why* they say it—distinguishing genuine conspiratorial endorsement from criticism, satire, or legitimate concern expressed using similar language. The authors propose an AI agent framework that can query social context (like user history, network ties, and temporal patterns) to make smarter judgments, and test it on Hebrew tweets spanning 2018–2023, showing that context-aware agents significantly outperform simple text classifiers.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two foundational concepts. First, **illocutionary force** from linguistics—the idea that the same utterance can perform different social acts depending on context (a statement like "the government is hiding something" could express genuine concern, mockery, or conspiracy endorsement). Second, it draws on recent advances in **agentic AI systems**—frameworks where language models act as reasoning agents that can call tools (retrieve data, run queries) adaptively rather than processing everything at once. The paper also engages with social media misinformation detection, which traditionally relies on lexical markers or explicit claims but struggles when conspiracy language is subtle or embedded in legitimate-seeming discourse.

## Core Contributions
1. **Frames conspiracy detection as intent inference**, not just topic classification—explicitly modeling illocutionary force as the core challenge.
2. **Proposes a tool-using agentic framework** where an LLM-based agent selectively queries social context (user posting history, network structure, temporal trends) on a per-case basis, rather than feeding all context upfront.
3. **Provides a massive, unique evaluation dataset**: 80–90% coverage of public Hebrew tweets over four years, enabling study of conspiracy discourse across multiple political cycles and crises (elections, COVID, vaccination debates).
4. **Demonstrates quantifiable gains**: context-aware agentic workflows substantially outperform text-only baselines and even non-agentic models given the same context.
5. **Analyzes efficiency tradeoffs**: discusses token economy and when the agent chooses to query context versus rely on text alone.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader efforts in [[LLM]]-based classification and [[In-Context Learning]], which show that reasoning over evidence beats static training. It also connects to work on [[Fact-Checking]] and [[Misinformation Detection]], but shifts the emphasis from veracity to *speaker intent*. The agentic design echoes recent trends in [[Tool Use]] and [[ReAct]] (Reasoning + Acting), where models decompose problems by calling external functions. Finally, it engages with research on **social embeddedness of language interpretation**—the linguistic principle that meaning depends on social context, not text alone.

## Practical Applications
1. **Election integrity monitoring**: Detecting when accounts are spreading conspiracy narratives vs. raising legitimate policy concerns during electoral campaigns.
2. **Public health communication**: Distinguishing anti-vaccine conspiracy rhetoric from genuine vaccine safety discussions or satire during health crises.
3. **Content moderation at scale**: Social platforms could use this to prioritize human review or apply context-sensitive removal policies rather than blanket keyword bans.
4. **Disinformation research**: Academic and governmental researchers tracking conspiratorial narratives in real time across languages and contexts.
5. **Crisis communication**: NGOs and government agencies responding to false narratives by understanding how communities are interpreting ambiguous claims.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Context-Aware Content Moderation API**: A SaaS platform for social networks that provides conspiracy/misinformation detection powered by agentic querying of user history, network, and temporal patterns—sold to platforms or third-party safety teams.
2. **Social Listening for Political Campaigns**: Real-time monitoring tool that flags emerging conspiracy narratives with nuance (distinguishing attack criticism from conspiracy endorsement) to help campaigns respond strategically.
3. **Narrative Intelligence for Public Health**: Platform for health ministries and NGOs to track vaccine hesitancy, conspiracy adoption, and satire in real time across regional social media, alerting teams to surges in specific types of misinformation.
4. **Linguistic Forensics for Dispute Resolution**: SaaS for legal/HR departments to analyze ambiguous statements in online disputes—determining speaker intent (sarcasm, conspiracy, concern) from social context for employment or defamation cases.
5. **Multi-Language Conspiracy Detector for News Organizations**: Newsroom tool that scans social media in multiple languages, surface conspiracy narratives with intent classification, enabling journalists to fact-check and contextualize emerging false claims.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Language-specific evaluation**: Currently tested only on Hebrew; generalization to other languages, especially those with different social media platforms, morphology, or conspiracy discourse styles is untested.
2. **Annotation subjectivity**: Labeling "intent" or "illocutionary force" is inherently subjective; the paper does not report inter-annotator agreement or discuss annotation guidelines in depth.
3. **Context availability assumption**: The framework assumes rich, queryable historical data (user posts, network graphs, timestamps) is available; many platforms or low-resource contexts lack this.
4. **Agent reasoning opacity**: While the paper measures token economy, it doesn't deeply analyze *which contexts* the agent relies on or whether it learns spurious patterns (e.g., using user follower count as a proxy for intent rather than linguistic evidence).
5. **Adversarial robustness untested**: No evaluation against actors deliberately gaming social context (fake followers, coordinated posting patterns, or crafted user histories).
6. **Scalability unclear**: The paper doesn't discuss latency or cost of running agentic queries across millions of tweets in real time.

## Open Questions
1. **How does performance degrade with incomplete or noisy context?** What if historical user data is sparse, deleted, or inaccurate?
2. **Can the framework transfer across languages and cultural conspiracy landscapes?** Does intent classification learned on Hebrew elections apply to, e.g., German or Indonesian contexts?
3. **What is the interpretability of agent decisions?** Can we extract decision rules or visual explanations of which social contexts swayed the agent's judgment?
4. **Does the agent learn to exploit spurious correlations** (e.g., "users with >10k followers are never conspiracy endorsers") rather than genuine linguistic or social signals?
5. **How do dynamically evolving conspiracy narratives affect performance?** Does the framework need retraining when novel conspiracy frames emerge post-2023?
6. **Can this extend to multimodal content** (videos, images with captions), or is it locked to text?

## Key Takeaway
AI agents querying social context outperform text-only models at detecting whether online speech endorses or mocks conspiracy theories.
