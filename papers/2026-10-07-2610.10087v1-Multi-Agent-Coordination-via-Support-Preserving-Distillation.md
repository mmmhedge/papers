# Multi-Agent Coordination via Support-Preserving Distillation

**Authors:** Sangmin Lee, Youngju Na, Chanmi Lee, Sung-eui Yoon
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10087v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10087v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies a failure mode in offline multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) where standard flow-based teacher policies produce invalid intermediate samples when learning multimodal behavior. The authors propose Mode-Support Semi-Discrete Optimal Transport (MoSDOT), a method that clusters replay data into discrete modes and uses optimal transport to route noise samples consistently, preventing the teacher from generating samples between conflicting coordination strategies that get propagated to student policies.

## Theoretical Background
Offline MARL uses centralized training with decentralized execution (CTDE) — a framework where a central teacher policy is trained on batch data, then distilled into independent one-step actors for deployment. Generative policies (often flow-based models) learn to model multimodal distributions of joint actions to handle the fact that multiple valid coordination strategies may exist. The distillation process typically minimizes regression loss between student outputs and the conditional mean of the teacher's output. Optimal transport is a classical tool for measuring and transforming probability distributions by finding the minimum-cost coupling between them. Semi-discrete optimal transport extends this to coupling a continuous distribution with a discrete set of modes.

## Core Contributions
1. **Problem identification**: Demonstrates that standard flow-based teachers fail when training data exhibits multimodal joint behavior — independent noise-to-target pairing causes the teacher to generate invalid interpolations between coordination modes.
2. **MoSDOT algorithm**: Proposes a two-stage approach that (a) clusters replay data into finite modes with capacity constraints, and (b) uses conditional semi-discrete optimal transport to deterministically assign noise samples to modes before teacher training, ensuring each noise input maps to a single valid mode.
3. **Shared-randomness analysis**: Introduces a variant that uses shared noise at execution time to measure the irreducible gap between strict product execution and centralized optimal behavior.
4. **Empirical validation**: Shows MoSDOT improves endpoint quality and routing consistency on controlled diagnostics and offline MARL benchmarks, especially on multimodal datasets.

## Connections to Other Work
This work builds on [[CTDE|Centralized Training Decentralized Execution]] paradigms in multi-agent RL. It extends prior work on generative policy distillation (e.g., diffusion-based and flow-based approaches) by addressing a fundamental mode-collapse problem. The use of optimal transport connects to recent applications in RL for reward optimization and imitation learning. It also relates to work on handling multimodality in behavioral cloning and offline RL more broadly, and to mode-seeking vs. mode-covering trade-offs in likelihood-free inference.

## Practical Applications
1. **Robotic manipulation**: Multi-robot systems performing collaborative assembly tasks where multiple valid strategies exist (e.g., different grasping approaches or handoff sequences).
2. **Autonomous vehicle coordination**: Fleets learning collision-free navigation policies in crowded environments where multiple valid passing strategies apply.
3. **Game AI**: Multi-agent game engines (e.g., real-time strategy games) where squads must coordinate without central command at inference time.
4. **Warehouse automation**: Coordinating multiple picking and sorting robots with multimodal task orderings learned from historical operation logs.
5. **Traffic control**: Decentralized intersection management where agents learn from aggregate traffic patterns with multiple valid flow patterns.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Mode discovery dependency**: Requires a principled way to choose the number of modes and per-mode capacities; misspecification can still collapse modes or leave valid strategies unlearned.
2. **Scalability to high-dimensional action spaces**: Semi-discrete optimal transport may become computationally expensive as the number of modes or agent count increases.
3. **Limited analysis of mode diversity**: No theoretical guarantee that discovered modes cover the full support of valid coordination strategies, especially on complex datasets.
4. **Execution-time assumptions**: Shared-randomness variant assumes synchronized random seeds across agents, which may be infeasible in real distributed systems with communication latency.
5. **Offline data requirements**: Assumes replay data is sufficiently rich to capture all coordination modes; sparse or biased historical data may lead to incomplete mode coverage.
6. **No convergence guarantees**: Lacks formal convergence analysis or sample complexity bounds for the MoSDOT procedure.

## Open Questions
1. How does MoSDOT scale to many agents (10+) or high-dimensional action spaces? What are the computational bottlenecks?
2. Can we automatically infer the number of coordination modes and their capacities from data, rather than treating them as hyperparameters?
3. Does MoSDOT preserve optimality of modes in terms of return, or does mode discretization incur a fundamental loss?
4. How sensitive is the method to the choice of conditional transport cost? Does the paper explore alternatives to standard optimal transport costs?
5. Can the framework be extended to online MARL or mixed online-offline settings?
6. How does MoSDOT compare to other multimodal policy distillation methods (e.g., mixture-of-experts, attention-based routing)?

## Key Takeaway
Mode-Support Optimal Transport prevents teachers from blending conflicting coordination strategies during offline MARL distillation, improving decentralized agent consistency.
