# Cross-Fitting Under Nonregularity: Normality and Inference via Locality

**Authors:** Bruno Fava
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.02944v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02944v1)

## Summary

This paper solves a critical problem in machine learning: standard confidence intervals fail when using cross-fitting (a technique that splits data into folds to avoid overfitting) in many real applications, leading to undercoverage—your 95% confidence interval might actually cover the truth only 80% of the time. The author shows that despite this "nonregularity" (statistical irregularity), a central limit theorem still holds if you adjust for correlations between folds, and provides a practical method to construct valid confidence intervals that actually work with modern methods like random forests and neural networks.

## Theoretical Background

Cross-fitting is a resampling technique widely used in causal inference and machine learning to reduce bias when estimating treatment effects or model performance. The classic challenge is that splitting data into folds creates dependence between estimates from different folds, violating standard independence assumptions needed for confidence intervals. This paper builds on long-standing work in empirical process theory and nonparametric statistics (e.g., Newey & McFadden's framework for M-estimation), which typically assumes "regularity conditions" — smoothness, uniqueness of solutions, and asymptotic normality. When these conditions fail (nonregular cases), conventional asymptotic theory breaks down. The paper's key innovation is a "locality condition" that preserves asymptotic normality even under nonregularity, while accounting for cross-fold correlation through an adjusted asymptotic variance.

## Core Contributions

1. **New locality condition**: Establishes sufficient conditions under which a broad class of cross-fitting estimators satisfy a central limit theorem despite nonregularity — a surprising theoretical result.

2. **Asymptotic variance adjustment**: Derives the correct form of the asymptotic variance accounting for cross-fold correlation, not just variation within folds.

3. **Practical estimation method**: Proposes an estimator for the cross-fold correlation parameter, enabling practitioners to compute adjusted confidence intervals without knowing the true correlation structure.

4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates that the method attains approximately nominal coverage in simulations with random forests and neural networks — showing this isn't just theory.

## Connections to Other Work

This work directly addresses failures of naive confidence intervals in [[Double Machine Learning]] and [[Neyman Orthogonality]]-based estimators, which rely on cross-fitting for bias reduction. It extends classical work on [[M-estimation]] and empirical processes to the nonregular case. The paper complements recent literature on valid inference post-selection (e.g., [[knockoff filters]] and debiased machine learning) but focuses specifically on the cross-fold dependence problem rather than model selection bias. Related to debates about uncertainty quantification in machine learning when [[conformal prediction]] and bootstrap methods give conflicting answers.

## Practical Applications

- **Causal inference in trials**: Estimating heterogeneous treatment effects (which model works best for which patients?) with machine learning, then constructing valid confidence intervals for effect sizes.
- **Model comparison testing**: Formally testing whether a new ML model beats a baseline (e.g., neural net vs. random forest) with valid p-values, not just point estimates.
- **Personalized medicine**: Determining optimal treatment regimes (drug A vs. drug B for individual patients) and quantifying uncertainty in recommendations.
- **Policy evaluation**: Estimating the value of optimal policy rules in observational data (e.g., optimal loan approval threshold) with valid confidence bands.

## Technical Limitations

- **Locality condition opacity**: While proven to hold in several applications (model testing, HTE, optimal regimes), the condition is abstract and may be hard to verify for novel problems; practitioners may not know if it applies to their setting.
- **Fold structure assumptions**: Method assumes a fixed fold structure; doesn't address optimal fold design or robustness when folds are data-dependent.
- **Correlation estimation**: The proposed correlation estimator itself may have high variance in small samples; simulations use relatively clean data (random forests, NNs) and may not capture messy real applications.
- **Computational cost**: Cross-fitting requires retraining models k times; no guidance on computational overhead vs. improved inference precision.
- **Sample size requirements**: Asymptotic normality may require moderate-to-large n; behavior in small/imbalanced settings unclear.

## Open Questions

- **Optimal fold number**: Does k (number of folds) affect the tightness of confidence intervals? How to choose k in practice?
- **Extension to sequential/online settings**: What happens when data arrives sequentially and folds must be defined adaptively?
- **Finite-sample coverage**: Can you derive finite-sample bounds on coverage (not just asymptotic), perhaps using empirical process tools?
- **Combination with other bias-reduction methods**: How does the locality condition interact with other debiasing techniques (e.g., Neyman orthogonality + regularization)?
- **High-dimensional regime**: Does the method scale when p (features) grows with n? Current work seems low-dimensional.

## Key Takeaway

Cross-fitting confidence intervals now work correctly under nonregularity by adjusting for fold correlations, enabling valid uncertainty quantification in causal ML.
