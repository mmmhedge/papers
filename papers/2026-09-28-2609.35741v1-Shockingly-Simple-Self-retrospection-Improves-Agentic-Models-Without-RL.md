# Shockingly Simple Self-retrospection Improves Agentic Models Without RL

**Authors:** Jonathan Light, Christopher Zhang Cui, Jeonghye Kim, Roger Creus Castanyer, Emiliano Penaloza et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35741v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35741v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that language model agents can improve their problem-solving performance by simply training on explanations of their own past attempts — without reinforcement learning or external rewards. The method, called Retrospection-Only Fine-Tuning (ROFT), has a language model agent attempt a task, generate a written explanation of what happened, and then fine-tune itself on predicting those explanation tokens. On software engineering benchmarks, ROFT matches or exceeds reinforcement learning baselines while being simpler and faster to train.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two key ideas. First, the observation that [[Large Language Models]] can improve through fine-tuning on token-level predictions (next-token prediction loss) — a standard supervised learning approach. Second, the psychological principle that humans learn not just by doing, but by reflecting on and articulating their experiences. The paper's novelty is combining these insights: instead of learning from trajectories of actions (the typical RL approach), the agent learns from *written reflections* on those trajectories. This sidesteps the need for reward signals or policy gradients, which are central to [[Reinforcement Learning]] methods like GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization).

## Core Contributions
1. **ROFT as a minimal procedure**: A simple online training loop (attempt → observe → retrospect → fine-tune) that isolates the effect of explanation-only training without RL machinery.
2. **Competitive performance on SWE-bench**: Achieves 49.2% solve rate on SWE-bench Verified (compared to GRPO's 48.0% in their runs) and 26.8% on Pro (vs. 25.3%), while training faster and requiring fewer sampled attempts.
3. **Learning from pure failures**: The model learns to solve individual tasks where *all 64 base-model attempts failed*, proving that successful trajectories are not strictly necessary as seeds.
4. **Indirect credit assignment**: Behavioral analysis shows ROFT implicitly learns to encourage good actions and discourage bad ones through the retrospection signal alone — without explicit action-level rewards.
5. **Explanation-to-action transfer**: Stylistic prompting (e.g., asking for "more direct solutions") influences subsequent attempt structure without explicit length penalties, suggesting explanations shape future behavior planning.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to several research threads:
- **[[RLHF]] and policy learning**: Unlike methods that directly optimize a reward function, ROFT learns indirectly through language. This echoes work on learning from natural language feedback, but here the feedback is self-generated.
- **Self-improvement in agents**: Connects to research on [[agents]] that learn from their own rollouts, but avoids the need for explicit value functions or advantage estimates.
- **Interpretability via explanations**: Aligns with work suggesting that model interpretability and performance improvement can co-occur (see [[mechanistic-interpretability]]); explaining behavior may force the model to learn more transparent decision patterns.
- **In-context learning vs. fine-tuning**: ROFT sits between purely in-context prompting and full RL — it uses online fine-tuning on self-generated language rather than frozen weights and demonstrations.

## Practical Applications
1. **Software engineering and code generation**: Autonomous agents that fix bugs or write code can reflect on failed attempts and improve without expensive verifier feedback, reducing computational overhead in systems like GitHub Copilot or enterprise code-assist tools.
2. **Autonomous research and literature review**: Research assistants that generate hypotheses, fail, and then retrospect on why, improving subsequent query generation and synthesis without manual labeling.
3. **Educational tutoring systems**: Virtual tutors that learn from their own explanations of why students misunderstood a concept, refining future teaching strategies.
4. **Multi-turn dialogue and task automation**: Conversational agents in customer service or technical support that learn to resolve recurring issues by reflecting on past failures.
5. **Scientific discovery and experiment design**: Agents that propose and run experiments, then generate written analyses of results to improve future experimental hypotheses without reinforcement signals from humans.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Self-Improving Code Agent Platform**: A managed service where companies deploy ROFT-based agents to their internal codebases; the agents continuously solve engineering tickets, generate retrospections, and improve without external oversight — competing with manual code review workflows.
2. **Reflection-Driven Learning Infrastructure**: A framework for any SaaS application to attach lightweight retrospection modules to AI agents, enabling self-improvement loops without RL pipelines — sold as an API to enterprises.
3. **Autonomous Research Assistant (ArXiv/Literature Mining)**: An agent that reads papers, attempts to extract insights, writes summaries, reviews its own work, and improves — helping researchers rapidly synthesize literature without manual annotation.
4. **Adaptive Tutoring without Human Labeling**: An edtech platform where AI tutors learn from their own explanations of student misconceptions, scaling personalized teaching without requiring human-labeled examples of good pedagogy.
5. **Business Intelligence & Data Analysis Agent**: A tool that queries databases, generates analyses, reviews its own outputs via written critique, and refines queries — enabling self-service analytics without explicit reward modeling.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Explanation quality assumption**: The method assumes the model can generate meaningful retrospections; if explanations are inaccurate or superficial, the fine-tuning signal may be harmful. No analysis of how explanation quality degrades over training.
2. **Task domain specificity**: Evaluated only on software engineering (SWE-bench). Unclear if ROFT generalizes to other domains (e.g., reasoning, math, creative tasks) where self-critique may be harder.
3. **Comparison fairness**: GRPO results are from "evaluated runs" and may not be fully comparable — different hyperparameters, sampling budgets, or hardware could explain performance differences.
4. **Scalability unknown**: Experiments use Qwen3.5-4B (a small model). Unclear how ROFT scales to larger models where explanations might become verbose or where fine-tuning dynamics change.
5. **No verifier dependency hidden**: While ROFT doesn't use a verifier at train time, SWE-bench evaluation itself requires running code and checking solutions — so the practical system still needs verification infrastructure.
6. **Failure modes not explored**: No discussion of how ROFT handles tasks where the model is confidently wrong or where retrospections rationalize poor decisions without actually improving behavior.

## Open Questions
1. **Explanation quality over time**: Does ROFT degrade when the model's explanations become self-deceiving or circular? How do we detect and prevent this?
2. **Cross-task transfer**: Can retrospections from one task improve performance on semantically different tasks, or is learning purely task-specific?
3. **Scaling to frontier models**: How does ROFT perform on models like GPT-4, Gemini, or Claude? Do larger models' retrospections transfer differently?
4. **Hybrid methods**: Can ROFT be combined with lightweight reward signals (e.g., binary success/failure labels without a full RL pipeline) to further accelerate learning?
5. **Theoretical analysis**: Why does training only on explanations indirectly improve actions? Can we formalize the credit-assignment mechanism and predict when it will or won't work?
6. **Human-in-the-loop variants**: What happens if humans sporadically critique retrospections? Does even sparse feedback amplify the effect?
7. **Curriculum and hard negatives**: Does the order or selection of attempts matter? Should the agent preferentially reflect on near-misses vs. complete failures?

## Key Takeaway
Language models can improve at tasks by training on their own written reflections, matching RL performance without reward signals or policy gradients.
