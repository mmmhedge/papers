# Partner Capability Estimation for Task-Agnostic Adaptation in Ad-Hoc Teamwork

**Authors:** Peter Tisnikar, Maja Swieczkowska, Benteng Ma, Gerard Canal, Matteo Leonetti
**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **arXiv:** [2607.27177v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27177v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles ad-hoc teamwork—where an agent must quickly adapt to work effectively with new, unknown partners—by inferring what each partner can actually do (their "hidden capabilities") without knowing the task in advance. The key insight is that instead of assuming partners always act optimally, the method learns task-invariant capability vectors and accounts for human behavioral diversity, allowing agents to plan better collaborative strategies with minimal prior information.

## Theoretical Background
Ad-hoc teamwork (AHT) extends multi-agent reinforcement learning to settings where an agent must cooperate with previously unseen teammates without communication. Prior work typically assumes: (1) a single fixed task, (2) known partner policies or capabilities, and (3) rational/optimal partner behavior. This paper builds on Bayesian theory for inference under uncertainty and contextual Markov Decision Processes (MDPs)—a framework that conditions agent decisions on contextual information like inferred partner capabilities. The work also draws on simulation-based inference methods (approximate Bayesian computation) to estimate latent partner properties from observed behavior.

## Core Contributions
1. **Multi-task AHT framing**: Reframes ad-hoc teamwork as joint planning with decentralized execution where capabilities are hidden and tasks vary—moving beyond single-task assumptions.
2. **CE-CM method**: An approximate Bayesian approach that infers task-invariant capability vectors via simulation-based sampling, enabling online belief refinement with minimal data and no population pre-training.
3. **CE-CM-Div extension**: Accounts for human behavioral diversity by evaluating capability hypotheses against multiple plausible rollouts rather than a single optimal trajectory, improving robustness to sub-optimal human strategies.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates rapid capability recovery in simulation and validates on 225 real human trajectories, showing capability-based modeling is interpretable and task-agnostic.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent reinforcement learning]], human-AI teaming, and Bayesian inference. It extends classical AHT (which assumes known or learnable partner models on fixed tasks) toward more realistic settings inspired by [[theory of mind]] problems in cognitive science. The contextual MDP formulation relates to conditional planning and [[meta-learning]] approaches that condition on context. Unlike [[RLHF]]-style methods that assume human preferences are fixed and known, this method explicitly models uncertainty about partner capabilities and behavior.

## Practical Applications
1. **Autonomous vehicle platoons**: Estimating driving capabilities of human or autonomous partners to coordinate safely on shared roads.
2. **Robotic teamwork in warehouses**: A robot determining what box-stacking or navigation capabilities its human coworker has to assign tasks safely.
3. **Crisis response teams**: Drones or rescue robots working with firefighters or rescue workers whose capabilities may be unknown or variable.
4. **Manufacturing assembly lines**: Collaborative robots learning the skill level and speed of human assembly line workers to pace and sequence joint tasks.
5. **Game AI**: Non-player characters adapting to diverse human player strategies in cooperative multiplayer games without pre-training on human behavior.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"TeamMate"**: An AI coordination layer for manufacturing that continuously profiles human and robotic worker capabilities across tasks, auto-scheduling jobs to maximize safety and efficiency without explicit human input.
2. **Adaptive rescue robotics platform**: Software that allows emergency response robots to infer rescue worker capabilities in real-time, recommending which tasks to delegate and which to undertake autonomously.
3. **Dynamic skill marketplace for contingent work**: Platform matching freelancers to gig tasks by having AI systems quickly estimate true capabilities from a few trial tasks, reducing hiring friction.
4. **Autonomous driving co-pilot**: A vehicle system that estimates driver capability (attention, reaction time, decision quality) on-the-fly and smoothly transitions control based on inferred partner state.
5. **Adaptive gaming engine**: Multiplayer game backend that profiles each player's playstyle and skill within minutes, optimizing teammate pairing and difficulty in real-time without static skill labels.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability to complex tasks**: Simulation-based inference (SMCABC) becomes expensive in high-dimensional action/state spaces; unclear how the method scales to realistic, long-horizon domains.
2. **Assumption of stationarity**: Learns task-invariant capabilities, but human performance may drift with fatigue, learning, or context—not addressed.
3. **Limited partner diversity in human study**: 15 participants is small; unknown if patterns generalize to more diverse human populations or capability distributions.
4. **No communication**: Method assumes zero communication between agents; gains may be trivial in settings where even minimal information exchange is allowed.
5. **Offline evaluation bottleneck**: Human study is offline (post-hoc analysis of trajectories), not real-time closed-loop; online deployment results unknown.
6. **Contextual MDP simplification**: Assumes capabilities can be represented in a fixed, task-invariant vector; may fail when capability relevance is highly task-dependent.

## Open Questions
1. How does the method perform when partner capabilities change over time (e.g., due to learning, fatigue, or damage)? Can online updates keep pace?
2. Can the approach scale to continuous or very large action spaces without prohibitive simulation costs?
3. How does performance degrade when the true capability distribution falls outside the implicit assumptions (e.g., partners with rare or adversarial strategies)?
4. In what ways could minimal communication improve capability estimation, and what is the efficiency frontier between communication overhead and estimation accuracy?
5. How robust is capability representation when tasks are fundamentally different (e.g., inference trained on tabletop manipulation—does it transfer to mobile manipulation)?
6. What cognitive experiments would validate that inferred capabilities are psychologically meaningful and match human self-reports of ability?

## Key Takeaway
Agents can quickly learn hidden partner capabilities and adapt teamwork by inferring task-invariant skill vectors, without knowing the task in advance or assuming partners act optimally.
