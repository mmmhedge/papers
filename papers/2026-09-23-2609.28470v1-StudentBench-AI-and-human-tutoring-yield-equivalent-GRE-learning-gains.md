# StudentBench: AI and human tutoring yield equivalent GRE learning gains

**Authors:** Curtis Northcutt, Inaara Hasmani, Kevin Feng, Trevor Khangi, Andreas Plesner et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28470v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28470v1)

## Summary
This paper demonstrates that AI tutors powered by large language models (LLMs) can produce learning gains equivalent to expert human tutors when teaching GRE test preparation, at a fraction of the cost. The researchers conducted a large-scale study with 2,383 students and analyzed over 175,000 student-AI interactions to show that AI tutoring matches human performance, and in several domains actually exceeds it—while costing 918 times less per unit of learning gain.

## Theoretical Background
This work sits at the intersection of educational psychology and LLM capabilities. It builds on prior research in computer-assisted learning, which has long explored how technology can support student instruction, and extends to recent work using transformers (neural networks that process sequences using attention mechanisms) for dialogue and instruction. The study employs rigorous experimental design with randomized control groups—comparing AI tutoring, human tutoring, and no tutoring—to establish causal learning gains. It uses standardized GRE questions as a quantifiable measure of knowledge acquisition, grounding claims in established educational assessment frameworks.

## Core Contributions
- **Empirical equivalence result**: First large-scale demonstration that AI tutoring achieves statistically equivalent learning gains to expert human tutoring (p = .015), with AI exceeding human performance in 5 of 7 GRE domains.
- **Cost-effectiveness analysis**: Quantifies the economic advantage: AI tutoring costs USD 0.0052 per percentage point of learning gain versus USD 4.81 for human tutoring.
- **StudentBench platform and dataset**: Introduces a reusable public platform and dataset of 175,000+ authentic student-AI tutoring conversations, enabling reproducible research in educational AI.
- **Mechanistic decomposition**: Identifies specific AI tutor capabilities (lesson planning, practice-problem creation, conversational pedagogy, engagement) and correlates pedagogical behaviors (e.g., response speed) with learning outcomes.

## Connections to Other Work
This research extends the practical evaluation philosophy of [[RLHF]] and human-in-the-loop alignment studies, but inverts the question: instead of measuring whether AI feedback aligns with human judgment, it asks whether AI instruction produces equivalent real-world outcomes to human expertise. It complements emerging work on LLM-based tutoring systems by providing rigorous causal evidence rather than anecdotal adoption metrics. The correlation analysis (faster replies → more engagement → better learning) echoes findings in the dialogue systems and conversational AI literature, connecting [[Transformers]]-based interactions to downstream task performance.

## Practical Applications
- **Scaled remedial education**: Affordable tutoring for test preparation (GRE, GMAT, SAT) in underserved regions or for low-income students.
- **Corporate training**: Cost-effective personalized onboarding and skill-building for employees, with AI generating custom lesson plans and practice materials.
- **Language learning**: AI tutors that adapt lesson pacing and generate contextual practice problems for ESL learners.
- **K-12 homework support**: After-school tutoring platforms where AI provides instant help, reducing wait times and scaling expert instruction.
- **Medical board exam prep**: AI tutors for high-stakes clinical knowledge assessments (USMLE, board certifications).

## Technical Limitations
- **Domain specificity**: Results are limited to standardized test domains (GRE Quantitative and Verbal). Generalization to open-ended learning, creative problem-solving, or skill domains (coding, writing) remains unproven.
- **Short-term measurement**: The study measures immediate learning gains on test items; retention, transfer to real-world contexts, and long-term outcomes are not assessed.
- **LLM variation**: Results depend on which LLM backs the tutor; the paper doesn't comprehensively compare across model architectures or sizes, so it's unclear which LLM properties drive equivalence.
- **Human tutor heterogeneity**: The human tutors studied represent a specific pool (likely high-quality, motivated by research participation). Findings may not generalize to average tutors.
- **Causal ambiguity on engagement**: While faster AI responses correlate with student engagement and learning, the causal direction and confounds (e.g., student motivation) are not fully disentangled.
- **Scalability unknowns**: No analysis of how AI tutor performance degrades under concurrent load, latency increases, or deployment to billions of users.

## Open Questions
- **Longitudinal retention**: Do learning gains from AI tutoring persist 6 months later, and how do retention curves compare to human tutoring?
- **Transfer learning**: Can knowledge gained through AI tutoring on GRE math transfer to college coursework or professional problem-solving?
- **Mechanistic attribution**: Which aspects of LLM design (scale, instruction-tuning, RLHF) most contribute to pedagogical effectiveness? Can smaller, cheaper models achieve equivalence?
- **Personalization limits**: Does AI tutoring plateau when students require highly idiosyncratic pedagogical approaches? How often do human tutors outperform AI on atypical learners?
- **Adversarial robustness**: How do AI tutors handle students who deliberately mislead or test boundaries? How do error rates and misconception propagation compare to human tutors?
- **Emotion and motivation**: Does AI tutoring generate equivalent motivation, confidence, or emotional support as human tutoring, especially for struggling students?

## Key Takeaway
AI tutors match expert human tutors on standardized test learning at 1/900th the cost, opening pathways to democratized test prep at scale.
