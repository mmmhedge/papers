# Self-Adapting Group of Experts for Multi-Agent Reasoning

**Authors:** Mohammad Atif Quamar, Nurbek Tastan, Karthik Nandakumar, Junpei Komiyama
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35412v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35412v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces SAGE, a framework that enables multiple language model agents to automatically adapt their reasoning strategies based on the problem at hand. Instead of keeping each agent's instructions fixed, SAGE identifies which agent is solving the problem best, extracts its reasoning approach, and teaches it to the other agents—all without needing to see the actual problem or solutions. The result is better performance across reasoning benchmarks.

## Theoretical Background
Multi-agent reasoning systems build on two key ideas: (1) **ensemble methods** — combining multiple models or approaches to improve overall performance — and (2) **collaborative filtering and peer review** — using agreement or consensus signals to identify quality. Prior work in multi-agent systems (like chain-of-thought prompting and agent frameworks) has shown that different agents with different roles can tackle problems by proposing, reviewing, and refining solutions. The paper builds on the insight that agents' **system prompts** (the instructions that define an agent's role and reasoning style) fundamentally shape behavior, and that adapting these prompts dynamically could improve performance. The framework uses metrics like **prefix consistency** (whether an agent's early reasoning steps align with others) and **answer agreement** to identify useful strategies.

## Core Contributions
1. **SAGE framework**: A training-free method for dynamically selecting and transferring reasoning strategies between agents based on their initial responses to a problem.
2. **Strategy transfer mechanism**: Uses only the original system prompts to extract and communicate a reasoning strategy from a high-performing agent to others, without exposing the problem or solutions.
3. **Dynamic routing**: After strategy adaptation, agents communicate through a sparse directed acyclic graph that preferentially routes information from higher-scoring agents to lower-scoring ones.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates consistent accuracy improvements across multiple language model backbones and reasoning benchmarks using only answer agreement, prefix consistency, and reciprocal peer review signals.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent collaboration]], [[prompting strategies]], and [[mixture-of-experts]] approaches. It relates to:
- **Chain-of-thought prompting and variants** — techniques for steering LLM reasoning through instructions.
- **Mixture of Experts (MoE)** — routing different inputs to different specialized models, here adapted for agents sharing a problem.
- **Self-consistency and ensemble decoding** — using agreement signals to improve LLM outputs.
- **Graph-based communication protocols** — inspired by directed acyclic graphs for information flow in multi-agent systems.
- **Training-free adaptation** — similar in spirit to in-context learning and few-shot prompting approaches that avoid fine-tuning.

## Practical Applications
1. **Customer support systems**: Multiple specialized agent roles (technical expert, empathy specialist, solution finder) dynamically adopt the best reasoning approach for each customer query.
2. **Scientific research acceleration**: Teams of agents (literature reviewer, hypothesis generator, experimental designer) adapt their strategies to the type of research problem.
3. **Legal document analysis**: Agents with different legal expertise (contract law, IP, regulatory) transfer successful analytical strategies to improve consistency and accuracy.
4. **Code review and debugging**: Multiple agent personas (security reviewer, performance analyst, maintainability expert) adapt their review strategies based on what's working best for the codebase.
5. **Financial analysis and reporting**: Agents with different analytical perspectives automatically adopt the most effective reasoning approach for the type of financial query.

2. **Multi-Agent Reasoning-as-a-Service (MaaS)**: A cloud API where companies submit complex reasoning tasks and get back answers from dynamically self-optimizing teams of agents, with transparent strategy selection and auditability.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Blind strategy transfer**: The framework transfers reasoning strategies without access to the problem or solutions, which may lead to transferring strategies poorly suited to the actual problem domain (false positive identification).
2. **Prompt-level abstraction**: The method assumes that reasoning strategies can be meaningfully captured and transferred via system prompts alone; deeper architectural or model-level adaptations are out of scope.
3. **Consensus dependency**: Relies on answer agreement and prefix consistency as signals of good strategy, which may fail when all agents are systematically wrong or when diversity is actually preferable to consensus.
4. **Scalability to many agents**: As the number of agents grows, identifying the best strategy donor and routing information through the DAG becomes computationally more complex; scaling properties are unclear.
5. **Role preservation vs. strategy alignment trade-off**: Preserving original roles while transferring strategies could create tension — agents designed for different tasks may not benefit equally from the same reasoning approach.
6. **Generalization across domains**: Trained on specific benchmarks; unclear how well strategy selection generalizes to out-of-distribution problems or novel reasoning types.

## Open Questions
1. **Can strategy transfer work across heterogeneous model families?** Does SAGE work when agents are powered by fundamentally different base models (e.g., GPT-4 and Llama)?
2. **What is the minimum problem context needed for effective strategy identification?** Could simpler or faster signals replace prefix consistency and answer agreement?
3. **How does performance degrade with noisy or adversarial agent responses?** What happens when some agents deliberately or accidentally provide misleading strategy cues?
4. **Can we interpret what strategies are being transferred?** What do the actual prompt modifications look like, and do they align with human intuitions about good reasoning?
5. **Does SAGE enable emergent specialization?** Over multiple problems, do agents converge to different roles, or does strategy homogenization occur?
6. **How sensitive is the approach to the DAG routing design?** Would other communication topologies (fully connected, hierarchical, peer-to-peer) change the results significantly?

## Key Takeaway
Language model agents automatically transfer reasoning strategies from the best-performing agent to others, improving ensemble accuracy without seeing problems or solutions.
