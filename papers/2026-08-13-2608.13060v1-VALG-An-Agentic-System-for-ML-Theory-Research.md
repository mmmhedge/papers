# VALG: An Agentic System for ML Theory Research

**Authors:** Dechen Zhang, Xuan Tang, Xinxiang Yin, Xingwu Chen, Jian Qian et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-13 | **arXiv:** [2608.13060v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13060v1)

## Summary
VALG is an autonomous AI system that conducts machine learning theory research by breaking down theorem-proving into manageable steps: formulating problems, constructing proofs, and learning from failures. When proofs fail, the system diagnoses whether the problem is a calculation error, proof structure, or flawed assumptions—then adjusts accordingly. Tested on open problems from a top theory conference, it produced two complete theorem candidates and several partial results, showing that agentic workflows can meaningfully contribute to mathematical discovery.

## Theoretical Background
Machine learning theory formalizes learning through mathematical models that specify data distributions, training protocols, loss functions, and success metrics. Proving theorems in this domain requires researchers to juggle three interdependent pieces: the problem formulation (what assumptions do we make?), the theorem statement (what do we claim to prove?), and the proof mechanism (how do we show it's true?). Traditional research is iterative: researchers form hypotheses, test them via preliminary analysis, and refine assumptions and proofs in cycles. VALG automates this feedback loop by organizing it as an agentic workflow — a sequence of AI-driven reasoning steps with explicit verification checkpoints, similar to how [[Reinforcement Learning]] agents learn through trial-and-error correction.

## Core Contributions
1. **Multi-level verification framework**: VALG maintains typed proof-dependency graphs (directed acyclic graphs that encode which lemmas depend on which earlier results) and verifies theorem-level composition—ensuring that proof pieces fit together mathematically.

2. **Intelligent failure routing**: When a proof attempt fails, VALG explicitly diagnoses whether the obstruction is in a derivation (calculational error), proof structure (wrong approach), or theorem formulation (incorrect assumptions), and routes the next attempt accordingly.

3. **Adaptive formulation mechanism**: When a proof fails due to flawed assumptions, VALG generates explicitly related variants or relaxations of the original problem while preserving mathematical traceability—the resulting theorem stays connected to the source problem.

4. **Empirical validation on real open problems**: Evaluation on nine subproblems from five COLT 2026 (a premier ML theory venue) open problems, yielding two internally finalized theorem candidates matching the scope of the original problem briefs.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[Large Language Models|LLMs]] (which generate proof steps and problem formulations) and automated theorem proving, extending recent work on AI-assisted mathematical discovery (e.g., [[AlphaProof]], [[Lean]] proof assistants). It relates to [[Reinforcement Learning]] agent design by treating theorem discovery as a sequential decision process with reward signals (proof verified or failed). The dependency-graph structure echoes formal verification tools in software engineering. Unlike pure symbolic approaches or pure neural approaches, VALG combines both: LLMs provide creative problem formulation and proof sketches, while symbolic verification ensures mathematical correctness.

## Practical Applications
1. **Accelerating mathematical research in ML theory**: Researchers can use VALG to explore the landscape of related theorems and relaxations around an open problem, generating candidate results faster than manual iteration.

2. **Automated conjecture refinement**: When a conjecture fails, VALG automatically generates weaker but provable variants, useful in settings where exact theorems are hard to prove (e.g., bounds that only hold under certain distributions).

3. **Educational tool for graduate students**: Provides structured feedback on failed proof attempts, teaching where the reasoning broke down and suggesting reformulations.

4. **Foundational research into AI-assisted discovery**: Benchmarking and improving how AI systems reason about abstract mathematical concepts, which generalizes beyond ML theory.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ML Theory Research Assistant (B2B SaaS)**: A web platform where researchers upload open problem statements and VALG autonomously explores the theorem landscape, generating candidate proofs and weaker results with full dependency tracking. Subscription model for academic institutions.

2. **Automated research paper pipeline**: VALG generates theorem candidates with proofs; researchers review and polish them for publication. Accelerates paper output for theory-focused ML labs.

3. **Interactive theorem refinement interface**: Visualization of proof-dependency graphs with failure diagnostics. Researchers click on failed branches to see why a conjecture broke and what relaxations VALG recommends.

4. **Theorem verification and benchmarking service**: Researchers submit candidate theorems; VALG verifies proofs and generates a "proof report" showing which assumptions are tight, which steps are fragile. Useful for conference submissions.

5. **Educational simulation for mathematical reasoning**: A teaching tool that shows students how professional mathematicians diagnose proof failures and refine problem formulations, using VALG's diagnostic outputs as teachable moments.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited scope of evaluation**: Nine subproblems from five open problems is a small test set. No comparison to human researcher time or to naive baselines (e.g., LLM without failure routing). Unclear how well the approach scales to truly novel or highly non-standard problem formulations.

2. **Proof verification assumption**: The system assumes proofs can be verified automatically (likely via a formal system like Lean or Coq). Many mathematical proofs, especially in ML theory, are written informally; verification requires reformalization, a labor-intensive step not addressed here.

3. **Dependency graph brittleness**: If the proof-dependency graph is malformed or incomplete, the system's verification and routing may fail silently or mislead. No discussion of how robust this is to modeling errors.

4. **Formulation space is finite and hand-coded**: The "adaptive formulation" mechanism generates variants or relaxations, but these are likely limited to a predefined set of transformations (e.g., weaken assumptions, strengthen conclusions, restrict to special cases). Truly novel problem reformulations may be out of reach.

5. **No guarantees on solution quality**: The system produces candidates, some of which match the source problem scope, some which are restricted-method results or conditional theorems. No principled way to measure progress or predict whether a truly better result is findable.

## Open Questions
1. **Scaling beyond COLT open problems**: How does VALG perform on open problems from other domains (e.g., pure mathematics, statistics)? Is the approach general or tuned to ML theory's structure?

2. **Formulation creativity**: Can VALG generate problem formulations that are not strict relaxations or restrictions of the source problem? Can it discover orthogonal reformulations that human researchers might miss?

3. **Human-AI collaboration**: How should a human researcher interact with VALG's outputs? When VALG proposes a relaxed theorem, should the human try to prove the original, or accept the relaxation as a stepping stone?

4. **Proof verification**: How much manual effort is needed to formally verify VALG's proof candidates? Can symbolic verification be tighter than informal checking?

5. **Failure modes**: On what classes of problems does VALG fail? Are there problem characteristics (e.g., highly non-standard assumptions, adversarial problem design) that defeat the routing logic?

6. **Comparison to human baselines**: How much wall-clock time does VALG save compared to a human researcher tackling the same open problems? On what metrics (time, number of theorems, proof quality) does it win or lose?

## Key Takeaway
AI agents can autonomously explore theorem-proof spaces by diagnosing why proofs fail and iteratively refining both assumptions and proof strategies.
