# IMPACT: Modeling Socially Interdependent Movement in a Generative Multi-Agent Simulation of a Pompeian Household

**Authors:** Tianqi Liu, Nayoung Kim, Julia Sebastien, Kathryn Gleason, Caitlín Eilís Barrett et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38113v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38113v1)

## Summary
This paper presents IMPACT, a multi-agent simulation architecture that models how people in a Roman household coordinate their movements and activities based on social roles and relationships. Rather than simulating independent agents, IMPACT captures how individuals wait for, prompt, and constrain each other's behavior through socially defined dependencies—demonstrating this through a five-hour simulation of a Pompeian dinner party where social hierarchy and obligations shape who moves where and when.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two converging ideas: (1) multi-agent simulations—computational systems where multiple autonomous agents interact to produce emergent collective behavior—and (2) activity-based modeling in archaeology and urban simulation, which represents how people use spaces through their everyday tasks and movements. Prior work on agent-based modeling typically assumes agents plan and act independently, with coordination emerging only through environmental interaction. This paper draws on social science concepts like role theory (the idea that people's behavior is constrained by their social position) and obligation structures (culturally defined rules about who must do what, when, and in relation to whom). The innovation is connecting these social concepts explicitly to agent planning mechanics.

## Core Contributions
1. **IMPACT architecture**: Four novel coordinating mechanisms—socially gated milestone planning (activities can only start when social conditions are met), wait-or-prompt resolution (agents pause or signal others), structured directive issuance (higher-status agents give commands), and directive integration (agents incorporate others' instructions into their plans).

2. **Empirical validation in archaeology**: A detailed instantiation showing how role-based dependencies reproduce historically plausible patterns (asymmetric waiting, co-movement by status) in a Pompeian household, validated by both user studies (37 participants) and expert interviews (6 archaeology scholars).

3. **Behavioral evidence**: Analysis demonstrating that interdependent planning produces observable patterns (co-location, asymmetric waiting patterns, directional flows) that single-agent planning cannot explain.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[multi-agent-systems|multi-agent reinforcement learning]] where agents learn coordination policies, but IMPACT uses rule-based social constraints rather than learned policies. It parallels work on [[agents|agent communication protocols]] and team planning in AI, but grounds these in historical and cultural specifics rather than abstract coordination problems. The archaeology application connects to broader digital humanities work reconstructing past behavior, but is unusual in emphasizing agent interdependence rather than independent task execution. The social role framework echoes work on social norms in multi-agent systems.

## Practical Applications
1. **Museum interpretation**: Interactive exhibits where visitors observe simulated historical scenarios, seeing how social rank influenced daily movement and space use.
2. **Heritage site management**: Simulating capacity and flow in reconstructed ancient spaces to inform restoration and public access decisions.
3. **Historical education**: Teaching students how social structure constrained everyday life by observing simulated behavior.
4. **Architectural analysis**: Understanding how building layouts in Pompeii afforded or constrained different social interactions.
5. **Ethnographic documentation**: Recording intangible cultural practices in present-day households with similar hierarchical structures before they disappear.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **HistoricalVR**: A VR platform where users inhabit roles in reconstructed historical households and experience period-appropriate constraints on movement and agency—educational tourism.
2. **Heritage Simulation-as-a-Service**: White-label software for museums and archaeological sites to generate custom simulations of their sites' daily life, configurable with local expertise.
3. **Social Simulation Authoring Tool**: Desktop software for historians/anthropologists to define role hierarchies and obligation rules, then auto-generate believable multi-agent simulations without coding.
4. **Organizational Behavior Training**: B2B tool using similar constraint logic to simulate how hierarchy, directives, and role obligations shape workplace movement and collaboration patterns—for workplace design.
5. **Cultural Game Studio**: Story-driven games set in reconstructed historical spaces where player agency is constrained by period-appropriate social roles and obligations, emphasizing authenticity.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability**: The five-agent dinner party is modest; unclear how wait-or-prompt and directive mechanisms scale to hundreds of agents or multi-day timescales.
2. **Historical grounding**: The paper relies on expert judgment for historical plausibility, not ground-truth video or archaeological evidence of actual Pompeian household routines (which don't exist).
3. **Role definition brittleness**: Social roles and obligation rules are hand-crafted; no principled method for extracting these from historical texts or generalizing across cultures.
4. **Static constraints**: Obligations don't adapt or learn; agents cannot renegotiate roles or violate norms based on context (no rebellion, innovation, or social change).
5. **Validation gap**: Participant ratings confirm "believability" but don't validate that simulation matches actual historical behavior—a unfalsifiable claim for ancient Rome.
6. **Limited generalization**: Demonstrates one household type; unclear if IMPACT's mechanisms transfer to other historical contexts, social structures, or non-Western cultures.

## Open Questions
1. Can IMPACT's constraints be learned or inferred from fragmentary historical records (texts, images, archaeological layout) rather than hand-authored?
2. How do agents' interdependencies change when norms are violated or when social status is contested?
3. Does the architecture scale to neighborhoods or cities, and what new coordination challenges emerge?
4. Can directives be refused or negotiated, and how does that affect realism for different historical periods or social contexts?
5. How sensitive are emergent patterns (co-location, movement flow) to the specific weights and thresholds in the constraint model—is there a robust "social physics"?
6. Can the framework model economic or resource constraints alongside social ones (e.g., limited food, fatigue)?

## Key Takeaway
Social roles and obligations guide agent movement more realistically than independent planning in historical simulations.
