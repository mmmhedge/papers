# Participatory Moral AI Is Not Neutral: The Invisible Hand of Developers

**Authors:** Taenyun Kim, Edyta Bogucka, Daniele Quercia
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14522v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14522v1)

## Summary
This paper reveals that "participatory moral AI"—the practice of polling people on ethical dilemmas to train AI systems—is never neutral. Before any vote happens, developers make three hidden choices: which moral features to ask about, which people to poll, and how to phrase the question. Each choice shapes the outcome. The research tests this across three real scenarios (kidney allocation, worker absence, AI-generated deceased people) and shows that these developer decisions can shift moral preferences by as much as a full rating-scale point—often without transparency or accountability.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two streams of research. First, **moral preference elicitation** — a family of methods in AI alignment that attempt to ground AI behavior in human values by aggregating judgments from many people. Second, **decision-making ethics** in contexts where AI systems make high-stakes, value-laden choices (like organ allocation or hiring). The paper also draws on **social science methods** around question design and sampling bias, which have long shown that how you ask shapes what you hear. Prior work in [[RLHF]] (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) and value alignment assumes that human preferences can be extracted and aggregated; this paper challenges the assumption that the extraction process is transparent or value-neutral.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical identification of three opaque developer choices** in the moral AI pipeline — framing, feature selection, and voter composition — each treated as "technical" but each carrying normative weight.
2. **Quantification of their effects**: Shows feature relevance shifts across deployment contexts (kidney allocation vs. simulated workers vs. deceased AI), ideological preference splits on ~one-third of features, and framing effects up to 1 full Likert point.
3. **Demonstration that framing changes moral foundation associations** — the same question asked differently activates different underlying values (care/fairness/loyalty/etc.).
4. **Concrete audit recommendation**: Argues that voting-based alignment requires *disclosure and audit* of each pipeline stage, not just aggregation.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader critiques of [[RLHF]] and human-in-the-loop AI that observe how human feedback is never a neutral "ground truth." It echoes concerns in **fairness and bias** literature (e.g., whose preferences get represented, whose are filtered out). The work sits at the intersection of **AI governance** and **participatory design** — questioning whether "participatory" inherently means democratic or fair. The ideological sensitivity findings resonate with work on **value disagreement** in AI alignment, which asks whether consensus-seeking is even the right goal when populations have genuine value differences.

## Practical Applications
1. **AI triage and healthcare systems**: Before deploying AI for organ allocation, transplant committees should audit which stakeholders were polled, what features were scoped as "relevant," and how dilemmas were framed.
2. **Content moderation and generative AI safety**: Social media platforms and AI labs auditing whose ethical preferences shaped content filters and model training.
3. **Hiring and employment AI**: Before an AI system decides on hiring, absence handling, or firing, firms should disclose the moral feature set and voter composition that shaped it.
4. **Public consultation on autonomous vehicles**: City planners holding participatory votes on trolley-problem-like scenarios should document question framing and voter demographics.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Moral AI Audit Platform**: A SaaS tool that generates automated reports on the feature-sampling-framing choices embedded in a company's moral AI system, with version history and transparency dashboards for regulators.
2. **Value Elicitation Consulting Firm**: Advise companies on best practices for *transparent* moral preference elicitation, including training on how question framing shapes outcomes and how to document design choices.
3. **Participatory Ethics Simulation Tool**: Interactive software that lets organizations test multiple framings/voter compositions on the same dilemma before deployment, showing sensitivity analyses.
4. **AI Bias and Ethics Due Diligence**: Third-party auditors who examine the hidden developer choices in alignment pipelines and certify (or flag) them for investors, regulators, or customers.
5. **Open Moral Preference Repository**: Crowdsourced database of moral preference profiles across different feature schemas, voter demographics, and framings — so the community can see how choices ripple through outcomes.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Hypothetical bias**: Participants voted on *scenarios*, not real-world decisions. Real-world moral choices carry stakes and emotion that vignettes don't capture.
2. **Limited demographic breadth**: N=809 across two phases in one country (implied); may not generalize across cultures, education levels, or age distributions.
3. **No model training**: The paper demonstrates effects on preference elicitation but doesn't show how these shifted preferences actually change downstream AI behavior when trained end-to-end.
4. **Framing test depth**: The framing experiments tested 2–3 variants per question; a full sensitivity analysis would explore many more phrasings.
5. **No solution provided**: The paper diagnoses the problem but doesn't propose an algorithmic or methodological fix—just calls for audit and transparency, which are necessary but not sufficient.
6. **Ideological measurement**: Political ideology was measured via self-report; more fine-grained value frameworks might reveal different patterns.

## Open Questions
1. **Can we design a "bias-resilient" elicitation process** that produces robust moral preferences across feature schemas and framings? Or is some framing-sensitivity inevitable?
2. **What is the right aggregation when preferences split ideologically?** Should AI systems reflect majority values, seek consensus, or express the disagreement transparently?
3. **How do real-world stakes (e.g., actual lives in organ allocation) change preference elicitation vs. hypothetical settings?** Does the effect size grow or shrink?
4. **Can we automatically detect and measure "framing effects" in natural language** to flag high-sensitivity questions before deployment?
5. **How do these findings scale to **multi-stakeholder** moral dilemmas where different groups have legitimate but conflicting preferences?** (Not just ideology, but patients vs. doctors vs. ethicists, etc.)
6. **What is the relationship between moral preference instability and the legitimacy of the resulting AI system?** When should stakeholders reject an AI system because it reflects unstable or contested values?

## Key Takeaway
Developer choices about which moral questions to ask, whom to ask, and how to phrase them invisibly shape AI ethics—requiring mandatory disclosure and audit.
