# Continual Reinforcement Learning with Neuroevolution

**Authors:** Eleni Nisioti, Andrea Cossu, Kathrin Korte, Sebastian Risi
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.01583v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01583v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether neuroevolution—an optimization approach that evolves neural networks through mutation and selection rather than gradient descent—can better handle continual learning tasks where an agent must adapt to changing environments without forgetting prior knowledge. The authors compare evolution strategies and genetic algorithms against state-of-the-art reinforcement learning methods and find that evolution strategies consistently achieve better stability-plasticity trade-offs, suggesting that weight-space perturbations may be a fundamentally better approach for learning across shifting tasks.

## Theoretical Background
Continual reinforcement learning addresses a core challenge in RL: when task distributions shift over time, standard gradient-based learning methods often suffer from "plasticity loss"—the inability to adapt to new tasks—or catastrophic forgetting of old ones. This tension between adaptation and stability is called the stability-plasticity dilemma. Neuroevolution sidesteps gradient-based optimization entirely by treating learning as a search problem: maintain a population of neural networks, score each on the current task, and breed/mutate the best performers. Evolution strategies (ES) use Gaussian perturbations and natural selection; genetic algorithms (GAs) use recombination and mutation operators. The authors analyze these approaches through the lens of loss landscape geometry—specifically, the width and shape of regions in weight space where a trained policy remains effective.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical comparison**: First systematic evaluation of neuroevolution methods against modern continual RL baselines across diverse environments, showing ES consistently outperforms on the stability-plasticity trade-off.
2. **Landscape analysis**: Demonstrates that ES finds wider "neighborhoods" in weight space (regions robust to perturbations), and the overlap between neighborhoods of consecutive tasks correlates with continual learning success.
3. **Absence of plasticity-loss pathologies**: Shows that common failure modes in gradient-based RL (e.g., dead neurons, learning rate decay problems) do not appear in neuroevolution, suggesting the optimization paradigm itself confers robustness.
4. **Plasticity-diversity interaction**: Reveals that novelty search increases GA plasticity but worsens forgetting, indicating a mechanistic link between behavioral diversity and forgetting.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Continual Learning]], [[Plasticity-Stability Dilemma]], and classical evolutionary computation. It challenges the implicit assumption that gradient-based methods are necessary for modern RL (as in [[Deep Q-Networks]], policy gradients, and actor-critic methods). The landscape analysis builds on loss landscape visualization techniques from interpretability research and connects to work on flat minima and generalization. The paper also engages with population-based RL methods (e.g., population-based training) and novelty search, showing how those ideas apply to the continual learning setting.

## Practical Applications
1. **Robotics in shifting environments**: Robots that must adapt to wear, changing terrain, or task drift without retraining from scratch (e.g., warehouse robots operating under seasonal or facility-specific changes).
2. **Autonomous vehicles**: Continual adaptation to new weather, road conditions, or traffic patterns without forgetting previously learned behaviors.
3. **Video game AI or interactive systems**: NPCs or game-playing agents that face evolving player strategies or level designs over extended play sessions.
4. **Recommendation systems**: Adapting to user preference shifts or seasonal trends without catastrophic forgetting of historical user behavior patterns.
5. **Industrial control**: Process control in manufacturing or chemical plants where operating conditions drift over time.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Continual Learning Platform for Robotics**: A no-code/low-code framework wrapping neuroevolution for industrial robots, enabling factories to deploy policies that self-adapt to equipment wear or task variation without manual retraining.
2. **Adaptive Game AI Engine**: A game development tool that auto-generates NPC behaviors using evolution strategies, allowing difficulty and strategy to evolve dynamically as players progress.
3. **Trading Strategy Evolution Service**: A quantitative trading platform that uses neuroevolution to evolve trading policies in non-stationary markets, leveraging ES's robustness to market regime shifts.
4. **Autonomous Vehicle Continual Learning SDK**: Middleware for AV stacks that uses ES-based policies to adapt to regional or seasonal driving conditions without retraining the entire model pipeline.
5. **Continual Learning Consultancy for Legacy RL Systems**: Services to migrate existing gradient-based RL agents (in production systems) to neuroevolution backends for improved stability in shifting task distributions, with landscape analysis diagnostics.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational cost**: Neuroevolution requires evaluating entire population each generation; the paper does not thoroughly discuss wall-clock time or sample efficiency compared to gradient methods, which typically use fewer function evaluations per step.
2. **Scalability ambiguity**: While the paper tests up to million-parameter networks, it's unclear how ES scales to modern large networks (billions of parameters) where gradient methods excel.
3. **Benchmark diversity**: Experiments appear focused on RL environments (likely Atari, MuJoCo variants); generalization to other domains (NLP, vision) is unexplored.
4. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The paper does not deeply analyze sensitivity of ES/GA to population size, mutation rates, or other hyperparameters in continual settings—these may require per-domain tuning.
5. **Task relatedness assumption**: The stability-plasticity trade-off analysis assumes tasks are somewhat related; performance on highly disparate task sequences is unclear.
6. **Theoretical gaps**: The link between neighborhood overlap and continual learning success is empirical; no formal guarantees or theoretical bounds are provided.

## Open Questions
1. **Sample efficiency**: How does neuroevolution sample efficiency compare to gradient-based methods per unit wall-clock time in continual learning? Can ES be combined with replay buffers or other gradient-based tricks to improve efficiency?
2. **Large-scale experiments**: Do ES/GA maintain their advantage at billion-parameter scales, or do gradient methods become necessary?
3. **Task transfer metrics**: Can we predict continual learning difficulty from task similarity or landscape overlap *before* training, enabling meta-learning or curriculum design?
4. **Hybrid approaches**: Can gradient-based fine-tuning be combined with ES-found initializations to get the best of both worlds?
5. **Biological plausibility**: The paper touches on perturbation-based learning; could these insights inform understanding of synaptic noise and learning in biological neural systems?
6. **Continual learning in other domains**: Does the ES advantage hold in vision, NLP, or other fields where gradient-based methods dominate?

## Key Takeaway
Evolution strategies match or outperform gradient-based continual RL by finding flatter solutions that generalize across shifting tasks without catastrophic forgetting.
