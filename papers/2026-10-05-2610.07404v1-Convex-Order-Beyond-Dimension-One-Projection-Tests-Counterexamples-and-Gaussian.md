# Convex Order Beyond Dimension One: Projection Tests, Counterexamples and Gaussian Mixtures

**Authors:** Olivier Guéant
**Published:** 2026-10-05 | **arXiv:** [2610.07404v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07404v1)

## Summary
This paper resolves a long-standing question about how to compare probability distributions in high dimensions when they have the same mean — a core problem in actuarial science and finance. The key finding: checking all one-dimensional "slices" (projections) of two distributions suffices to determine convex order when comparing a Gaussian with a mixture of Gaussians, even though this test fails for general distributions.

## Theoretical Background
Convex order is a mathematical tool for ranking random variables (or probability distributions) when they share the same mean but differ in risk or variability. In one dimension, this ordering is straightforward through established characterizations. The natural extension to higher dimensions — comparing all possible one-dimensional projections — is *necessary* but not *sufficient* in general (a gap highlighted by Pinelis using Popoviciu's inequality on variance). The paper builds on recent work by Jourdain and Pagès comparing Gaussian distributions with Gaussian mixtures, where they established necessary and sufficient conditions but left open whether these conditions are equivalent in general. The framework uses gauge functions (homogeneous convex functions) and radial distributions (where probability density depends only on distance from origin) to structure the analysis.

## Core Contributions
1. **Explicit counterexamples via gauge functions** – Revisits and extends Pinelis's counterexample, showing that gauge functions alone cannot characterize multivariate convex order even for symmetric distributions in dimension two.
2. **Radial counterexamples** – Constructs a systematic family of counterexamples in every dimension ≥ 2 using radial distributions, making transparent why projection tests fail in general.
3. **Positive result for Gaussian mixtures** – **Proves that projection tests ARE sufficient** when comparing a centered Gaussian with a mixture of two centered Gaussians in any finite dimension. This closes an open question: the Gaussian is dominated by the mixture in convex order if and only if all one-dimensional projections satisfy convex order.
4. **Sufficiency for linear transformations** – Shows projection tests work for any family of distributions that are linear transformations of a common radially distributed random vector (includes all elliptical distributions with common radial generator).

## Connections to Other Work
This work directly extends [[Gaussian mixture modeling]] problems in quantitative finance and actuarial science. It refines the framework of Jourdain and Pagès by settling their open equivalence question for the two-component Gaussian mixture case. The use of radial distributions and gauge functions connects to classical convex analysis and stochastic dominance theory. The result bridges [[risk-ordering]] methods (used in portfolio comparison and insurance) with modern distributional comparisons in high dimensions.

## Practical Applications
- **Portfolio risk assessment**: Comparing tail risk and variability of multivariate portfolio return distributions when means are equal.
- **Insurance and actuarial pricing**: Determining whether one claims distribution is riskier than another under convex ordering (relevant for premium setting and reserve calculation).
- **Model validation**: When fitting Gaussian mixtures to real financial data, verifying whether the mixture captures more tail risk than a single Gaussian baseline.
- **Stress testing**: In risk management, determining whether scenario-based distributions satisfy ordering relationships for regulatory compliance.

## Technical Limitations
- **Restricted scope**: The main positive result applies only to Gaussian and Gaussian mixture comparisons; projection tests still fail for broader distribution families (e.g., heavy-tailed or skewed distributions).
- **Two-component restriction**: The settled result covers mixtures of *two* Gaussians; extension to three or more components remains open.
- **Centered distributions only**: The main theorem requires *centered* Gaussians; non-centered cases are not addressed.
- **Dimension dependence**: While proven for all finite dimensions, computational cost of projection testing scales badly; no dimension-reduction technique is provided.
- **Practical testing**: The paper is purely theoretical; no algorithm is proposed for testing convex order from finite samples (only asymptotic theory is developed).

## Open Questions
1. Does the projection test suffice for Gaussian mixtures with 3+ components?
2. Can the result extend to non-centered Gaussians and their mixtures?
3. What is the finite-sample complexity of testing convex order empirically? How many projections must be sampled?
4. Do similar results hold for other heavy-tailed mixture models (Student's t, stable distributions) relevant in finance?
5. Can gauge functions or other convex analytic tools characterize multivariate convex order for broader symmetric distribution classes?

## Key Takeaway
Projection tests fully determine Gaussian-to-Gaussian-mixture convex order, settling an open theoretical question and enabling practical risk comparisons in high dimensions.
