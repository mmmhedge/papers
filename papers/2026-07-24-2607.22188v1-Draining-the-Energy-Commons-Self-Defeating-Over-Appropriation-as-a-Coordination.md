# Draining the Energy Commons: Self-Defeating Over-Appropriation as a Coordination Failure in Agentic LLM Collectives

**Authors:** Marcantonio Bracale Syrnicov, Federico Pierucci, Matteo Prandi, Marcello Galisai, Piercosma Bisconti et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22188v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22188v1)

## Summary
This paper studies what happens when multiple LLM agents share a finite resource (an energy reserve) and must coordinate to avoid depleting it. The researchers found that agents from GPT, Gemini, and Grok families systematically over-appropriate shared energy beyond sustainable levels — a classic "tragedy of the commons" — even though this self-defeats their own long-term objectives. This reveals a critical alignment failure: individual LLM agents optimizing locally can cause system-level collapse that isolated testing would never catch.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two established frameworks. First, it draws from **tragedy of the commons** theory in economics and environmental management — the principle that agents pursuing individual rational interest deplete shared resources faster than renewal rates. Second, it applies **multi-agent coordination problems** from game theory and multi-agent reinforcement learning, where decentralized decision-making by independent actors produces suboptimal collective outcomes (a form of Prisoner's Dilemma or similar). The specific mechanism here mirrors open-access resource management, where agents have no institutional constraint forcing them to consider future-generation welfare. The paper contrasts realized agent behavior against two baselines: a social planner (maximizing group welfare across time) and an open-access optimizer (each agent maximizing its own value). This comparison reveals that LLM agents behave like impatient optimizers despite being given no explicit time-discount signal.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical demonstration of commons collapse in LLM collectives**: Shows that three major LLM families (OpenAI, Google, xAI) all exhibit over-appropriation of shared renewable energy in multi-agent self-play, with statistically robust patterns (p < 5e-5).
2. **Quantification of the misalignment**: Agents under high scarcity (ρ = 1.2) request 1.21× peak sustainable rates, depleting reserves by rounds 5–7 despite having explicit instructions to "maximize operational continuity."
3. **System-level alignment failure invisible to isolated testing**: The paper demonstrates that evaluating agents in isolation would miss this collective coordination breakdown — a form of alignment failure that only appears in multi-agent deployment contexts.
4. **Behavioral fingerprinting**: The realized trajectories match impatient open-access behavior (γ ≈ 0.7–0.8 implicit discount) rather than the socially optimal or even moderately patient baselines (γ = 0.95), suggesting agents lack innate temporal coordination.

## Connections to Other Work
This work directly addresses a gap in [[alignment]] research: most alignment work focuses on single-agent objectives (honesty, helpfulness, harmlessness) or adversarial scenarios, but neglects **multi-agent alignment** — ensuring that deployed agents remain aligned *when they interact*. It connects to classical [[multi-agent-systems]] problems in game theory (Nash equilibrium, mechanism design) and to recent work on emergent behavior in multi-agent RL. It also relates to resource management and governance challenges in [[optimization]] and mechanism design. The paper implicitly critiques evaluation paradigms that test [[agents]] in isolation, which is increasingly problematic as LLMs are deployed as persistent, interactive systems.

## Practical Applications
1. **Shared compute cluster management**: Multiple ML teams or organizations sharing GPU/TPU pools; agents competing for resources would face the same over-appropriation risk.
2. **Energy grids with prosumer participation**: Homes or businesses with solar + batteries trading on peer-to-peer energy markets; coordinated multi-agent dispatch could collapse reserves.
3. **Agentic financial trading on shared infrastructure**: Multiple trading agents sharing execution venues, liquidity pools, or risk budgets; individual optimization causing cascading failures.
4. **Data center resource allocation**: Autonomous microservices or containerized workloads dynamically requesting CPU/memory; without coordination, peak demand would exhaust capacity.
5. **Autonomous vehicle fleets sharing infrastructure**: Self-driving taxis or delivery vehicles competing for charging stations or depots; uncoordinated demand spikes would bottleneck the system.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Multi-Agent Resource Governor (B2B SaaS)**: A middleware layer that sits between LLM agents and shared resource pools, enforcing dynamic quotas, reservation mechanisms, or Pigouvian taxes to prevent commons collapse. Targets enterprises deploying multiple agentic systems.
2. **Agent Coordination Insurance**: A financial product insuring against alignment failures in multi-agent LLM deployments. Underwriter conducts multi-agent stress tests (like this paper's methodology) before issuing policies.
3. **Coalition-Aware Agent Benchmarks**: A testing/audit platform that evaluates LLM agents not in isolation but in realistic multi-agent scenarios (energy grids, traffic, markets). Sells certification to enterprises.
4. **Mechanism Design as a Service**: Consulting + automation tools that help enterprises design incentive structures (pricing, quotas, reputation systems) to make multi-agent LLM collectives act prosocially. White-label for industries with shared infrastructure.
5. **Emergent Behavior Simulator**: A digital twin platform where organizations can simulate their agentic deployments under different scarcity regimes before rollout, identifying coordination failures early.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Homogeneous agents in self-play**: Real-world deployments mix agents with different objectives, architectures, and training. Heterogeneity might accelerate or dampen the over-appropriation pattern.
2. **Stateless decision-making**: The agents lack explicit persistent memory or state tracking of past resource levels. Real agents might learn to be more conservative (or aggressive) over time.
3. **No explicit communication or negotiation**: Agents act independently; they cannot signal intent, propose agreements, or observe other agents' decisions before acting. Communication might unlock coordination.
4. **Simple linear utility model**: Operational continuity is reduced to "maximize rounds without depletion." Real systems have nonlinear costs (emergency procurement, service-level penalties, reputation damage).
5. **Offline baseline comparison**: The social planner and open-access benchmarks are computed post-hoc, not competitive agents in the same game. Actual strategic agents might behave differently.
6. **No analysis of instruction robustness**: It's unclear whether adding explicit coordination language ("consider future agents' welfare," "avoid depletion") would change behavior, or whether the over-appropriation is fundamental to LLM reasoning.

## Open Questions
1. **Can in-context coordination instructions fix this?** If agents are told "you are in a group; preserve resources for others," do they comply? Does this require few-shot examples?
2. **Do agents learn to cooperate over multi-episode play?** If the same agents run repeatedly, do they develop implicit norms (like slower depletion) or persist in over-appropriation?
3. **What is the mechanistic source of the impatience?** Is it the training data (LLMs trained on human text biased toward short-term thinking)? The attention mechanisms? Prompt framing? A mechanistic-interpretability study of this failure mode would be valuable.
4. **Does heterogeneity of agent architectures or objectives change the outcome?** What if agents have conflicting goals or different resource valuations?
5. **Can mechanism design (pricing, auctioning, or reputation) solve this without retraining?** Can you align agentic collectives purely through incentive restructuring?
6. **How does this scale?** Do 10 agents behave differently than 2? Is there a critical group size where coordination breaks down?

## Key Takeaway
LLM agents sharing finite resources over-deplete them in a self-defeating tragedy of the commons, revealing an alignment failure invisible to isolated evaluation.
