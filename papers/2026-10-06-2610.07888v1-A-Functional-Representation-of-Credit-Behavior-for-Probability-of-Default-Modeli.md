# A Functional Representation of Credit Behavior for Probability of Default Modeling

**Authors:** Jonas Brunholm, Bjarne Højgaard, Thomas D. Nielsen, Orimar Sauri
**Published:** 2026-10-06 | **arXiv:** [2610.07888v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07888v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes a new way to predict whether borrowers will default on loans by treating credit behavior as continuous functions over time rather than monthly snapshots. Instead of aggregating credit data into discrete monthly values, the authors retain account balances, credit limits, and account types as continuous processes, then use functional logistic regression to predict default risk—achieving performance comparable to XGBoost while remaining interpretable enough for regulators.

## Theoretical Background
Credit risk modeling traditionally relies on logistic regression, which is interpretable and regulatory-friendly but often underperforms machine learning methods. This work builds on functional data analysis (FDA)—a statistical framework for analyzing data that are curves or functions rather than discrete points. The paper also leverages standard credit risk variables like utilization rate, available funds, and overdraft behavior, which are typically computed as monthly aggregates. The authors recognize that these aggregates lose intra-monthly information and cyclical patterns (e.g., weekly spending cycles around paydays) that may predict default better.

## Core Contributions
1. **Continuous functional representation**: Shows that three time-varying quantities (account type, balance, credit limit) can faithfully reconstruct all commonly used credit variables without information loss from aggregation.

2. **Relative time alignment**: Develops a framework that aligns recurring events (e.g., banking day cycles) across borrowers using ordinal position rather than calendar time, removing borrower-specific timing drift and revealing universal cyclical patterns.

3. **Functional logistic regression for credit**: Applies functional data analysis to default prediction, demonstrating that the continuous representation recovers XGBoost-level predictive power while preserving the interpretability of linear models—a critical advantage under regulatory scrutiny.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of classical credit risk modeling (logistic regression, [[risk-management]] in finance) and modern machine learning (XGBoost, feature engineering). It addresses a perennial tension in regulated industries: regulators favor interpretable models (linear models, [[mechanistic-interpretability]]-adjacent) but business teams want predictive power ([[quantitative-finance]], machine learning). The relative time framework resembles alignment techniques in time-series analysis and recalls functional data approaches used in other domains where cyclicality matters.

## Practical Applications
1. **Loan origination**: Financial institutions can use this model to make faster, more accurate approval/denial decisions at application time.

2. **Portfolio risk monitoring**: Banks can continuously update default probabilities for existing borrowers as new transaction data arrives, improving early warning systems.

3. **Regulatory reporting**: The model's interpretability makes it easier to explain decisions to regulators and auditors, reducing compliance friction.

4. **Credit limit optimization**: By understanding which cyclical patterns predict default, lenders can dynamically adjust credit limits or alert customers at high-risk points in their cycle.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RegTech compliance tool**: SaaS platform that wraps functional data models to help smaller banks deploy machine-learning-grade default prediction while maintaining regulatory approval and audit trail documentation.

2. **Real-time credit risk API**: Continuous monitoring service that consumes transaction feeds and returns updated default probabilities and early warning signals, sold to fintech lenders and embedded finance platforms.

3. **Credit bureau alternative**: A data analytics startup that partners with neobanks to build proprietary functional models of borrower credit behavior, offering alternative credit scoring for underbanked populations.

4. **Dynamic pricing engine**: Uses cyclical behavioral patterns to optimize loan pricing, APR, and terms at the time of origination and dynamically through the loan lifecycle.

5. **Borrower intervention platform**: Alerts and nudges tool that identifies when a borrower enters a high-risk phase of their transactional cycle and triggers outreach (refinancing offers, spending tips) before default.

## Technical Limitations
- **Data availability**: Requires high-frequency (intra-daily or real-time) transaction data; many lenders or credit bureaus only retain monthly snapshots or aggregates.
- **Interpretability trade-off**: While more interpretable than XGBoost, functional logistic regression is still less immediately intuitive than simple logistic regression on hand-crafted features; regulators unfamiliar with FDA may require additional explanation.
- **Cyclical assumption**: The framework assumes all borrowers exhibit repeating cycles aligned to ordinal banking days; this may not hold for very small accounts, inactive accounts, or accounts with irregular usage.
- **Extrapolation**: Functional models can be fragile when applied to new borrower types or economic regimes that violate the cyclical patterns observed in training data.
- **Computational cost**: Functional data analysis and alignment procedures add computational overhead compared to simple logistic regression, though likely acceptable for batch scoring.

## Open Questions
1. How does the functional model generalize to borrowers from different geographies or banking systems with different payment cycles?
2. Can the framework be extended to competing risks (e.g., prepayment, account closure) simultaneously?
3. What is the minimum frequency of transaction data required to capture the cyclical structure without noise?
4. How stable are the learned functional patterns over economic cycles (booms vs. recessions)?
5. Can explainability techniques (e.g., SHAP, influence functions) be applied to functional logistic regression to further aid regulatory approval?

## Key Takeaway
Continuous transaction-level data reveals cyclical borrower patterns that improve default prediction while staying interpretable for regulators.
