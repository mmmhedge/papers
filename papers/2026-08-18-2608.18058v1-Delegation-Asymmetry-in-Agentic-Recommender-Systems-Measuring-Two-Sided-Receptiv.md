# Delegation Asymmetry in Agentic Recommender Systems: Measuring Two-Sided Receptivity in Online Dating

**Authors:** Daria Leshchikova, Valentina V. Kuskova, Dmitry Zaytsev, Valerii Klimov
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18058v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18058v1)

## Summary

This paper studies a critical but overlooked problem in AI-mediated dating platforms: users must be willing not just to delegate conversation to an autonomous agent, but also to receive messages from other users' agents. Using surveys of nearly 3,000 dating app users, the authors show these are psychologically distinct choices with a dramatic asymmetry—people are far more willing to deploy their own agent than to engage with someone else's. This matters because most agent-based platforms assume symmetric receptivity, but real deployments will fail if this imbalance isn't designed for.

## Theoretical Background

This work builds on research in human-agent interaction, measurement theory (specifically graded response models — statistical tools for measuring latent psychological constructs from survey data), and two-sided marketplace design. Prior work on recommender systems and matching platforms has focused on algorithmic quality, but largely ignored the behavioral prerequisites for agentic systems. The paper also draws on social psychology concepts around delegation (giving tasks to others) and receptivity (willingness to accept something from others). The measurement approach uses item response theory, a framework that estimates underlying traits from multiple survey responses, allowing the authors to separate correlated but distinct constructs.

## Core Contributions

1. **Identification of delegation asymmetry**: Empirically demonstrates that willingness to deploy an agent (threshold: -0.38 on latent scale) and willingness to engage a counterpart's agent (threshold: +0.32) are measurably distinct constructs, highly correlated but separable via statistical model comparison (Δ BIC = 52).

2. **Quantification of mismatch**: Shows that under realistic pairing scenarios, only 4–13% of directed interactions successfully combine deployment with engagement—a severe constraint on viability.

3. **Gender-directional imbalance**: Documents that the asymmetry is not uniform; gender-specific patterns in deployment vs. engagement create additional friction.

4. **Actionable design interventions**: Tests concrete mitigation levers (reciprocity requirements, receptivity-aware routing) and shows routing on receive receptivity triples per-contact engagement with external validation (AUC 0.88, 3.1× quartile lift).

5. **Measurement model with partial invariance**: Develops a psychometrically sound latent-variable model that holds across languages, enabling cross-cultural comparisons.

This research sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent-systems]] (where coordination and mutual acceptance are central), recommender system design (e.g., matching algorithms in two-sided markets), and human-centered AI. It relates conceptually to work on [[alignment]] and user trust in autonomous systems, though focuses on behavioral measurement rather than safety. The finding of asymmetric user preferences echoes phenomena in other delegation contexts (e.g., automation bias, over-trust in one's own tools) but here provides rare quantitative evidence in a real, high-stakes application domain.

## Practical Applications

- **Dating platform design**: Implement receptivity-aware message routing and agent-contact prioritization to increase engagement without forcing users into symmetric reciprocity.
- **Disclosure and consent UX**: Use measured receptivity thresholds to calibrate which users see agent notices, reducing friction from unsolicited agent-mediated contact.
- **Two-sided marketplace platforms** (beyond dating): Apply the measurement model to other platforms where autonomous agents mediate transactions (e.g., customer service, recruitment, peer-to-peer services).
- **A/B testing frameworks**: Use the receptivity construct as a diagnostic metric to evaluate agentic feature rollouts and identify which user segments resist agent-mediated interactions.
- **Gender-inclusive design**: Tailor agent deployment policies to account for observed gender-directional imbalances, preventing marginalization of certain user groups.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **Receptivity-Adaptive Agent Middleware**: A platform layer that sits between dating/matching apps and LLM agent services, measuring user receptivity via lightweight signals (behavior, surveys) and automatically routing agent messages only to receptive users while respecting deployment asymmetry. B2B SaaS model.

2. **Agent Trust & Disclosure Toolkit**: A white-label package for two-sided platforms implementing the graded response model, providing dashboards to measure user comfort with agents, A/B test disclosure framing, and track engagement lifts from receptivity-aware routing.

3. **Asymmetry-Aware Matchmaking API**: A matchmaking service that, instead of treating all users uniformly, clusters users by deployment vs. engagement receptivity and pairs agent-deployers only with high-engagement users, improving match quality and reducing abandonment.

4. **Agent Reputation & Reciprocity Market**: A consumer app where users can opt into a "verified agent" tier with reciprocity requirements (if you deploy, you accept agents), creating a smaller but higher-trust submarket within dating platforms.

5. **Behavioral Segmentation for Agentic Systems**: A B2B analytics tool for any platform rolling out autonomous agents (customer service, job platforms, marketplace apps) that measures and visualizes deployment-vs.-engagement asymmetry by demographic, predicts which segments will churn, and recommends design changes.

## Technical Limitations

- **Survey-based measurement**: Relies on stated preferences, not revealed behavior—users may overstate comfort with agents in surveys or behave differently when actually exposed to agent-mediated contact.
- **Single-platform study**: Findings come from one dating platform; generalizability to other contexts (recruitment, customer service, B2B) unknown.
- **Cross-sectional design**: Cannot establish causality or measure how receptivity evolves over time; longitudinal follow-up needed to test whether routing interventions sustain engagement or trigger habituation or backlash.
- **Latent construct validity**: The graded response model assumes a unidimensional underlying receptivity construct; unmeasured heterogeneity (e.g., agent type, conversation quality) could confound results.
- **Partial invariance only**: Model does not fully hold across languages, raising questions about cultural differences in agent receptivity that the paper does not fully explore.
- **Counterfactual assumptions**: The random-pairing counterfactual and routing simulation assume user preferences remain stable if routing changes, ignoring potential behavioral adaptation (e.g., learning, strategic gaming).

## Open Questions

- **Temporal dynamics**: How do receptivity and deployment propensity evolve after users have experience with agent-mediated interactions? Does the asymmetry shrink or harden?
- **Agent quality effects**: Does receptivity depend on agent quality or conversation quality? The paper treats agents as a monolithic category; segmenting by agent performance (e.g., conversation coherence, match accuracy) could reveal thresholds where engagement jumps.
- **Causal mechanisms**: Why does the asymmetry exist? Is it distrust in others' agents, privacy concerns, or something else? Targeted experiments manipulating disclosure, agent transparency, or guarantees could isolate drivers.
- **Out-of-platform generalization**: Do these findings hold in other two-sided platforms (e.g., job matching, customer service, marketplace apps)? A multi-platform study is needed.
- **Long-term platform effects**: Do routing interventions that triple per-contact engagement sustain conversion (dates, matches, sales) or just engagement with the agent? Revenue impact is unmeasured.
- **Bias and fairness**: The noted gender-directional imbalance deserves deeper analysis. Which genders prefer deploying vs. receiving, and do the proposed design interventions exacerbate or mitigate gender-based friction?

## Key Takeaway

Users are three times more willing to deploy their own agent than to receive strangers' agents—a massive asymmetry that breaks two-sided agentic platforms unless routing and design account for it.
