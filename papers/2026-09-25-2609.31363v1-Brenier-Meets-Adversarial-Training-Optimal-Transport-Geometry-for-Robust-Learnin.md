# Brenier Meets Adversarial Training: Optimal Transport Geometry for Robust Learning

**Authors:** Alireza Abdollahpoorrostam, Ehsan Sharifian, Buse Şen, Marco Cuturi, Daniel Kuhn
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31363v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31363v1)

## Summary

This paper solves a key problem in robust machine learning: how to train models that perform well even when data distributions shift unexpectedly. The authors connect optimal transport theory (a mathematical framework for comparing probability distributions) with adversarial training (a technique that hardens models against worst-case inputs), proving that proper robustness requires respecting geometric constraints called cyclic monotonicity—something standard adversarial training violates. They propose two practical fixes that consistently outperform existing methods on tasks ranging from image classification to control problems.

## Theoretical Background

Distributionally robust optimization (DRO) seeks to build models that handle distribution shift by protecting against a worst-case distribution within some distance (measured by a Wasserstein metric—a way to quantify how different two probability distributions are). Standard adversarial training defends against attacks by tweaking individual samples, but this is geometrically inefficient. Optimal transport theory, pioneered by Gaspard Monge and later formalized via the Kantorovich problem, asks: what is the cheapest way to transform one distribution into another? A key insight is Brenier's theorem, which states that optimal transport maps between smooth distributions have a special structure: they are gradients of convex functions and satisfy cyclic monotonicity (a global consistency constraint). The paper's core insight is that robust learning through adversarial perturbations is actually an optimal transport problem in disguise, and ignoring the geometric structure of optimal maps leads to inefficient and weaker defenses.

## Core Contributions

1. **Reformulation as optimal transport**: Proves that the Wasserstein-penalized DRO adversary's problem is equivalent to finding optimal transport maps pushing empirical samples to adversarial versions, with cyclic monotonicity as a necessary optimality condition.

2. **Diagnosis of standard adversarial training**: Shows formally that per-sample local adversarial optimization violates cyclic monotonicity and wastes transport budget, leading to suboptimal robustness.

3. **Multi-start particle ascent (MPSA)**: A practical algorithm that enforces cyclic monotonicity by alternating parallel gradient ascent on all samples with reassignment, ensuring global consistency.

4. **Input-convex neural networks (ICNNs) as transport maps**: Parameterizes adversarial perturbations as gradients of ICNNs, which automatically satisfy cyclic monotonicity by construction—a structural guarantee.

5. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates consistent improvements over baselines on robust regression, image classification, and robust control tasks, with better generalization under distribution shift.

## Practical Applications

1. **Autonomous driving robustness**: Train perception and control systems to handle distribution shifts (weather, sensor degradation, new environments) with mathematical guarantees on worst-case performance.

2. **Medical imaging under domain shift**: Build classifiers robust to variations in scanner hardware, patient populations, and imaging protocols by enforcing transport-theoretic consistency.

3. **Financial fraud detection**: Defend against adversarial data poisoning by adversaries constrained to realistic distribution budgets (Wasserstein balls), improving model reliability under market regime changes.

4. **Adversarial example defense for NLP**: Harden language models against compositional attacks by respecting semantic transport geometry rather than isolated word-level perturbations.

5. **Reinforcement learning in sim-to-real transfer**: Train control policies robust to environment model mismatch using optimal transport penalties, ensuring physical plausibility of adversarial shifts.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **RobustML-as-a-service**: A cloud platform that automatically hardens user-supplied models using ICNN-based transport maps, with no retraining required—apply a "robustness wrapper" to any differentiable model.

2. **Autonomous vehicle certifier**: Software that validates perception and control pipelines using Wasserstein DRO bounds, providing insurers and regulators mathematical certificates of robustness.

3. **Domain adaptation marketplace**: A toolkit for enterprises to adapt models across data distributions (supplier A to supplier B, region X to region Y) using principled optimal transport, not heuristic fine-tuning.

4. **Enterprise fraud/anomaly detection**: Off-the-shelf classifier that explicitly handles distribution shift via transport penalties, marketed to fintech, e-commerce, and insurance as "adversarial-proof" detection.

5. **Robust reinforcement learning simulator**: A simulation environment where RL agents train against worst-case environments within a Wasserstein budget, ensuring learned policies transfer to real-world distribution shifts.

## Technical Limitations

- **Computational cost**: MPSA requires parallel gradient ascent across all samples and periodic reassignment, scaling poorly to very large datasets; ICNNs add architectural constraints that may reduce expressiveness.
- **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: The Wasserstein penalty weight and MPSA reassignment frequency are critical but require tuning; no principled selection rule is provided.
- **Cyclicity in high dimensions**: Cyclic monotonicity is exact only for smooth, sufficiently separated distributions; in high-dimensional spaces with clusters or discrete modes, the geometric structure may be poorly approximated.
- **Limited to continuous perturbations**: The framework assumes the adversary can smoothly move samples; it does not naturally handle discrete attacks (e.g., missing features, label flips) or hard constraints.
- **Computational budget for adversary unclear**: The paper assumes the Wasserstein budget is fixed, but the relationship between this budget and real-world distribution shift magnitude is problem-dependent and often unknown.

## Open Questions

1. **Sample complexity and finite-sample guarantees**: How many samples are needed to reliably estimate cyclic monotone transport maps? What are generalization bounds?

2. **Adaptive adversaries**: What if the adversary knows about the Wasserstein penalty and structures attacks to exploit it? Can the approach handle adversaries with different penalty structures?

3. **Comparison to other geometric robust methods**: How does this compare theoretically and empirically to other transport-based robustness methods or certifiable defenses?

4. **Extension to discrete/structured perturbations**: Can cyclic monotonicity be adapted for discrete actions, graph perturbations, or semantic attacks?

5. **Efficient solvers for large-scale MPSA**: Can the reassignment problem be solved approximately or hierarchically? What is the trade-off between reassignment frequency and convergence?

6. **Tightness of Wasserstein bounds in practice**: In real distribution shifts (e.g., domain adaptation benchmarks), how tight are the actual Wasserstein distances? Is the penalty structure realistic?

## Key Takeaway

Optimal transport geometry reveals that standard adversarial training is geometrically inefficient; using transport-theoretic constraints provably improves robustness and generalization.
