# Global Convergence of DGM and PINN Algorithms for Solving Nonlinear PDEs

**Authors:** Justin Sirignano, Konstantinos Spiliopoulos, Samuel Cohen
**Published:** 2026-07-27 | **arXiv:** [2607.24726v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24726v1)

## Summary
This paper proves that neural networks trained with gradient descent to solve nonlinear PDEs (partial differential equations) will converge to the actual PDE solution, not just to a local minimum of the loss function. This is a fundamental theoretical result for Deep Galerkin Method (DGM) and Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) — two increasingly popular techniques that use neural networks to solve scientific computing problems — because it answers the long-standing question of whether these methods actually work.

## Theoretical Background
PINNs and DGM solve PDEs by encoding the PDE constraint directly into a neural network's loss function. Instead of discretizing the domain (as traditional numerical methods do), they train a network to minimize the residual — the error left over when the network's prediction is plugged into the PDE. The challenge is that this residual objective is non-convex, meaning gradient descent could get stuck at a local minimum that doesn't satisfy the PDE. Prior work applied these methods empirically with success, but lacked convergence guarantees. The paper builds on optimization theory for neural networks and PDE theory, connecting gradient descent dynamics to the actual solution of semi-linear PDEs (those that are nonlinear in the solution and its first derivative).

## Core Contributions
The main contribution is a rigorous proof that for a class of semi-linear PDEs, gradient descent applied to the PDE residual objective globally converges to the true PDE solution. This is non-trivial because: (1) the objective is non-convex, (2) the solution space is infinite-dimensional, and (3) gradient descent typically only guarantees convergence to stationary points, not to specific solutions. The paper likely provides explicit convergence rates and conditions on the network architecture or learning dynamics required.

## Connections to Other Work
This work advances the theoretical understanding of [[Physics-Informed Neural Networks|PINNs]], which emerged as a framework for incorporating domain knowledge into neural networks. It relates to broader questions about neural network optimization (e.g., why does gradient descent escape bad local minima?) and connects to the intersection of deep learning and scientific computing. The result complements empirical successes and addresses concerns raised in recent work questioning PINN reliability on certain PDEs.

## Practical Applications
- **Computational physics**: Solving complex PDEs in fluid dynamics, heat transfer, or quantum mechanics where traditional finite-difference or finite-element methods are expensive or infeasible.
- **Real-time PDE solving**: Deployed neural networks can evaluate PDE solutions at any point instantly, useful for design optimization in engineering.
- **High-dimensional problems**: PDEs in 50+ dimensions are intractable for grid-based methods; neural networks scale better.
- **Inverse problems**: Inferring PDE coefficients from observational data by embedding the PDE as a constraint.

## Technical Limitations
- **Limited scope**: The result applies only to semi-linear PDEs; it is unclear if the proof extends to fully nonlinear PDEs (nonlinear in higher derivatives) or systems of PDEs common in practice.
- **Network architecture assumptions**: The proof likely requires specific network widths, depth, or activation functions; practical networks may not satisfy these conditions.
- **Convergence rates**: Even if global convergence is guaranteed, the rate may be very slow (exponential in dimension), making training impractical for high-dimensional PDEs.
- **Boundary conditions**: Handling of Dirichlet or mixed boundary conditions and their effect on convergence are not addressed.
- **Empirical-theory gap**: Theoretical assumptions (e.g., infinite width limits, smooth activations) may not reflect finite, practical networks used in applications.

## Open Questions
- Does the result extend to fully nonlinear PDEs (e.g., Hamilton-Jacobi equations, Monge-Ampère equations)?
- What are the explicit convergence rates in terms of network width, depth, and PDE parameters?
- How robust is convergence to modifications like mini-batch gradient descent, adaptive learning rates, or regularization?
- Can the analysis handle systems of PDEs or coupled PDEs arising in multiphysics?
- What happens empirically when the theoretical assumptions are violated (e.g., finite networks, non-smooth domains)?

## Key Takeaway
Gradient descent provably solves nonlinear PDEs with neural networks, settling a major open question in scientific machine learning.
