# ConventionPlay: Capability-Limited Training for Robust Ad-Hoc Collaboration

**Authors:** Abhishek Sriraman, Eleni Vasilaki, Robert Loftin
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.11842v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11842v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces ConventionPlay, a reinforcement learning method that trains agents to collaborate effectively with partners of unknown capabilities. Instead of assuming partners follow a single fixed strategy, the method trains agents against a diverse population where some partners are flexible and others are inflexible—forcing agents to learn to probe their partner's capabilities and steer them toward compatible shared strategies.

## Theoretical Background
Ad-hoc collaboration—where agents must work with unknown partners without prior coordination—is a core problem in multi-agent reinforcement learning. Prior work assumes partners follow established conventions (shared behavioral rules), and trains agents to quickly adapt to whatever convention the partner uses. This paper builds on convention learning and population-based training, but introduces a key insight: real partners often have heterogeneous capabilities—some can only follow one convention, while others can switch between a few. The theoretical foundation rests on the idea that training against this heterogeneous population (rather than homogeneous partners) creates pressure for agents to actively learn their partner's flexibility and coordinate accordingly.

## Core Contributions
1. **ConventionPlay algorithm**: An RL training procedure that constructs a learnable partner population explicitly varying in how many conventions each partner can execute (from single-convention to multi-convention agents).
2. **Active probing behavior**: The method induces trained agents to systematically test their partner's capabilities rather than passively adapting, leading to more robust collaboration.
3. **Empirical validation**: Demonstration that agents trained this way outperform existing ad-hoc collaboration baselines when tested against multi-convention-capable partners—a setting where prior methods fail.

## Connections to Other Work
This extends [[ad-hoc teamwork]] and [[convention learning]] in multi-agent RL by relaxing the assumption that partners are homogeneous. It relates to [[population-based training]] (training against diverse opponent populations) and [[curriculum learning]] (using progressively harder or more varied training conditions). The capability-limited training idea parallels [[behavioral cloning]] and [[inverse RL]] in that agents must infer latent properties (here, adaptability) about their partners. It also connects to work on [[emergent communication]] and [[cooperative RL]], where agents must discover shared strategies without explicit coordination protocols.

## Practical Applications
1. **Human-robot collaboration**: Robots working with humans who have varying levels of training or experience—the robot learns what the human can do and adapts accordingly.
2. **Autonomous vehicle coordination**: Self-driving cars collaborating with human drivers of different skill levels or communication preferences.
3. **Swarm robotics with heterogeneous units**: Mixed teams of robots where some are capable of multiple behaviors and others are single-purpose; learning when to rely on which robot.
4. **Emergency response teams**: Coordinating with human partners (police, firefighters) who may have different training levels or specializations.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Adaptive robot training platform**: SaaS for companies deploying collaborative robots—trains your robot to work with your specific workforce composition (mixed skill levels) without manual tuning.
2. **AI teammate simulation for training**: Enterprise training tool that simulates realistic teammates with varying adaptability, helping human teams practice ad-hoc collaboration scenarios (military, emergency response).
3. **Multi-convention middleware**: Invisible coordination layer for mixed human-AI teams—automatically detects team members' capabilities and negotiates shared strategies on the fly.
4. **Game AI that adapts to player diversity**: Game NPCs that learn whether the player is rigid or flexible in strategy, then coordinate accordingly—improves co-op game experience without explicit player profiling.
5. **Autonomous fleet optimizer**: Software for warehouses or delivery services managing both autonomous and human-operated vehicles; learns capability profiles and auto-assigns tasks accordingly.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Population design dependency**: The method requires designing a good distribution of partner capabilities; if the training population doesn't reflect true partner diversity, transfer will fail.
2. **Scalability to many conventions**: As the number of possible conventions grows, the state space explodes; unclear how the method scales beyond small convention sets.
3. **Communication assumption**: The approach relies on agents being able to observe or infer partner behavior; in partially observable settings, capability probing becomes much harder.
4. **Training cost**: Requires training and maintaining a large population of partners; computationally expensive compared to single-policy baselines.
5. **Limited to cooperative settings**: Assumes all partners are cooperative; breaks down if partners are adversarial or have misaligned incentives.
6. **Generalization beyond training population**: No theoretical guarantees that probing behavior trained on one population will transfer to entirely new convention sets or partner types.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance degrade as the number of possible conventions scales? Is there a sample-complexity bound?
2. Can agents generalize to convention sets not seen during training, or does the method require retraining for new task domains?
3. How does partial observability (agents can't directly see all partner actions) affect the ability to probe capabilities?
4. Can this be extended to competitive or mixed-motive settings where partners have conflicting goals?
5. What is the minimal partner population diversity needed to achieve robust performance? Can you characterize this theoretically?
6. How does performance depend on the time horizon—can agents probe effectively in short-horizon tasks?

## Key Takeaway
Training agents against diverse partners with varying flexibility teaches them to probe capabilities and coordinate robustly across unknown teammates.
