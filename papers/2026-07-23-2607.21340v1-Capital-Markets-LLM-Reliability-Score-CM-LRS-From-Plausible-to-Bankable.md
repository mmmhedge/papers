# Capital Markets LLM Reliability Score (CM-LRS): From Plausible to Bankable

**Authors:** Prerit Ahuja
**Published:** 2026-07-23 | **arXiv:** [2607.21340v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21340v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces CM-LRS (Capital Markets LLM Reliability Score), a framework for evaluating whether language model outputs are "bankable"—defensible to regulators and counterparties in actual financial workflows. Rather than measuring generic fluency or question-answering accuracy, CM-LRS scores models across seven dimensions (factual accuracy, traceability, numerical consistency, completeness, discipline, usefulness, auditability) on real capital markets tasks like M&A analysis and debt issuance document generation. The work shows that frontier closed-source models cluster tightly (within 0.22 points), but all struggle with retrieval and synthesis tasks, revealing where LLMs remain unreliable for high-stakes finance.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two parallel traditions: (1) LLM evaluation frameworks that moved beyond generic benchmarks to domain-specific reasoning (FinanceBench, FinQA, ConvFinQA evaluate financial question-answering); and (2) regulatory frameworks in capital markets where outputs must be auditable and defensible. The key insight is that existing finance benchmarks evaluate at the question-answer layer, but practitioners defend full workflow outputs—transaction documents, synthesis summaries, precedent analyses. CM-LRS anchors its rubric to signals that human reviewers in regulated settings actually use: traceability to source documents, consistency across numerical calculations, completeness of required terms. The framework treats capital markets workflows as a distinct evaluation domain where "plausibility" (fluent text) differs fundamentally from "bankability" (defensible decisions).

## Core Contributions
1. **CM-LRS framework**: A seven-dimensional scoring rubric (0–5 scale per dimension) specifically designed for capital markets workflow outputs, not isolated QA pairs. Dimensions are anchored to regulatory and practitioner signals rather than generic accuracy metrics.

2. **Workflow-layer evaluation**: Demonstrates CM-LRS on five authentic workflows (DCM transaction-terms extraction, precedent retrieval, issuer profiling, M&A comparables reasoning, ECM extraction) using real SEC EDGAR filings and UK takeover releases, shifting evaluation from benchmark questions to actual practitioner deliverables.

3. **Empirical gaps map**: Identifies that closed-source frontier models (Sonnet, Opus, GPT-5.5) cluster tightly but all gap significantly on retrieval (2.23 points) and synthesis (2.15 points) vs. extraction (0.84 points)—revealing where the reliability problem concentrates.

4. **Multi-judge evaluation protocol**: Uses four independent LLM judges across three model families to score outputs, reporting inter-judge agreement metrics and exposing Decision Usefulness as the dimension with widest cross-model dispersion (4.0 points on one task).

## Connections to Other Work
CM-LRS extends the finance-QA benchmarking lineage ([[FinanceBench]], [[FinQA]], [[ConvFinQA]]) by moving evaluation upstream to workflow outputs. It echoes broader LLM reliability concerns in [[alignment]] and [[risk-management]] literature but grounds them in a specific high-stakes domain where regulatory defensibility is non-negotiable. The multi-judge consensus approach parallels [[RLHF]] and reward modeling in that it uses human (or LLM-proxy-for-human) signals to define quality, but applies them post-hoc to evaluate outputs rather than during training. The focus on traceability and auditability connects to emerging work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] and model transparency, though from a practitioner rather than mechanistic angle.

## Practical Applications
1. **Pre-release validation in DCM/ECM**: Underwriters can use CM-LRS to gate LLM-drafted term sheets, offering letters, or pricing decks before they reach clients—catching factual gaps, numerical errors, or missing regulatory boilerplate before reputation risk.

2. **Precedent research automation**: Legal and investment banking teams deploy LLM-powered precedent systems (M&A comps, similar transactions) and use CM-LRS to flag synthesis hallucinations or incomplete source traceability before lawyers and bankers review.

3. **Regulatory compliance reporting**: Corporate issuers or M&A advisors use LLM assistants for prospectus drafting or disclosure; CM-LRS evaluates whether outputs would survive SEC review or due diligence.

4. **Internal audit and model governance**: Compliance/risk teams monitor LLM tool deployments in trading, lending, or capital allocation workflows, using CM-LRS to detect when models slip below auditability or usefulness thresholds.

5. **Client-facing advisory**: Elite advisory firms offer "LLM-assisted research with CM-LRS certification" as a differentiated product—guaranteeing outputs meet institutional standards for traceability and defensibility.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **CM-LRS-as-a-Service platform**: Offer a hosted evaluation engine that banks, advisors, and corporates can plug LLM outputs into, scoring and reporting against CM-LRS rubrics. Revenue: per-workflow evaluation fees or enterprise subscriptions. Competitors: none yet; first-mover advantage in capital markets compliance automation.

2. **"Bankable AI" consulting**: Audit existing LLM deployments in finance, identify gaps (retrieval, synthesis), recommend fine-tuning or retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improvements, and validate improvements against CM-LRS. Revenue: managed services.

3. **Specialized finance LLM provider**: Train or fine-tune an LLM to maximize CM-LRS scores on capital markets workflows (e.g., via supervised fine-tuning on high-scoring human-reviewed transactions). Sell as a managed API or self-hosted model.

4. **Workflow template marketplace**: Package CM-LRS + templates for common workflows (M&A comparables, debt issuance, precedent retrieval) so mid-market banks and corporates can deploy LLM tools with built-in reliability guardrails. Revenue: SaaS subscription.

5. **Regulatory-grade document generation**: Build LLM + CM-LRS backbone for automated prospectus, offering memorandum, or transaction document drafting, with audit trails showing every source and every calculation. Target: smaller investment banks and corporate finance teams that can't afford elite advisory.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Rubric subjectivity**: While anchored to "signals reviewers use," the seven dimensions and 0–5 scoring remain subjective. Different reviewers or regulators may weight traceability vs. decision usefulness differently; CM-LRS does not resolve this tension, only makes it visible.

2. **Limited workflow coverage**: Five workflows tested; capital markets spans hundreds (securitization, structured products, derivatives, lending syndication, equity research, etc.). Generalizability to untested domains is unclear.

3. **Synthetic data and fictional supplements**: Part of the evaluation uses "fictional synthetic supplements," which may not reflect real adversarial or edge-case scenarios. Real SEC filings are messy; robustness on badly formatted or ambiguous documents is untested.

4. **LLM-as-judge bias**: Using four LLMs as judges introduces model-family bias and circularity—the judges themselves are products of training that may align with the models being evaluated. Inter-judge agreement (r = 0.52 on best dimension) is moderate, not high.

5. **No cost-benefit analysis**: The paper does not quantify the business value of improving CM-LRS scores (e.g., speed gain, error reduction cost, risk avoidance) vs. the cost of running evaluations. "Bankable" is aspirational without ROI grounding.

6. **Closed-source model dependence**: Evaluates four specific model versions. Model releases and fine-tuning strategies evolve rapidly; CM-LRS may become stale without continual benchmarking and dimension refinement.

## Open Questions
1. **How does CM-LRS correlate with actual downstream outcomes?** E.g., do high CM-LRS scores on M&A comps correlate with deal success, valuation accuracy, or reduced due diligence rework? Paper shows inter-judge agreement but not predictive validity.

2. **Can CM-LRS dimensions be automated further?** Current scoring relies on LLM judges; could numeric consistency or traceability be checked via deterministic rules, and what is the trade-off in precision vs. interpretability?

3. **How do specialized finance LLMs (or fine-tuned open-weights models) perform on CM-LRS?** Paper focuses on frontier closed-source and one open-weights baseline. Can targeted fine-tuning close the retrieval/synthesis gap?
