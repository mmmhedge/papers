# Fine-Tuning LLMs for Translation: General Forgetting Mitigation Does Not Preserve MT-Specific Instruction Following

**Authors:** Niklas Scholz, David Thulke, Abdallah Nasir, Will Allred, Evgeny Matusov et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28395v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28395v1)

## Summary
When fine-tuning large language models on translation data, they forget general knowledge — a problem called catastrophic forgetting. This paper shows that common forgetting-mitigation methods (like Elastic Weight Consolidation) preserve general abilities well, but fail to preserve machine translation-specific instruction-following: the ability to modify translations by request (e.g., making them more formal or controlling grammatical gender). The key finding is that general-purpose forgetting solutions don't solve the translation-specific problem.

## Theoretical Background
Fine-tuning adapts pre-trained models to new tasks by updating their weights on task-specific data. Catastrophic forgetting — where a model loses capability on previously-learned tasks after fine-tuning — is a long-standing challenge in continual learning. Forgetting mitigation typically uses three strategies: (1) auxiliary data methods that mix in original training examples, (2) output-anchored methods that constrain predictions to stay close to the base model's outputs, and (3) parameter-anchored methods like Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC), which penalize changes to important weights. Machine translation instruction-following — the ability to follow user directives like "make this more formal" — is a distinct capability from base translation quality, and treating it as equivalent to general benchmark performance is the paper's central critique.

## Core Contributions
1. **Problem redefinition**: Shows that preserving general capabilities (via EWC or similar) does not guarantee preservation of MT-specific instruction-following; the two are empirically decoupled.
2. **Empirical scope**: Evaluates forgetting mitigation specifically for translation fine-tuning (not just general QA or summarization) across two language pairs and two model sizes (1B and 8B parameters).
3. **Diagnostic insight**: Demonstrates that only data mixing with control-task examples preserves formality and grammatical gender control — but this preservation does not generalize to unseen prompts for the same control task (overfitting to seen examples).
4. **Practical gap**: Quantifies the cost of forgetting mitigation: EWC reduces general benchmark drop from 11.0 to 1.7 points on the 8B Spanish model, yet MT-IF scores remain degraded compared to standard fine-tuning.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[catastrophic forgetting]] in continual learning, [[instruction fine-tuning]] of large language models (as in [[RLHF]]), and machine translation quality metrics. It relates to broader alignment concerns: if instruction-following is a proxy for model controllability, then fine-tuning for one task while preserving instruction-following is partly an [[alignment]] problem. The paper echoes findings from [[LORA]] and other parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods that aim to reduce forgetting, but extends the critique to domain-specific instruction semantics rather than just benchmark retention.

## Practical Applications
1. **Production MT systems**: Organizations deploying specialized translation systems (e.g., legal or medical domains) need both good translation quality AND the ability to respect user instructions (formality levels, terminology constraints). This paper shows current methods fail at the latter.
2. **Multilingual assistants**: Chat models tuned for translation across multiple language pairs must preserve instruction-following (e.g., "translate but use informal register") after domain adaptation.
3. **Customer-facing translation APIs**: Services offering style or tone control in translation (Duolingo, professional translation platforms) need fine-tuning methods that preserve these controls without forgetting general language capability.
4. **Localization workflows**: Game and software localization often requires translations that follow style guides (voice, formality, length constraints) — fine-tuning pipelines must handle this.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited scale**: Tested only on Llama 3.1/3.2 models; results may not generalize to other architectures (GPT, Claude, Mistral) or significantly larger models (70B+).
2. **Data mixing overfitting**: Data mixing with control-task examples preserves seen instructions but fails to generalize to unseen prompts — suggesting the solution is brittle and domain-specific, not principled.
3. **MT-IF definition**: The paper evaluates only formality, grammatical gender, and length control. Other instruction types (terminology preservation, stylistic constraints, pragmatic coherence) are not tested.
4. **Bidirectional pairs only**: Experiments use Arabic-English and Spanish-English; low-resource and morphologically complex language pairs are not covered.
5. **No theoretical grounding**: The paper is empirical; it lacks a formal framework explaining *why* parameter-anchored methods fail for instruction-following. This makes it hard to design better solutions.
6. **Instruction prompt format sensitivity**: Results may depend heavily on how instructions are phrased in prompts; robustness to paraphrase is not tested.

## Open Questions
1. **Generalization of data mixing**: Can data mixing be made to generalize to unseen control prompts via meta-learning or prompt-level diversity during training?
2. **Instruction-aware regularization**: Can we design a new forgetting mitigation method that weights parameters or losses by their importance to instruction-following, not just general performance?
3. **Mechanistic understanding**: Which transformer layers or attention heads are responsible for instruction-following? Can we selectively protect them during fine-tuning?
4. **Cross-task transfer**: Do mitigation strategies that work for formality control also work for other instruction types (terminology, tone, length)? Is there a unified approach?
5. **Scaling**: Do the forgetting vs. instruction-following trade-offs change with model size? Is there a scale at which the problem dissolves?
6. **Instruction semantics in base models**: Are instruction-following capabilities already present in base Llama models, or do they emerge only after instruction fine-tuning? If the former, how are they encoded?

## Key Takeaway
Forgetting mitigation preserves general knowledge during translation fine-tuning but fails to preserve instruction-following abilities like formality control.
