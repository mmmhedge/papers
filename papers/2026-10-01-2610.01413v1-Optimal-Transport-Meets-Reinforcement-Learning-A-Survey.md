# Optimal Transport Meets Reinforcement Learning: A Survey

**Authors:** Yujie Zhu, Charles A. Hepburn, Matthew Thorpe, Giovanni Montana
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.01413v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.01413v1)

## Summary
This survey examines how optimal transport — a mathematical framework for measuring the cost of transforming one probability distribution into another — can be integrated into reinforcement learning algorithms. The key insight is that optimal transport handles cases where probability distributions overlap weakly (a common problem in imitation learning and offline RL) better than standard divergence measures, by explicitly modeling the geometric cost of moving probability mass between distributions.

## Theoretical Background
Reinforcement learning trains agents to maximize cumulative rewards by learning policies (decision rules) that interact with environments. A central challenge is comparing different probability distributions: the state visitation distributions induced by a learned policy versus an expert's policy, or the action distributions from a learned model versus real data. Traditionally, RL uses divergences like KL divergence or Wasserstein distance to compare these distributions, but these can become ineffective when the distributions have weak overlap — a frequent problem in imitation learning (learning from demonstrations), offline RL (learning from fixed datasets without environment interaction), and distribution shift (when test environments differ from training). Optimal transport (OT) offers an alternative perspective: instead of just measuring how different two distributions are, it models the minimal cost of physically "moving" probability mass from one distribution to another, where the cost structure encodes task-relevant geometry. This ground cost can encode properties like state similarity or action consequences, making OT particularly expressive for sequential decision problems.

## Core Contributions
1. **Systematic taxonomy** of OT applications in RL: categorizes existing methods by which distributions are compared (state visitation, action, transition), which OT formulations are used (entropic regularization, Sinkhorn iterations, etc.), and how temporal structure is handled.
2. **Identification of design choices**: clarifies the role OT plays in different RL objectives, how to design meaningful ground costs, and when OT is preferable to simpler divergences.
3. **Problem synthesis**: highlights concrete open challenges — scalable trajectory-level transport (moving mass across full sequences, not just individual states), principled treatment of mass mismatch (when distributions have different total mass), and theoretical guarantees for OT-regularized RL.
4. **Practical guidance**: discusses computational bottlenecks and implementation trade-offs for practitioners.

## Practical Applications
1. **Imitation Learning from Demonstrations**: Using OT to match expert state visitation distributions helps learn policies that follow expert behavior even when expert coverage is sparse, improving sample efficiency in learning from human demonstrations.
2. **Offline RL in Robotics**: When training robot policies from fixed datasets (e.g., motion capture data), OT can align the learned policy's state distribution with the dataset's distribution, preventing distribution shift that causes poor performance at test time.
3. **Safe RL Deployment**: In safety-critical domains (autonomous driving, medical control), OT can constrain a learned policy to stay close to a safe baseline policy's distribution, ensuring smooth deployment under environment shift.
4. **Domain Adaptation in RL**: When transferring a policy across similar but different environments (e.g., simulation to reality), OT can align the source and target transition distributions using task geometry, improving adaptation.
5. **Multi-task RL**: OT can compare policies across tasks by using task-specific ground costs, enabling better transfer and multi-task learning.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational Complexity**: Solving the full OT problem scales poorly with dimension and batch size. While entropic regularization (Sinkhorn algorithm) accelerates computation, it remains more expensive than simple KL divergence, limiting applicability to large-scale RL problems.
2. **Ground Cost Design**: The choice of ground cost is crucial but often ad-hoc. The paper acknowledges this is not well-principled; poor cost choice can make OT ineffective or computationally intractable.
3. **Trajectory-Level OT**: Most existing work compares single states or actions. Extending OT to full trajectories (matching state-action sequences with temporal structure) remains open and computationally hard.
4. **Mass Mismatch**: When the policy and expert have different effective "support" (e.g., expert visits 80% of relevant states, learned policy only 60%), standard OT formulations struggle. Principled solutions are missing.
5. **Theoretical Guarantees**: The survey notes a lack of convergence guarantees and sample complexity bounds for OT-regularized RL algorithms, making it hard to predict when OT will outperform simpler methods.
6. **Non-stationary Environments**: OT assumes static distributions, but in RL with function approximation or online learning, distributions shift during training, potentially breaking OT-based guarantees.

## Open Questions
1. **Scalable Trajectory Transport**: How can we efficiently compute OT over full trajectories (sequences of state-action pairs) rather than marginals, while respecting causal temporal structure?
2. **Mass Mismatch Solutions**: What are principled, theoretically grounded ways to handle cases where two distributions have different total mass or support?
3. **Ground Cost Learning**: Can we learn the ground cost end-to-end from data rather than specifying it manually? What would the optimization landscape look like?
4. **OT for Model-Based RL**: How should OT be used to align learned transition models with real environments, and what are the convergence implications?
5. **Computational Complexity in Practice**: For which problem scales and dimensions does OT-based RL outweigh the computational overhead compared to KL-based methods?
6. **Theoretical Analysis**: What are finite-sample convergence rates and regret bounds for OT-regularized RL, and how do they compare to simpler alternatives?
7. **Combination with Modern RL**: How do OT methods integrate with recent advances like diffusion-based RL or transformer-based policies?

## Key Takeaway
Optimal transport offers a geometrically-principled way to match probability distributions in RL, excelling when distributions overlap weakly—but scaling and theoretical guarantees remain open.
