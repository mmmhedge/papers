# GAMBIT: Learning to Plan Continuous Multi-Robot Trajectories

**Authors:** Rishabh Jain, Akmaral Moldagalieva, Lorenzo Magnino, Michael Amir, Keisuke Okumura et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-05 | **arXiv:** [2610.06290v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.06290v1)

## Summary
GAMBIT learns how to coordinate the trajectories of multiple robots in continuous motion spaces by combining imitation learning with reinforcement learning. The key insight is that robots sometimes need to sacrifice local efficiency for team-level coordination—like sacrificing a pawn in chess for strategic advantage. The system scales to over 1,000 robots while maintaining collision-free execution and planning speeds of hundreds of milliseconds.

## Theoretical Background
Multi-robot coordination builds on classical motion planning (finding collision-free paths) and reinforcement learning (RL — training agents to maximize rewards through trial-and-error). The paper uses motion primitives — pre-computed short trajectory segments that robots can chain together — which reduces the planning complexity compared to computing trajectories from scratch. The work operates on double-integrator dynamics (standard continuous control where force/acceleration is the control input), a common model in robotics. Prior work typically relies on manually designed heuristics or centralized planners that compute globally optimal solutions but don't scale; GAMBIT instead learns decentralized reactive policies that coordinate implicitly through learned behavior.

## Core Contributions
1. **Coordinated motion-primitive selection via learning**: Uses imitation learning to bootstrap policies that mimic expert (centralized planner) behavior, then fine-tunes with RL to discover self-sacrificial coordinated strategies that explicit heuristics miss.
2. **Safeguarded rollout mechanism**: Introduces a collision-avoidance guarantee by maintaining backup trajectories, ensuring safe execution even if the learned policy fails.
3. **Demonstrated scalability**: Validates coordination on 1,000+ robots with <few-hundred-millisecond latency—orders of magnitude beyond typical decentralized baselines—while maintaining continuous dynamics (not grid-based).

## Connections to Other Work
GAMBIT bridges [[decentralized multi-agent planning]] and [[imitation learning + reinforcement learning]] workflows. It relates to reactive collision avoidance methods (e.g., reciprocal collision avoidance) but replaces hand-crafted rules with learned ones. The "sacrifice for team benefit" framing echoes multi-agent game theory and social-welfare maximization problems. The safeguarded rollout idea parallels fail-safe RL approaches in safety-critical systems.

## Technical Limitations
- **Limited to double-integrator dynamics**: Real robots have acceleration limits, friction, and wheel constraints that aren't modeled; transfer to hardware may require domain adaptation.
- **Motion-primitive dependence**: Performance is bottlenecked by the quality and coverage of primitive library; no guarantee of finding good solutions if primitives don't cover the required maneuvers.
- **Scalability claims are simulation-only**: Testing on 1,000 robots is in simulation; real hardware (communication delays, heterogeneity, sensor noise) could degrade coordination.
- **Requires expert demonstrations**: Imitation learning requires access to a good centralized planner for bootstrapping; in novel environments, collecting such data is expensive.
- **Collision guarantees via rollbacks**: The safeguarded rollout mechanism adds latency and computational overhead; unclear how it performs under extreme congestion or adversarial scenarios.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance degrade with partial observability (robots can't see all others, only nearby teammates)?
2. Can the learned policies transfer across environments without retraining, or does each deployment require fine-tuning?
3. What is the failure mode when all rollback trajectories lead to collisions (worst-case guarantees)?
4. How sensitive is the method to the motion-primitive library? Is there an automated way to generate or adapt primitives on-the-fly?
5. Does the approach handle dynamic obstacles (humans, moving objects) or only static collision avoidance?
6. Can the framework be extended to non-prehensile manipulation or heterogeneous robot types?

## Key Takeaway
Learning-based coordination scales multi-robot planning to thousands of agents while guaranteeing collision safety through learned policies plus backup trajectories.
