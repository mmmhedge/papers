# Whose doctor does the AI recommend? An algorithm audit of reputation and demographic signals in large language model-assisted physician choice

**Authors:** Syeda Anshrah Gillani, Mirza Samad Ahmed Baig
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14399v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14399v1)

## Summary
This paper audits whether large language models recommend physicians fairly when patients ask for referrals. Researchers tested seven LLMs with 40,000+ randomized physician profiles and found that while reputation signals (ratings, fees) dominate recommendations, demographic biases exist but are hidden — models rarely mention gender or ethnicity in their explanations, making these invisible to standard transparency checks.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on **algorithm audits** — experimental methods that randomize attributes to measure causal effects of system inputs on outputs. This approach originated in hiring discrimination studies (correspondence audits, where researchers send identical resumes with varied demographic signals to employers). The authors apply this to LLMs acting as **AI infomediaries**: algorithmic intermediaries that shape visibility and choice by deciding which options to recommend. The work sits at the intersection of **algorithmic fairness** (does the system treat demographic groups equally?) and **model interpretability** (can we trust what models say about their own decisions?).

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical measurement of hidden demographic bias**: While female and minority-signaled physicians gain a small statistical advantage (2.5–2.9 percentage points, worth $7–$14 per visit), this bias is *invisible* in models' explanations — mentioned in <0.03% of stated reasons.
2. **Demonstration that transparency fails**: Standard requirements to explain recommendations would not detect these biases because models do not mention demographics even when those factors causally influence choices.
3. **Repeatable audit design**: A frozen, standardized stimulus set that allows any future model to be tested against identical conditions — enabling recurring behavioral audits rather than one-off evaluations.
4. **Quantified reputation dominance**: Ratings and fees are the primary decision drivers (±30 pp for ratings; ±20 pp for fees), establishing a baseline for what "fair" recommendations should be calibrated against.

## Connections to Other Work
This extends classical **correspondence-audit methodology** from labor economics into AI governance. It relates to broader work on [[Alignment|model alignment]] — whether AI systems do what humans actually want (not just what they say) — and challenges assumptions underlying [[Mechanistic-Interpretability|mechanistic interpretability]] efforts that assume model explanations are honest. The finding that models can harbor systematic biases while claiming neutrality echoes concerns in [[RLHF]] and other training paradigms where proxy metrics diverge from true objectives. The paper also sits alongside work on **AI impact measurement** and regulatory approaches like EU AI Act auditing requirements.

## Practical Applications
- **Healthcare regulators**: Audit LLM-based physician recommendation systems before deployment, using the paper's methodology to detect hidden biases.
- **Patient-facing health platforms** (e.g., Zocdoc, Healthgrades): Test whether AI-powered recommendation features introduce undetected discrimination.
- **Hospital networks**: Monitor whether patient-facing chatbots steer patients toward certain providers in ways that violate antitrust or fairness rules.
- **Insurance companies**: Check whether claims advisors and provider recommendations show demographic steering.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Audit-as-a-Service**: A compliance platform that runs standardized behavioral audits on LLMs before and after deployment, generating repeatable reports for regulators and internal governance teams.
2. **Healthcare AI Transparency Layer**: A browser extension or middleware that flags hidden demographic signals in AI physician recommendations and explains the monetary value of each bias.
3. **Recurring Model Monitoring Dashboard**: A SaaS tool that runs the same frozen audit periodically on new model versions to detect drift in biases over time.
4. **Algorithmic Fairness Certification**: A third-party certification scheme (like SOC 2 for AI) that uses behavioral audits rather than self-report; startups could offer pre-audit consulting to help healthcare AI companies pass.
5. **Bias Detection for Healthcare IT Vendors**: Sell the audit methodology + software to EHR and health platform vendors to certify their recommendation engines don't harbor hidden demographic effects.

## Technical Limitations
- **Name-based signals only**: Gender and ethnicity are inferred from names; real-world demographic signals (photos, language cues, neighborhood proxies) may produce different effects.
- **Synthetic profiles**: Fictional physician cards lack rich contextual information (publications, hospital affiliation, patient reviews). Real scenarios may reveal different patterns.
- **Static snapshot**: The audit captures behavior at one moment; model updates and changes to underlying training data are not tracked.
- **Limited demographic coverage**: Only three ethnic groups and gender binary tested; intersectionality and other social categories not explored.
- **Causality within constraints**: The paper identifies which *attributes* move recommendations, but not *why* — the mechanisms (learned associations, training data patterns, embedding spaces) remain opaque.
- **Generalization**: Results are specific to family medicine and physician choice; whether patterns hold for other specialties or recommendation domains is unknown.

## Open Questions
- Do the hidden biases persist when demographics are signaled through richer channels (profile photos, biographical text) rather than names alone?
- What causes the female and minority-preference tilt? Is it training data patterns, architectural features, or RLHF misalignment?
- How do users interpret and act on these biased recommendations? Does a 2.5 pp algorithmic tilt translate to real-world patient flows?
- Can we modify LLMs to make demographic effects *visible* and controllable, rather than hidden?
- Does the audit methodology generalize to other high-stakes recommendation domains (loans, jobs, college admissions)?
- What remedies work? Fine-tuning, prompt engineering, output filtering, or human-in-the-loop review?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs secretly favor female and minority physicians in referral recommendations, but hide this bias from users — transparency alone cannot detect algorithmic discrimination.
