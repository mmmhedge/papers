# Correct Answers, Invalid Traces: What Verifiable Grade-School Math Reveals About Chain-of-Thought Traces

**Authors:** Ratish Puduppully, Pranabendu Misra, Paarth Iyer, Durgesh Kalwar, Vardhan Palod et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38107v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38107v1)

## Summary

This paper investigates whether chain-of-thought traces—the step-by-step reasoning that language models generate—actually reflect how models solve problems or are just post-hoc rationalizations. Using a specially designed math benchmark (iGSM) where correct solutions can be mechanically verified, the authors discover that models frequently produce correct answers with logically invalid reasoning traces, even when those traces pass surface-level checks. This matters for AI safety: if we can't trust model reasoning to be genuine, we can't rely on interpretability methods for debugging or auditing AI systems.

## Theoretical Background

Chain-of-thought prompting (asking models to "think step by step") has become a standard technique for improving reasoning in large language models. Prior work assumes these traces reveal the model's internal reasoning process, supporting claims about emergent planning and logical thinking. However, mechanistic interpretability—studying how neural networks actually compute—has raised doubts about whether natural language outputs faithfully represent computation. The iGSM benchmark builds on this by making reasoning traces verifiable: each math problem has explicit dependencies (which quantities must be computed first) and correct operations, so traces can be checked programmatically rather than just by human reading.

## Core Contributions

1. **Verifiable trace framework**: iGSM enables step-by-step programmatic validation of mathematical reasoning, unlike natural language tasks where "correctness" is ambiguous.

2. **In-distribution vs. out-of-distribution decoupling**: Models trained on valid traces show near-perfect answer correctness, but on harder problems, 31.6% of correct answers have invalid traces—revealing a gap between answer quality and reasoning validity.

3. **Semantic vs. syntactic failures**: Many invalid traces pass arithmetic and syntax checks but violate logical dependencies (using a quantity before it's defined), suggesting models learn shallow patterns rather than genuine reasoning.

4. **Robustness to trace corruption**: Shuffling tokens in 10% of training traces or swapping entire traces completely preserves in-distribution performance, indicating models do not rely on trace content for learning.

## Connections to Other Work

This work directly challenges optimistic interpretations of [[Chain-of-Thought Prompting]] and relates to broader concerns in [[alignment]] about whether model outputs can be trusted for interpretability. It connects to debates around [[mechanistic-interpretability]]—whether we can read off genuine reasoning from activations or text outputs. The findings also echo work on [[Spurious Correlations]] in deep learning, where models exploit surface patterns rather than learning intended concepts. Related to [[RLHF]] and supervision-based training: if models don't actually use the reasoning traces we supervise them with, what are we actually training?

## Practical Applications

1. **AI auditing and oversight**: Organizations trying to verify AI reasoning for high-stakes domains (medicine, law, finance) cannot safely rely on trace inspection alone.

2. **Model debugging**: Engineers attempting to fix incorrect answers by examining model traces may be looking at post-hoc fabrications, not the actual locus of error.

3. **Safety monitoring**: Systems that flag "suspicious reasoning" in model outputs would miss cases where answers are correct but traces are invalid or reversed.

4. **Curriculum learning design**: Better understanding of what trace properties actually matter for learning could improve training efficiency.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **Trace verification middleware**: A tool that integrates with LLM APIs to programmatically validate reasoning traces against formal schemas (math, logic, code) and flag high-risk "correct-but-invalid-trace" outputs.

2. **Trustworthy AI audit platform**: SaaS product for enterprises to stress-test LLM deployments with out-of-distribution examples, measuring the answer-validity gap and generating audit reports.

3. **Synthetic benchmark generation service**: Automated tooling to create domain-specific verifiable benchmarks (like iGSM) for law, medical diagnosis, financial modeling—enabling better evaluation of reasoning reliability.

4. **Chain-of-thought distillation**: Train smaller, more interpretable models by filtering training data to include only examples where traces are genuinely valid, improving efficiency and debuggability.

5. **AI transparency certification**: Offer certification for LLM applications (e.g., "reasoning-validated for healthcare") using systematic trace verification, differentiating trustworthy deployments.

## Technical Limitations

- **Scope to formal domains**: The findings are strongest for math; it's unclear whether invalid traces are as prevalent in language understanding, creative writing, or dialogue tasks where ground truth is ambiguous.

- **Model scale**: Experiments appear limited to smaller models; it's unknown whether larger models or differently-trained models (e.g., via RLHF with process reward models) would show the same decoupling.

- **Trace generation process**: The paper doesn't deeply explore *why* models generate invalid traces—is it a sampling artifact, a training signal issue, or fundamental to how transformers work?

- **Minimal trace assumption**: The definition of "minimal" traces (fewest steps, no redundancy) may not reflect optimal reasoning for all learners; some redundancy could aid generalization.

- **Out-of-distribution brittleness**: The hardest instances used to detect the decoupling are synthetic and may not reflect real-world distribution shifts.

## Open Questions

1. **Do process reward models help?** Can training models to score reasoning steps (not just answers) reduce the invalid-trace problem, or do they perpetuate the same spurious correlations?

2. **What causes the decoupling?** Is it a sampling temperature artifact, or does the model genuinely not use trace content? Mechanistic analysis (activation probing, intervention) could clarify.

3. **Does scale matter?** Do larger models or those trained with more compute show better trace-answer alignment?

4. **Can traces be fixed post-hoc?** If a model has the right answer but wrong trace, can a separate "trace validator" module fix the reasoning without retraining?

5. **Cross-domain transfer**: Can a model trained to produce valid traces on math transfer to other domains, or is trace validity domain-specific?

## Key Takeaway

Models often get the right answer for the wrong reasons—even when reasoning traces look plausible—undermining trust in AI interpretability for safety.
