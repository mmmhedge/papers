# Generation-Powered Inference for Distribution-Valued Outcomes

**Authors:** Yijiao Zhang, Hongzhe Li
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14542v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14542v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes generation-powered inference (GPI), a statistical framework that uses imperfect generative models to improve inference on distribution-valued outcomes — like predicting how cells respond to genetic changes. The key insight is that even flawed generative models contain useful information; GPI shows how to extract and leverage that information rigorously, with statistical guarantees, without assuming the generative model is correct.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on [[prediction-powered inference]] (PPI), which combines labeled data with unlabeled predictions from auxiliary models to improve estimation. Here the authors extend this to cases where the outcome of interest is itself a distribution (not a point estimate). They use Wasserstein barycenters — a concept from optimal transport theory that defines a meaningful "average" of distributions in a way that preserves geometric structure. The core technical trick is a "function-valued bridge representation" that converts the nonlinear problem of averaging distributions in Wasserstein space into a linear estimation problem in a Hilbert space (a type of infinite-dimensional vector space with nice mathematical properties). This makes the problem tractable and allows them to apply classical statistical machinery like asymptotic normality and confidence bands.

## Core Contributions
1. **GPI framework**: A general methodology for leveraging auxiliary generative models in distribution-valued inference, with formal statistical guarantees (consistency, asymptotic normality, confidence bands).
2. **Function-valued bridge representation**: A mathematical transformation that linearizes inference on Wasserstein barycenters, enabling optimal information borrowing from generative models.
3. **Robustness theory**: Proofs that GPI estimators remain valid and efficient even when the generative model is misspecified — they degrade gracefully rather than catastrophically.
4. **Practical inference tools**: Methods for constructing simultaneous confidence bands and valid inference on linear functionals of distributions and Wasserstein distances.
5. **Biological validation**: Application to Perturb-seq data showing that synthetic perturbation responses from foundation models improve real-world genomic inference.

## Connections to Other Work
This is a direct extension of [[prediction-powered inference]], moving from scalar outcomes to distributional ones. It relates to broader work on leveraging imperfect auxiliary models (a growing theme as generative models become ubiquitous). The use of optimal transport and Wasserstein geometry connects to recent work on foundation models in biology that output distributional predictions. The statistical machinery (asymptotic theory, confidence bands) draws from functional data analysis and nonparametric statistics. It sits at an intersection of modern machine learning (generative models) and classical statistics (rigorous inference under model misspecification).

## Practical Applications
- **Single-cell genomics**: Inferring consensus gene expression distributions after genetic perturbations, using synthetic cell responses from pretrained models to augment sparse experimental data.
- **Drug response prediction**: Combining limited measured drug sensitivity distributions with large-scale model-generated predictions to infer patient-level treatment outcomes.
- **Climate modeling**: Using imperfect generative weather models to improve inference on distributional forecasts (e.g., precipitation uncertainty).
- **Materials science**: Leveraging neural network simulations of material properties (which output distributions over quantum states) to refine experimental inference with limited samples.
- **Epidemiology**: Merging mechanistic disease models (which output distributions over outbreak trajectories) with sparse real-world data for better uncertainty quantification.

## Technical Limitations
- **Computational cost**: The method requires computing Wasserstein barycenters and function-valued representations, which can be expensive for high-dimensional distributions or large datasets.
- **Model misspecification bounds**: While the paper proves robustness, the practical efficiency gains degrade as generative model error grows; there is a threshold beyond which labeled data alone becomes preferable.
- **Assumption on auxiliary predictions**: The framework assumes the generative model outputs are well-calibrated distributions (not just point estimates); if outputs are over/underconfident, gains may vanish.
- **Scalability to very high dimensions**: Optimal transport computations become prohibitively slow in very high dimensions (curse of dimensionality); applications may be limited to moderate-dimensional distributional spaces.
- **Limited theory for model selection**: The paper does not provide guidance on when to use GPI vs. standard inference — practitioners must diagnose whether their generative model is "good enough."

## Open Questions
- How does GPI perform with multimodal or heavy-tailed generative model outputs? The current theory assumes reasonable regularity.
- Can the framework handle sequential or online settings where new data and new model predictions arrive over time?
- What is the optimal architecture for generative models used in GPI? Should they be trained to minimize Wasserstein distance to ground truth, or does standard loss work?
- How sensitive are confidence bands to the choice of metric on the space of distributions (e.g., Wasserstein vs. other divergences)?
- Can GPI be extended to hierarchical or functional response models (e.g., time-series distributions or spatial fields)?
- How does GPI interact with other forms of side information (e.g., prior knowledge, expert annotations) in a unified framework?

## Key Takeaway
Imperfect generative models can rigorously improve statistical inference on distributional outcomes if you leverage them via optimal transport theory and classical statistics.
