# Same Text, Different Numbers: The Divergence of LLM-Based Measures

**Authors:** Hamid Boustanifar, Sasan Mansouri
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31013v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31013v1)

## Summary
This paper reveals a critical problem: different LLMs produce dramatically different numerical scores when asked to measure the same textual properties (sentiment, uncertainty, risk) in corporate earnings calls, even though they're reading identical documents. The authors show that model choice matters more than the actual content being measured, which undermines the reliability of LLM-based variables widely used in financial research and practice.

## Theoretical Background
Large language models are neural networks trained on vast text corpora to predict and generate human language. Recent years have seen a surge in using these models to convert unstructured text (like corporate disclosures) into structured numerical measures for downstream analysis. In quantitative finance, researchers use text-based variables to predict stock returns, analyze firm risk, and study market behavior. Prior work has generally assumed that high-quality LLMs produce consistent measurements of linguistic properties, treating model outputs as reliable proxies for underlying textual attributes. This paper challenges that assumption by examining whether different LLM providers agree on the same measurements.

## Core Contributions
1. **Large-scale empirical validation**: The authors systematically compare thirteen different textual measures (sentiment, clarity, uncertainty, answer specificity, climate risk, political risk) across seven major LLMs from different providers using 500 companies' earnings transcripts.

2. **Quantified disagreement**: They show cross-model rank correlations average only 0.52 (where 1.0 would be perfect agreement), indicating substantial divergence. Only 34% of score variation is common across all providers.

3. **Model-specific bias is not noise**: They demonstrate that cross-model disagreement does not predict downstream analyst or market disagreement, suggesting the variation is a model artifact rather than legitimate ambiguity in the underlying text.

4. **Downstream inference instability**: Model choice significantly affects empirical results—coefficient signs, magnitudes, and statistical significance change materially across LLMs when used as predictors in financial models.

5. **Ensemble limitations**: Averaging scores across models improves ranking stability for some constructs but does not stabilize absolute score levels.

## Practical Applications
1. **Financial research validation**: Finance researchers using LLM-based text measures must now validate results across multiple models before publishing, rather than treating a single LLM output as ground truth.

2. **ESG and risk measurement**: Companies selling ESG scores, climate risk measures, or political risk indices derived from LLMs face credibility challenges; they must disclose model choice or ensemble methodology transparently.

3. **Regulatory compliance**: Auditors and compliance teams using LLM-generated variables for regulatory reporting (e.g., risk disclosures) need to establish which models are acceptable or require cross-validation.

4. **Analyst tools**: Financial platforms offering LLM-powered sentiment or uncertainty scores must manage user expectations about model variance and stress-test predictions under multiple model scenarios.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM-based financial variable validation suite**: A SaaS platform that automatically cross-validates LLM-generated textual measures across N providers, flags divergence, and ranks models by stability and predictive power for specific financial outcomes.

2. **Ensemble scoring API**: A middleware service that combines multiple LLMs into calibrated, ensemble-based textual metrics with confidence intervals and model-specificity warnings, marketed to asset managers and fintech firms.

3. **Financial text benchmark**: A curated dataset of corporate disclosures with ground-truth labels (from human annotation or market validation) to benchmark LLM performance on finance-specific text measures, sold as a research/evaluation tool.

4. **Model-agnostic financial NLP toolkit**: Open-source or commercial libraries that wrap textual measurement pipelines with automatic multi-model comparison, logging, and drift detection for use in quant workflows.

5. **LLM-based variable explainability tool**: A diagnostic service showing which specific words or passages drive disagreement between models on a given text, helping researchers understand whether divergence is semantic or an artifact of model training.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Model selection bias**: The seven LLMs tested may not represent the full space of possible models; newer or smaller open-source models might show different patterns.

2. **Prompt engineering not fully explored**: The paper doesn't deeply investigate how prompt phrasing, few-shot examples, or temperature settings affect cross-model consistency; small prompt changes could shift results.

3. **Causality claims are correlational**: The finding that disagreement doesn't predict analyst/market disagreement is suggestive but doesn't prove that disagreement is model-specific rather than legitimate ambiguity; other confounds could explain the null result.

4. **Financial domain specificity**: Results are restricted to earnings call transcripts and S&P 500 companies; generalization to other texts (news, social media, regulatory filings) or non-U.S. contexts is unclear.

5. **Construct validity**: The paper assumes the thirteen measures (sentiment, clarity, etc.) are well-defined; if the constructs themselves are ambiguous, even high agreement wouldn't validate meaningfulness.

6. **Ensemble averaging is unprincipled**: Averaging scores stabilizes rankings but doesn't address the core problem—averaging two inconsistent models doesn't guarantee the result is more truthful.

## Open Questions
1. **What drives disagreement?** A mechanistic analysis of which LLM architectural choices, training corpora, or fine-tuning procedures cause divergence on specific constructs would be valuable.

2. **Can we predict which model is "right"?** Do any of the LLM outputs correlate with ground-truth financial outcomes (e.g., actual risk realizations)? Which model is most predictive?

3. **Is disagreement systematic or random?** Do LLMs disagree in interpretable ways (e.g., one is consistently more conservative), or is it noise? Can disagreement be modeled or predicted?

4. **How do instructions and prompts affect stability?** A controlled experiment varying prompt design, examples, and temperature settings could isolate whether disagreement is fundamental or technique-dependent.

5. **Does fine-tuning on finance data help?** Would domain-specific fine-tuning of LLMs reduce cross-model disagreement on financial text measures?

6. **Can we build a ground-truth benchmark?** Labeling a subset of transcripts with human-validated truth values would let researchers test whether disagreement tracks genuine ambiguity or is pure model artifact.

## Key Takeaway
Different LLMs score the same text divergently, and model choice matters more than content—financial variables built on single LLMs are unreliable without multi-model validation.
