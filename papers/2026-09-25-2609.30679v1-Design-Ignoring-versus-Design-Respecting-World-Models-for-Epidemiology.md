# Design-Ignoring versus Design-Respecting World Models for Epidemiology

**Authors:** Xiangyu Yu, Weiyu Liu
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.30679v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30679v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a critical problem in epidemiological modeling: when a world model learns from data collected via a specific study design (randomized trial, survey sampling, measurement protocol), it may reconstruct the observed data perfectly while learning the wrong causal effect of an intervention. The authors formalize the distinction between "design-respecting" models that encode study constraints and "design-ignoring" models that ignore them, then show empirically that only design-respecting models produce stable intervention estimates across different data collection scenarios.

## Theoretical Background
Epidemiological inference relies on study designs (randomized trials, observational studies, etc.) that involve assignment mechanisms, sampling procedures, and measurement processes. Classical causal inference frameworks like those in [[RLHF]] or structural causal models distinguish between observational and interventional distributions, but epidemiologists often work with data where the collection process fundamentally shapes what can be learned. World models — neural network systems that learn latent representations of dynamics — have become popular for forecasting and counterfactual reasoning in complex domains. The key insight here is that a world model can achieve high fidelity on observed trajectories while remaining blind to how the data-generating process (the study design itself) constrains valid causal conclusions.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal framework**: Casts study design as explicit constraints on a world model's latent-world interface, action mechanism, observation likelihood, and readout function — making design a first-class modeling choice, not an afterthought.
2. **Design-respecting vs. design-ignoring distinction**: Operationalizes this gap concretely: design-respecting models encode assignment and observation processes; design-ignoring models fit records without relating them back to the intervention question.
3. **Empirical vulnerability test**: Demonstrates that across 500 resampling experiments with different sampling intensities in a cluster-randomized trial, design-ignoring models show large median shifts in estimated contrasts (~20–40%), while design-respecting models remain stable (~1–3%). This holds even when sampling varies across clusters.
4. **Evaluation methodology**: Argues that factual reconstruction accuracy is insufficient; instead, testing whether fitted contrasts preserve design-implied observation-process invariances is a sharper diagnostic.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of causal inference (e.g., Rubin's potential outcomes, instrumental variables), epidemiological study design, and machine learning for dynamics modeling. It echoes concerns in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] about ensuring learned models respect domain constraints, and relates to debates in [[alignment]] about whether a model that matches observed data truly "understands" the underlying causal structure. The notion that high predictive accuracy can coexist with wrong causal estimates is well-known in econometrics and causal ML, but applying it systematically to world models in epidemiology is novel.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical trial analysis**: Ensuring that machine-learning-based post-hoc analyses of randomized trials don't accidentally learn the wrong treatment effect when trials use adaptive randomization, stratification, or complex sampling.
2. **Real-world evidence from electronic health records (EHR)**: When fitting models to EHR data (which is heavily shaped by patient flow, physician behavior, recording practices), design-respecting constraints prevent spurious causal conclusions.
3. **Infectious disease forecasting under intervention**: During a pandemic, a world model trained on observational data from varying testing regimes (test-negative design) must encode testing protocol to correctly predict the effect of interventions like vaccination campaigns.
4. **Observational study synthesis**: When combining data from multiple studies with different designs (e.g., case-control vs. cohort), explicitly modeling design differences prevents pooled estimates from conflating study-specific artifacts with true effects.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **EHR causal inference audit tool**: Software that ingests a fitted predictive/causal model and a description of the data collection process, then flags potential design-alignment violations and suggests model retraining. Target: hospital systems, pharma companies doing real-world evidence studies.
2. **Epidemiological model validator**: A service for regulatory bodies (FDA, EMA) that automatically tests whether submitted analyses (e.g., for a drug approval) use design-respecting models, reducing the risk of invalid causal claims.
3. **Trial design-aware ML platform**: A training library that automatically embeds common trial designs (RCT, cluster-RCT, test-negative design) as constraints into world models, reducing user error.
4. **Causal inference insurance**: A consulting or validation service for biotech/pharma that stress-tests causal estimates from machine-learning models across different design assumptions, offering confidence that conclusions are robust.
5. **Open-source epidemiology ML framework**: A PyTorch/JAX library providing drop-in world model architectures (e.g., latent ODEs, neural processes) pre-configured for standard epidemiological designs, making design-respecting modeling the default.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited scope of empirical test**: The paper evaluates one design (cluster-randomized test-negative trial) on one dataset; it's unclear how well the findings generalize to other designs (stratified RCTs, adaptive designs, case-cohort studies).
2. **Design specification burden**: A practitioner must correctly specify the study design as model constraints; misspecification of the design itself could lead to false confidence in a "design-respecting" but actually misspecified model.
3. **Scalability to high-dimensional latent spaces**: The paper doesn't address how to encode complex designs (e.g., multi-level hierarchies, time-varying assignment) into the latent-world interface of a large, realistic world model.
4. **Offline evaluation only**: All comparisons are retrospective (resampling from fixed data). It's unclear whether the stability gains of design-respecting models hold under prospective validation or distribution shift.
5. **No comparison to classical epidemiological methods**: The paper doesn't benchmark against standard approaches (inverse probability weighting, regression adjustment) that epidemiologists routinely use and trust.

## Open Questions
1. **How do design-respecting and design-ignoring models compare under **model misspecification** (e.g., latent structure is wrong)? Does design-respecting architecture still help?**
2. **Can you automatically infer or test the adequacy of a design constraint given observed data, or must it always be specified a priori?**
3. **For complex, multi-stage designs (e.g., two-phase case-control within a cohort), how does one systematically encode all constraints without combinatorial explosion?**
4. **Do design-respecting models require larger sample sizes or more carefully tuned hyperparameters than design-ignoring ones?**
5. **Can these ideas transfer to other domains (e.g., economics, climate science) where study design shapes inference, or are they specific to epidemiology?**

## Key Takeaway
Perfect data fit isn't enough; world models must encode study design constraints to learn stable, valid causal effects.
