# Learned, Then Lost: A Measured Single-Example Counterfactual in Pre-training

**Authors:** Zachary Speck, Asa Shepard
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.19168v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19168v1)

## Summary
This paper measures—rather than estimates—how much a single training example influences a finished language model by running expensive counterfactual pre-training experiments. The authors trained 32 GPT-2 models from scratch, injecting a 194-token passage into one batch at a critical moment, then tracked what happened to the model's weights, loss landscape, and learned representations. They find that examples are learned quickly but then forgotten completely, leaving almost no trace in the final model.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on **mechanistic interpretability** — the study of how individual training data shapes neural network behavior — and traces back to foundational work on data attribution and influence functions. Prior work typically estimates a single example's importance using gradient-based approximations (influence functions) rather than measuring it directly, because the true measurement requires training identical models that differ only in one training row. The authors use **loss landscape geometry** (barrier measurements, kernel alignment across layers) and **weight displacement** as tools to detect influence, drawing on concepts from loss landscape analysis and neural network geometry that study how training trajectories navigate high-dimensional parameter space.

## Core Contributions
1. **First large-scale measured counterfactual**: Rather than approximate, the authors actually ran 24 pairs of full pre-training runs (32 models total) differing in a single batch element. This is genuinely rare at any scale.
2. **Quantified learning and forgetting**: They show a passage is learned to measurable advantage (0.039–0.044 nats) within 50 steps, then completely forgotten by end of training (no detectable difference, p > 0.25).
3. **Geometric evidence of local relocation**: Weight displacement reaches 44% of seed-to-seed variance but the loss landscape barrier stays at only 3%, implying the model moves within its local basin without escaping it.
4. **Null findings on downstream impact**: No detectible effect on held-out cross-entropy, interpolation loss, or per-layer kernel alignment at final checkpoint — the injection literally vanishes.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates directly to [[influence functions]] and data attribution, but sidesteps their approximation errors through brute-force measurement. It complements [[mechanistic-interpretability]] work like circuit analysis and feature attribution by measuring *causal impact on model geometry* rather than activation patterns. The "learned then forgotten" pattern echoes results from continual learning and catastrophic forgetting literature, though here the forgetting is natural and complete. The loss landscape analysis connects to work on mode connectivity and loss surface geometry that emerged from papers studying neural network loss landscapes.

## Practical Applications
1. **Data valuation for dataset curation**: Understanding which examples are truly retained could inform which data is worth including in expensive pre-training runs.
2. **Contamination detection**: If a harmful or copyrighted passage sneaks into training, this methodology could measure whether it actually influences the final model or is naturally forgotten.
3. **Training efficiency**: Identifying which training steps matter most (the paper's injection at step 200 of 9,536 was deliberate) could guide selective retraining or data ordering strategies.
4. **Model transparency audits**: For regulatory or safety purposes, demonstrate empirically that a single problematic example doesn't persist in model weights.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"DataDNA" — a data valuation platform** that runs counterfactual pre-training at scale to identify which rows in your dataset actually matter for downstream performance, selling to ML teams optimizing training costs.
2. **Contamination insurance for LLM providers** — periodically inject synthetic test data and measure if it persists; offer insurance that harmful data won't survive to production models.
3. **Training efficiency optimizer** — analyze loss landscapes and weight displacement to identify "dead zones" in training data and skip redundant examples.
4. **Audit-as-a-service for LLM safety**: Large labs pay to have counterfactual experiments run on their pre-training to certify that problematic data (leaked secrets, personal info, etc.) doesn't influence final weights.
5. **Research consulting**: Offer expensive but authoritative counterfactual experiments for disputes over data attribution or model IP.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Tiny scale**: 124M parameters (GPT-2 small) is orders of magnitude below modern LLMs; forgetting may work differently at 7B, 70B, or 500B parameters.
2. **Single example per run**: The paper injects *one* passage per training run. Real contamination or adversarial scenarios involve repeated or correlated examples, which may accumulate differently.
3. **Artificial injection point**: Inserting at step 200 of 9,536 during peak learning rate is convenient but may not reflect natural distribution or critical training phases.
4. **Statistical power vs. effect size**: Minimum detectable effects are sometimes comparable to or larger than effects of interest (e.g., 0.079 nats for held-out vs. -0.00044 nats observed).
5. **Closed dataset**: Only OpenWebText; generalization to code, math, domain-specific data is unknown.
6. **No mechanistic explanation**: The paper measures *that* forgetting happens, not *why* — no analysis of which layers or attention heads participate in learning or unlearning the passage.

## Open Questions
1. **Does forgetting scale with model size?** Run the same experiment at 350M, 1B, 7B parameters to see if larger models retain more information.
2. **What if the example is repeated?** Inject the same passage twice, four times, or at multiple training steps — does forgetting still occur?
3. **Which phases matter?** Repeat injection at early, mid, and late training (not just step 200) to map which periods show irreversibility.
4. **Is forgetting uniform?** Use mechanistic interpretability tools (layer-wise probes, attention analysis) to show *which parts of the model* learn and forget the passage.
5. **Does fine-tuning resurrect it?** If you fine-tune the final model on that passage, is learning faster (suggesting a trace remains) or identical to learning a new passage?
6. **Does adversarial design help retention?** Try adversarially-crafted passages designed to be "sticky" — can you engineer examples that survive to the final model?

## Key Takeaway
Training data is learned quickly but completely forgotten by end of training, leaving no measurable trace in model weights or loss landscape geometry.
