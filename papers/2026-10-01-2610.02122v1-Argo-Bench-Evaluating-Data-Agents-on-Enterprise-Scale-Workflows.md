# Argo-Bench: Evaluating Data Agents on Enterprise-Scale Workflows

**Authors:** Gabriel Tomitsuka, Arman Raayatsanati, Emma Xing, Duke Gand, Joseph J Ma
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02122v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02122v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Argo-Bench, a benchmark for evaluating AI agents on realistic enterprise data workflows. Unlike existing text-to-SQL benchmarks that test query generation in isolation, Argo-Bench requires agents to reason across hundreds of tables, perform statistical analyses, and take real-world actions—all graded by their actual consequences in a simulator. Even the strongest models solve only 35% of tasks, revealing a significant gap between current capabilities and enterprise-ready AI.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on a long history of semantic parsing and text-to-SQL benchmarks (e.g., WikiSQL, Spider), which evaluate how well models translate natural language into SQL queries. However, these benchmarks have two known weaknesses: they assume business logic fits in a single table, and their answer keys often contain errors. Argo-Bench extends beyond query generation by introducing *agent-based reasoning*—the idea that AI systems must not only retrieve data but also plan multi-step operations, navigate complex schemas, and execute actions with real consequences. The benchmark simulates a complete enterprise resource planning (ERP) system modeled on Oracle E-Business Suite, grounding tasks in realistic economics, fraud patterns, and marketplace dynamics.

## Core Contributions
1. **Argo-Bench itself**: A 210-task benchmark with 235 tables and 7.5 billion rows, simulating a food delivery platform at true scale with 81 million orders, grounded in real regulatory filings and peer-reviewed industry literature.
2. **Executable ground truth**: Every task has a solvable reference solution; the simulator's true state is hidden from agents, forcing them to reconstruct facts from incomplete warehouse views.
3. **Outcome-based evaluation**: Tasks are graded not on query correctness but on consequences—whether banning a fraudulent account, allocating budgets, or issuing refunds actually improves marketplace health in the simulator.
4. **Reality-gap measurement**: Demonstrates that frontier LLMs (both proprietary and open-weight) achieve only 59.5 average points and 34.8% success on hard tasks, despite near-100% performance on simpler benchmarks.

## Connections to Other Work
This work directly critiques and extends [[text-to-SQL]] benchmarks like Spider and WikiSQL. It aligns with the broader push toward [[agents]] that can plan and act (rather than just predict), similar to work on [[reinforcement-learning]] for decision-making and tool-use frameworks. The outcome-based grading mirrors ideas from [[alignment]] research, where model outputs are evaluated by their real-world consequences rather than superficial correctness. The emphasis on navigating complex schemas and multi-hop reasoning connects to work on reasoning with knowledge graphs and [[graph-neural-networks]].

## Practical Applications
1. **Enterprise data automation**: Automating analytics, anomaly detection, and corrective actions in supply chains, e-commerce, and financial operations.
2. **Fraud detection and remediation**: Automatically identifying fraudulent accounts or transactions and taking action (blocking, issuing refunds) without manual review.
3. **Inventory and resource allocation**: Agents allocating budgets, incentives, and resources based on real-time warehouse data.
4. **Compliance and auditing**: Automated reconstruction of facts from messy enterprise databases for regulatory filings and internal audits.
5. **Decision support**: AI assistants that synthesize data across dozens of tables to recommend strategic decisions to humans.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Enterprise AI co-pilot**: A software product that runs on companies' existing ERP warehouses, answering complex business questions and automatically executing safe actions (e.g., approving refunds, flagging fraud). Monetize per-seat or per-action.
2. **Data integrity and forensics service**: SaaS for reconstructing facts from messy, multi-table warehouses; audit logs and compliance reports auto-generated by agents. Target mid-market and regulated industries.
3. **Marketplace intelligence platform**: For e-commerce and logistics companies—real-time monitoring of fraud, incentive optimization, and supplier/courier performance. Competitor to Stripe's fraud or Uber's algorithmic dispatch.
4. **Self-healing data warehouse**: Agents that detect schema inconsistencies, missing joins, and data quality issues, then propose and execute fixes. Sell to data teams managing legacy ERP systems.
5. **Autonomous analytics for regulated sectors**: Compliance-aware data agents for financial institutions, healthcare, and pharma that generate audit-ready reports and recommend actions within regulatory boundaries.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Simulator vs. reality gap**: Argo-Bench simulates a single domain (food delivery). Real ERP systems have idiosyncratic schemas, business rules, and data quality issues that may not transfer.
2. **No multi-agent coordination**: Agents work solo; real enterprises need multiple systems (inventory, finance, HR) to coordinate, raising complexity beyond this benchmark.
3. **Perfect action semantics**: The benchmark assumes agents can execute actions with well-defined consequences. In reality, actions often have side effects, latency, and rollback requirements.
4. **Schema and documentation quality**: The benchmark assumes clean, well-documented schemas. Many enterprises inherit undocumented or inconsistently named tables.
5. **Limited to deterministic logic**: Fraud detection, incentive allocation, and other tasks assume deterministic rules. Real-world decisions often involve judgment, negotiation, and human override.
6. **Scalability of reasoning**: As warehouses grow (tens of thousands of tables), the agent's ability to navigate and reason over the schema degrades.

## Open Questions
1. What architectural innovations (e.g., schema summarization, hierarchical reasoning) are needed for agents to scale to truly massive warehouses?
2. How do agents recover from or explain incorrect actions? Can they be trained to ask for human approval before taking high-stakes actions?
3. Do agents trained on Argo-Bench generalize to other industries and ERP schemas, or is each domain a separate task?
4. Can agents learn from the consequences of past actions (reinforcement learning) to improve, or do they require explicit feedback?
5. How much in-context schema documentation or domain knowledge does an agent need to achieve >80% performance? Can this be learned rather than provided?
6. How do agents handle data quality issues—missing values, duplicate records, schema drift—that are rampant in real warehouses?

## Key Takeaway
Enterprise data agents must navigate complex, multi-table databases and act on their findings; existing LLMs struggle, revealing a major gap in real-world AI capabilities.
