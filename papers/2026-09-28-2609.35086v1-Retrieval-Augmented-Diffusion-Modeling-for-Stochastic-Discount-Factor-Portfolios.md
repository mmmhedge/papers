# Retrieval-Augmented Diffusion Modeling for Stochastic Discount Factor Portfolios

**Authors:** Kelvin J. L. Koa, Xinyang Li, Ke-Wei Huang
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35086v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35086v1)

## Summary
This paper combines diffusion models (a type of generative AI that learns data distributions by gradually denoising noise) with retrieval-augmented learning to optimize stock portfolios under the stochastic discount factor framework. The key insight is that financial markets shift regimes unpredictably, and the paper's method (RADAR) learns better representations of market risk by conditioning on similar historical states, achieving better risk-adjusted returns than existing approaches.

## Theoretical Background
Portfolio optimization traditionally relies on the stochastic discount factor (SDF) framework — a financial theory that prices assets by discounting their future cash flows using a stochastic factor that varies with market conditions and investor risk preferences. Diffusion models are neural networks that learn data distributions by iteratively denoising randomly initialized noise; they've become powerful for generating and modeling complex distributions. The paper builds on two key prior concepts: (1) the limitation that standard diffusion models assume isotropic Gaussian noise (simple, spherical noise distributions) which fails when financial uncertainty is state-dependent (varies with market conditions), and (2) retrieval augmentation — the practice of conditioning neural models on semantically similar historical examples to improve generalization. The combination addresses non-stationary financial dynamics, where market regimes shift and historical patterns are relevant but not directly applicable.

## Core Contributions
1. **RADAR framework**: A retrieval-augmented diffusion model specifically designed for SDF-based portfolio optimization that conditions the diffusion process on similar historical market regimes rather than using fixed noise assumptions.
2. **Context-dependent noise distributions**: Uses retrieval to construct noise distributions that adapt to current market state, replacing the standard isotropic Gaussian assumption.
3. **Empirical initialization**: Initializes the diffusion process with historical statistics to reflect state-dependent uncertainty rather than random initialization.
4. **Multimodal representation learning**: Handles heterogeneous financial data (price signals, news sentiment) within a unified diffusion framework that denoise and fuses them into coherent market representations.
5. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates state-of-the-art risk-adjusted performance metrics and produces economically interpretable signals for asset returns and correlations.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[generative-models|generative modeling]] and quantitative finance. It extends diffusion models — which have seen recent success in vision, NLP, and time-series modeling — to the financial domain. It relates to [[RLHF|conditional generative modeling]] in the sense that both condition generation on external signals (here: historical regimes). The retrieval component echoes in-context learning approaches used in large language models. The core contribution differs from standard conditional diffusion because it explicitly uses *similarity-based retrieval* rather than class labels or explicit conditioning variables. It also contrasts with [[reinforcement-learning]] approaches to portfolio optimization (which learn policies) by taking a representational learning angle: RADAR learns better market state representations that then enable better portfolio decisions.

## Practical Applications
1. **Institutional asset management**: Deploy RADAR in quantitative hedge funds and asset managers to optimize multi-asset portfolios (stocks, bonds, commodities) under realistic non-stationary market conditions, potentially improving Sharpe ratios by 10–30 basis points.
2. **Risk monitoring systems**: Use learned state representations to flag regime shifts and automatically rebalance portfolios when the model detects transitions to historically rare market states.
3. **Tail risk hedging**: Leverage the state-dependent uncertainty estimates to identify and hedge tail risks more accurately than standard methods, reducing drawdowns in market crises.
4. **Factor-based investing**: Extract the learned market representations as new factors for factor investing strategies, potentially uncovering regime-dependent risk premiums.
5. **Real-time news integration**: Combine price and sentiment data (multimodal) to dynamically adjust portfolio allocations based on emerging market narratives.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational overhead**: Diffusion models require many denoising steps (often 1000+); scaling to real-time portfolio rebalancing at high frequency may be prohibitive without GPU infrastructure.
2. **Regime definition ambiguity**: The paper doesn't fully specify how "similar historical regimes" are identified. If the retrieval similarity metric is poorly calibrated, conditioning on wrong historical analogs will harm performance.
3. **Multimodal fusion challenges**: Combining price and news data introduces data quality and alignment issues (news may lag or mislabel assets); no clear ablation shows robustness to news quality.
4. **Non-stationarity assumption**: Even with retrieval, the assumption that past regimes meaningfully predict future ones breaks during structural market changes (e.g., introduction of new asset classes, regime shifts beyond historical data).
5. **SDF framework constraints**: The SDF framework itself assumes rational expectations and pricing efficiency; it may fail in illiquid markets or during market dislocations.
6. **Limited backtest horizon**: Results likely tested on recent years; unclear if the method generalizes to different economic eras (e.g., high-inflation, negative-rate regimes).

## Open Questions
1. How sensitive is RADAR to the choice of retrieval metric and neighborhood size? What ablations would show this?
2. Can the learned noise distributions be interpreted economically (e.g., do they correspond to known risk factors like volatility, illiquidity)?
3. How does performance degrade in regimes with very few historical analogs (tail scenarios)?
4. Can RADAR be extended to derivatives pricing or options hedging, or does it only apply to spot portfolios?
5. Does conditioning on retrieved regimes outperform simpler baselines like regime-switching models (e.g., Hidden Markov Models) on the same SDF problem?
6. How does RADAR perform out-of-sample on truly unseen market regimes (e.g., COVID-era volatility if trained pre-2020)?

## Key Takeaway
Diffusion models improve portfolio optimization by learning market representations conditioned on similar historical market states rather than generic noise.
