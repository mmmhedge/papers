# From transcription to semantic corpus analysis: unsupervised learning of sentence representations for ancient languages

**Authors:** Th{é}otime de la Selle
**Published:** 2026-07-27 | **arXiv:** [2607.24542v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.24542v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles the challenge of finding semantic similarities in ancient texts (Latin and Ancient Greek) where standard language models fail. The authors develop unsupervised methods to adapt specialized language models into sentence encoders that can identify text reuse—when ancient authors quote or paraphrase earlier works—without requiring labeled training data, demonstrating strong results on identifying biblical passages reused in patristic literature.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two unsupervised sentence embedding methods: **TSDAE** (a denoising autoencoder approach) and **CSE** (contrastive sentence embedding, which learns representations by contrasting similar and dissimilar sentence pairs). These methods adapt **token-level language models**—neural networks trained to predict words in context—into sentence-level encoders that represent entire sentences as vectors in a shared space.

The paper addresses a core problem in computational philology: existing multilingual language models (like mBERT) don't capture the nuances of ancient languages because they were trained on modern texts, and anisotropic representation spaces (where embeddings cluster poorly) limit downstream tasks. The authors leverage **UMAP** (a dimensionality reduction technique) to visualize how different training strategies reshape embedding geometry.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical comparison** of TSDAE vs. CSE on ancient language sentence embeddings, showing they have complementary strengths: TSDAE excels at detection tasks with large corpora, CSE at retrieval with minimal data (4-8k sentences).
2. **Robustness to noise**: Systematic evaluation on artificially corrupted text simulating OCR/HTR (Handwritten Text Recognition) errors and scribal abbreviations—a critical real-world condition often ignored in NLP benchmarks.
3. **Cross-domain transfer**: Demonstration that CSE learned on one set of ancient texts generalizes to unseen authors and works, even after retraining on noisy post-OCR output.
4. **Practical artifact**: Release of Paraphrasis, an online tool packaging the full pipeline (segmentation → fine-tuning → cross-corpus search) for non-specialists, lowering barriers to computational textual analysis.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of:
- **Sentence embedding literature** ([[Sentence-BERT]], contrastive learning methods) but adapted to a low-resource, non-modern-language setting where those baselines fail.
- **Computational philology / digital humanities**: Extends prior work on [[text reuse detection]] to handle multiple ancient languages and OCR noise simultaneously.
- **Few-shot / zero-shot learning**: CSE's ability to work with 4-8k sentences foreshadows broader trends in efficient adaptation of language models.
- **Noisy text robustness**: Related to work on handling HTR and OCR errors, but here integrated into end-to-end evaluation rather than treated as preprocessing.

## Practical Applications
1. **Digital editions and critical apparatus**: Automatically populate scholarly editions with cross-references to parallel passages, reducing manual scholarly labor.
2. **Text reuse forensics**: Detect plagiarism, borrowing, and authorship chains in ancient/medieval corpora (e.g., identifying which Church Fathers cite earlier theologians).
3. **Intertextuality analysis**: Map literary influence networks in classical literature (Homer echoes in Virgil, etc.).
4. **Manuscript collation**: Assist in aligning fragmentary texts or variant manuscripts by finding semantically equivalent passages.
5. **Corpus linguistics for endangered/historical languages**: Extend to other low-resource ancient languages (Old Church Slavonic, Akkadian) where modern NLP tools don't exist.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Scholarly annotation plugin**: Browser extension for digital libraries (Perseus, JSTOR) that highlights parallel passages in real time while reading ancient texts, powered by fine-tuned encoders.
2. **DH-SaaS for institutions**: Subscription service for universities to upload their digitized manuscript collections and automatically generate reuse maps and critical apparatuses.
3. **Paraphrasis premium**: Freemium tool with advanced search (semantic + syntactic filters, author/time-period constraints), collaboration features for research groups, and export to scholarly publishing formats.
4. **Intertextuality visualizer**: Interactive network graph product showing how ideas/phrases flow between authors across centuries—marketable to classicists, theologians, literary scholars.
5. **Custom language fine-tuning as a service**: Offer sentence embedding adaptation (TSDAE/CSE) for any historical/specialized corpus (medieval manuscripts, 17th-century legal texts, etc.) via API, handling OCR noise automatically.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Evaluation limited to two language pairs**: Results on Latin and Ancient Greek may not generalize equally to other ancient languages with different morphological complexity (e.g., Akkadian, Sanskrit).
2. **Noise model is synthetic**: Artificial corruption may not capture the full distribution of real OCR/HTR errors; performance on unseen real historical document types is untested.
3. **Scalability unclear**: No reported metrics on training time, memory, or computational cost for very large corpora; CSE's contrastive learning can be expensive with weak negatives.
4. **Circular definition of "reuse"**: Expert-verified parallels are used as ground truth, but what counts as meaningful reuse is subjective and domain-dependent; the 2,935-parallel dataset is modest.
5. **Assumes target domain data available**: CSE still requires 4-8k in-domain sentences; transfer to truly unseen ancient languages with zero in-domain data is not demonstrated.
6. **Ablation studies sparse**: Limited analysis of which design choices (loss functions, pretraining objectives, hyperparameters) most matter.

## Open Questions
1. **Cross-lingual ancient-language transfer**: Can a CSE model trained on Latin reuse transfer to Ancient Greek without retraining? How do typological features (inflection, word order) affect transfer?
2. **Paraphrase vs. quotation**: Can the method distinguish verbatim quotation from paraphrase, or does it conflate both as "reuse"? Real scholars need this distinction.
3. **Longer-range dependencies**: How do methods perform on multi-sentence parallels or thematic reuse (same ideas, different words) rather than lexical/syntactic overlap?
4. **Interactive active learning**: Could a scholar iteratively correct false positives to improve the encoder online, without full retraining?
5. **Alignment and span detection**: Current evaluation separates detection and retrieval; can the method localize *which exact spans* correspond between two documents?
6. **Temporal awareness**: Do representations respect chronology (later authors cite earlier ones)? Can the method weight earlier texts as "sources"?

## Key Takeaway
Unsupervised sentence encoders fine-tuned on small ancient-text corpora find semantic parallels better than generic multilingual models, even when text is corrupted by OCR.
