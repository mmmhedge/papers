# The Missing Minimal Pair: Stereotype Evaluation in LLMs

**Authors:** Nataliya Stepanova, Ivan Titov, Emily Allaway, Björn Ross
**Published:** 2026-10-06 | **arXiv:** [2610.08747v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08747v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies a critical flaw in how we measure bias in large language models: comparing just two opposite sentences often produces logically inconsistent results. The authors propose a better method using "dual minimal pairs" — comparing along two dimensions simultaneously — and introduce metrics based on information theory to make stereotype evaluation more reliable across languages and models.

## Theoretical Background
Bias measurement in LLMs typically relies on minimal pairs — two nearly identical sentences that differ only in a key attribute (e.g., gender or ethnicity). The idea is to compare how likely the model finds each sentence by looking at log-likelihoods (probability scores). However, this approach assumes that a higher score for one sentence automatically means a lower (more biased) score for the other. The paper builds on prior work in [[bias evaluation]] and [[stereotype measurement]] in NLP, but identifies a gap: existing single-pair comparisons can be logically contradictory. The authors introduce mutual information (MI) — a concept from information theory measuring how much knowing one variable tells you about another — as a more principled framework for capturing bias strength.

## Core Contributions
1. **Demonstration of inconsistency**: Shows empirically that flipping stereotypes in existing datasets (e.g., "women are nurses" → "men are nurses") can yield contradictory model preferences, undermining confidence in single-pair evaluations.
2. **Dual minimal pair framework**: Proposes comparing along two independent axes simultaneously, which constrains model behavior and reduces logical inconsistency.
3. **Data augmentation pipeline**: Develops a systematic method to generate paraphrases and alternative attributes, filling gaps in existing stereotype datasets across four languages (English, Russian, Spanish, Chinese).
4. **MI-based evaluation metric**: Introduces a mutual information metric that quantifies the strength of association between social groups and stereotyped attributes in a way that aggregates robustly across models and languages.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends recent criticism of simplistic [[bias measurement]] approaches in NLP. It complements broader work on [[model alignment]] and [[fairness in LLMs]], which also grapple with reliable ways to measure and mitigate unwanted model behaviors. The mutual information framing relates to work in [[mechanistic interpretability]] insofar as it aims to understand what associations a model has learned. The multilingual focus echoes growing interest in understanding how bias manifests across languages differently.

## Practical Applications
- **Model evaluation pipelines**: Companies deploying LLMs for customer-facing applications (customer service, hiring, content moderation) can use this framework to audit bias more reliably before release.
- **Compliance and auditing**: Regulatory and compliance teams checking whether models violate fairness standards in hiring, lending, or content recommendation systems.
- **Model comparison**: Researchers and practitioners choosing between different LLMs can now make fairer comparisons of bias profiles.
- **Multilingual model development**: Teams building systems for non-English markets can assess whether stereotypes differ across languages and adapt accordingly.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Bias-as-a-Service platform**: A SaaS tool that runs this dual minimal pair evaluation on any custom or third-party LLM, generating a comprehensive bias report by social group and attribute; target: enterprises using LLMs in sensitive domains (hiring, lending, healthcare).
2. **Model governance dashboard**: Internal tool for AI teams to track bias metrics over model versions, with alerts when stereotypes drift; integrates with model registries and CI/CD pipelines.
3. **Multilingual fairness auditor**: Specialized consultancy that evaluates how LLMs behave differently across languages and cultural contexts, helping companies avoid country-specific bias issues.
4. **Open-source stereotype corpus**: Curated, multilingual dataset of stereotypes with dual minimal pairs and associated metrics, monetized via paid annotation, custom updates, or research partnerships.
5. **Red-teaming / adversarial testing service**: On-demand testing where clients submit models and receive detailed bias and stereotype reports, similar to security auditing but for fairness.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scope**: The framework focuses on explicit stereotypes (occupations, traits) and may miss implicit or structural biases that emerge in context-dependent tasks.
- **Language coverage**: While multilingual, the approach is demonstrated on only four languages; it's unclear how well it generalizes to lower-resource or morphologically different languages.
- **Paraphrase quality**: The data augmentation relies on generating paraphrases and alternate attributes; errors or biases in generation could propagate into evaluation.
- **Attribute selection**: Requires manual specification of which attributes to evaluate (e.g., gender, race); choosing incomplete or culturally inappropriate attributes could miss important biases.
- **Single-model evaluation**: Metrics are designed for a specific model and snapshot in time; it's unclear how sensitive they are to model retraining, fine-tuning, or prompt engineering.
- **Causality vs. correlation**: MI-based metrics measure association, not causal stereotype endorsement; a model may have learned correlations without actually "believing" in stereotypes.

## Open Questions
- How does the dual minimal pair approach scale to more complex, multi-faceted stereotypes (e.g., intersectional gender + race + class)?
- Can the framework be extended to implicit or context-dependent biases, rather than just explicit pairwise comparisons?
- How robust are the MI-based metrics when paraphrases or alternate attributes are noisy or incomplete?
- Does the framework predict downstream harm? (i.e., does a model with high stereotype strength in this evaluation actually produce biased outputs in real tasks?)
- How do these metrics correlate with human judgments of stereotype offensiveness or harmfulness across different cultural contexts?
- Can the approach guide bias mitigation — e.g., does it identify which stereotypes are easiest to reduce via retraining or prompt engineering?

## Key Takeaway
Comparing just two opposite sentences is unreliable for measuring bias in LLMs; dual comparisons and information-theoretic metrics give more consistent results across languages.
