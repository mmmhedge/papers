# Expressivity In Multimodal Contrastive Learning

**Authors:** Andrew Stuart, Florian Wolf
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.17203v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17203v1)

## Summary
This paper analyzes the theoretical expressivity of multimodal contrastive learning architectures—specifically models like CLIP that learn joint representations of text, images, and other modalities. The authors prove that the standard two-tower CLIP design can represent any joint distribution (universal approximation), but a common three-or-more-modality extension cannot, motivating a new "Hadamard-CLIP" variant that restores universal approximation while staying computationally efficient.

## Theoretical Background
Contrastive learning trains models by pulling similar examples together and pushing different ones apart in an embedding space. CLIP — a foundational model in vision-language AI — learns a shared representation by matching image and text embeddings. The paper adopts a **population-level density-estimation view**: instead of asking "does this model work on real data?", they ask "which probability distributions can this architecture *in principle* represent?" This connects to classical approximation theory questions: universal approximators are function families flexible enough to match any target function arbitrarily well. The paper's contribution is proving which contrastive architectures have this universal property and which don't.

## Core Contributions

1. **Expressivity characterization of two-modality CLIP**: Proves the standard CLIP architecture is a universal approximator for any two-modality joint distribution.

2. **Impossibility result for naive multimodal extension**: Shows that the natural three-or-more-modality generalization (summing pairwise similarities in the loss) *cannot* represent arbitrary joint distributions—only all pairwise conditionals. This is a hard theoretical limitation.

3. **Hadamard-CLIP**: A simple fix adding a single learned weight vector that (a) restores universal approximation for any number of modalities, (b) preserves fast, precomputable-embedding retrieval (critical for real-world systems), and (c) has minimal computational overhead.

4. **Formal proofs**: Rigorous density-estimation arguments characterizing exactly which distributions lie in each architecture's representational capacity.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader efforts to understand [[CLIP]] and vision-language models theoretically. It sits at the intersection of **representation learning expressivity** (e.g., prior work on neural network universal approximation) and **contrastive learning theory** (understanding when and why contrastive objectives work). The density-estimation framing is novel in this domain—most prior work on multimodal learning focuses on empirical performance rather than theoretical capacity. It also connects implicitly to questions in [[mechanistic-interpretability]]: understanding what these models *can* and *cannot* represent is a step toward understanding their internal behavior.

## Practical Applications

1. **Improving multimodal retrieval systems**: Any product using 3+ modalities (e.g., text + image + audio retrieval) can benefit from Hadamard-CLIP's better expressivity without speed penalties.

2. **Vision-language model pretraining**: Scaling CLIP-style models to audio, video, 3D geometry, and other modalities—Hadamard-CLIP removes a theoretical bottleneck.

3. **Cross-modal recommendation**: E-commerce and media platforms matching users across product images, descriptions, reviews, and user behavior can use more expressive architectures.

4. **Medical imaging + text reports**: Aligning radiology images with clinical notes and structured patient data requires multimodal expressivity.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **Multimodal search API**: A drop-in service offering Hadamard-CLIP embeddings for documents, images, video, and audio—faster and theoretically more expressive than competitors' CLIP variants.

2. **Smart content tagging platform**: Automatically tag e-commerce or media content across multiple modalities (text, image, video, metadata) with a unified model—leverage the improved expressivity for rare content types.

3. **Medical diagnosis assistant**: Combine patient records (text notes), imaging (X-ray, CT, MRI), and lab results in a single expressive contrastive model for clinical decision support.

4. **Real estate search engine**: Embed property listings across photos, floor plans, neighborhood text descriptions, and virtual tours in a unified space—Hadamard-CLIP's efficiency enables fast retrieval.

5. **Accessibility-first content discovery**: Build a system that indexes the same content across text, audio descriptions, visual captions, and sign-language video—multimodal expressivity ensures no modality is lost.

## Technical Limitations

1. **Population-level vs. finite-sample performance**: The paper proves what *can* be represented in principle; it does not address how many samples or how much compute is needed to learn these representations from real data. Expressivity ≠ learnability.

2. **Loss function agnostic**: The results characterize architecture capacity but do not account for whether standard contrastive losses (e.g., InfoNCE) actually drive learning toward expressive regions of the parameter space in practice.

3. **No empirical validation**: The paper is purely theoretical. Hadamard-CLIP may have the right expressivity but could underperform empirically if the learned weights don't converge well or if the added vector creates optimization challenges.

4. **Computational cost not fully analyzed**: While "preserved," the cost of computing the Hadamard-CLIP weight vector and its interaction during training isn't rigorously benchmarked.

5. **Assumption: continuous distributions**: Results assume densities; discrete or mixed modalities (e.g., categorical attributes + images) may behave differently.

## Open Questions

1. **Sample complexity**: How many training examples are needed to actually learn a Hadamard-CLIP model that matches the theoretical expressivity? Is there a sample-complexity gap?

2. **Loss landscape**: Does the standard contrastive loss (InfoNCE, NT-Xent) have favorable geometry for Hadamard-CLIP? Are there local minima that prevent learning expressive representations?

3. **Empirical comparison**: How does Hadamard-CLIP perform on real multimodal benchmarks (vision-language, audio-text, etc.) versus current practice? Does expressivity translate to downstream task improvement?

4. **Scalability to many modalities**: The paper proves universality for "any number" of modalities, but what happens at 10+ modalities? Is there a phase transition in optimization difficulty?

5. **Extension to other architectures**: Can similar expressivity gaps and fixes be identified for other multimodal architectures (e.g., transformer-based fusion, product-based models)?

6. **Conditional expressivity**: The pairwise-similarity architecture captures all pairwise conditionals but not the full joint. Is there a natural middle ground—e.g., a loss capturing all *k*-way conditionals for some *k* < number of modalities?

## Key Takeaway
Two-tower CLIP is theoretically universal, but naive multimodal scaling fails—Hadamard-CLIP fixes this with one learned vector.
