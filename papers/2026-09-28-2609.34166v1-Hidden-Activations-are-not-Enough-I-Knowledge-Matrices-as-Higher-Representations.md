# Hidden Activations are not Enough I: Knowledge Matrices as Higher Representations

**Authors:** Marco Armenta
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.34166v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.34166v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes that "knowledge matrices"—a mathematical structure derived from trained neural networks—better capture what networks have learned than hidden activations alone. The authors show these matrices have useful geometric properties: they're invariant to certain transformations, they directly encode attribution information, and they can detect adversarial perturbations. The work matters because understanding what neural networks actually compute is critical for both interpretability and robustness.

## Theoretical Background
Neural network interpretability typically focuses on analyzing hidden activations (the intermediate outputs of neurons). This paper builds on representation theory and quiver representations—mathematical frameworks for understanding how networks decompose inputs. The key insight is that networks can be viewed as maps through a space of abstract representations, and a single matrix captures the essential information about what the network has learned for a given input. The authors work primarily under locally constant slope (LCS) assumptions, which holds for ReLU networks where the activation function is piecewise linear. The knowledge matrix $M(x)$ is constructed so its rows sum to the network's final logits (output scores), grounding it in both linear algebra and attribution theory—methods that explain which input features drive predictions.

## Core Contributions
1. **Knowledge matrices as complete representations**: Shows that knowledge matrices $M(x) \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times (d+1)}$ fully recover the "germ" (local learned structure) of a network, whereas hidden activations alone are insufficient and gauge-covariant (sensitive to hidden transformations).

2. **Invariance and stabilizer theory**: Characterizes what transformations leave the knowledge matrix unchanged—the stabilizer group is exactly the germ stabilizer. Neuron permutation emerges as a special case, providing formal grounding for symmetries practitioners already observe.

3. **Attribution decomposition**: Proves the matrix decomposes into per-class gradient×input plus an exact aggregate bias term, connecting to attribution theory and enabling efficient computation via $C$ vector-Jacobian products rather than probing.

4. **Alignment-free distance metric**: The fixed matrix shape enables architecture-agnostic comparison between ResNet-152, DenseNet-121, and GoogLeNet without alignment procedures.

5. **Adversarial robustness analysis**: Introduces a unit-free coherence measure $A = (d_\Psi/d_M)^2$ that reveals adversarial perturbations move along specific germ directions. Median coherence $A \leq 0.23$ across attacks, with attack-family ordering consistent across six architectures (Kendall $W = 0.921$).

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several interpretability threads:
- [[Mechanistic interpretability]] approaches (like circuit analysis) that aim to reverse-engineer learned computations
- Attribution methods that explain predictions via gradients and input importance
- Neural network symmetries and equivalence—the paper formalizes why different parameter configurations compute identical functions
- Adversarial robustness research, offering a new geometric lens on why perturbations fool networks
- Representation learning theory, particularly how networks factor computations through abstract spaces

The paper contrasts with pure gradient-based interpretability by proposing a richer, matrix-level representation that respects network geometry.

## Practical Applications
1. **Model debugging and comparison**: Compare what different architectures learn without retraining—useful for architecture search and transfer learning analysis.

2. **Adversarial defense**: Early detection of adversarial examples using the coherence metric $A$, since adversarial moves exhibit characteristic low-coherence germ displacements.

3. **Network pruning and distillation**: Identify which neurons contribute meaningfully to the knowledge matrix, informing which to prune or compress.

4. **Trustworthy AI audits**: Attribute model predictions to specific learned patterns in a way that aligns with both mathematics and human intuition, improving explainability for stakeholders.

5. **Transfer learning diagnosis**: Determine whether fine-tuning preserves essential knowledge structures or corrupts them.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Neural Network Transparency SaaS**: A platform that computes knowledge matrices for trained models and generates human-readable attribution reports. Target: compliance-heavy industries (finance, healthcare) needing model explainability.

2. **Adversarial robustness testing tool**: Automated service that tests models using the coherence metric $A$ to flag vulnerability to specific attack families before deployment.

3. **Model audit and comparison software**: Enterprise tool for comparing ML models across teams/vendors without re-training, using the alignment-free distance metric.

4. **Interpretable model distillation service**: Automatically identify and distill only the "knowledge-matrix-essential" parts of large networks into smaller, auditable models.

5. **Real-time model monitoring**: Production monitoring system that tracks whether a deployed model's knowledge matrices degrade over time (dataset drift) or shift unexpectedly (data poisoning).

## Technical Limitations
1. **LCS assumption restriction**: Results assume locally constant slope activations (ReLU, LeakyReLU, etc.). Smooth activations (sigmoid, tanh, GELU) are not addressed; applicability unclear.

2. **Regular input assumption**: The theory requires "regular inputs" (no vanishing coordinates). Boundary cases and inputs near decision boundaries may violate this.

3. **Limited empirical scope**: Experiments on relatively small networks and datasets (AlexNet/CIFAR-10, ResNet-152). Scalability to transformers and large language models unexplored.

4. **Honest negatives**: The paper acknowledges that on AlexNet/CIFAR-10, penultimate layer features outperform knowledge matrices on 5 of 6 adversarial detectors and all 16 attacks—limiting claimed generality.

5. **Counterfactual failure**: The matrix-direction counterfactual approach fails (0 out of 54 cases), suggesting the representation may miss certain aspects of network behavior.

6. **Computational cost**: Requires $C$ vector-Jacobian products per input ($C$ = number of classes), which may be expensive at scale.

## Open Questions
1. **Transformers and attention**: Can knowledge matrices be extended to architectures with attention mechanisms? Do they capture the role of learned attention patterns?

2. **Training dynamics**: How do knowledge matrices evolve during training? Can they predict generalization or identify overfitting?

3. **Scaling laws**: Does the coherence metric $A$ remain predictive for large-scale models and adversarial attacks?

4. **Smooth activations**: What is the correct generalization of knowledge matrices for smooth activation functions?

5. **Cross-architecture transfer**: Can knowledge matrices from one architecture be meaningfully transferred or compared to another without retraining?

6. **Theoretical completeness**: Under what conditions are knowledge matrices provably *necessary* (not just sufficient) for understanding a network's function?

## Key Takeaway
Knowledge matrices reveal what networks learn better than hidden activations and enable architecture-agnostic adversarial detection via geometric properties.
