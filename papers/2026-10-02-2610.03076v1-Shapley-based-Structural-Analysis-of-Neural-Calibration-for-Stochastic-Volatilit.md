# Shapley-based Structural Analysis of Neural Calibration for Stochastic Volatility Models

**Authors:** Shaïn Afzali, Serena Della Corte, Antonis Papapantoleon
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03076v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03076v1)

## Summary
This paper uses Shapley-based explainability methods to understand how neural networks learn to calibrate stochastic volatility models—essentially reverse-engineering which parts of market volatility data matter most for inferring model parameters. The work shows that neural calibration methods are interpretable and can be simplified without losing accuracy, moving beyond just measuring prediction errors to understanding the learned structure itself.

## Theoretical Background
Stochastic volatility models (like Heston and rough Heston) describe how asset price volatility changes over time; they're central to derivatives pricing and risk management but require "calibration"—finding model parameters that match observed market prices. Traditionally this is done via optimization, which is slow. Recent work uses neural networks to learn direct mappings from market data (implied volatility surfaces) to parameters, trading interpretability for speed. Shapley values (from game theory) explain predictions by measuring each input feature's marginal contribution; SHAP captures sensitivity (how much does output change if we remove this feature?) while νSHAP captures sufficiency (how much can we predict with just this feature?). The paper applies these complementary methods to neural calibration for the first time.

## Core Contributions
1. **Systematic Shapley-based structural analysis** of neural calibration mappings across three architectures (MLPs, highway networks, softmax-parametrized highways), showing which regions of the implied volatility surface drive parameter recovery.
2. **Complementary explanation framework**: SHAP and νSHAP reveal distinct notions of relevance (sensitivity vs. sufficiency), exposing redundancy in calibration inputs that single-metric approaches miss.
3. **Dimensionality reduction via νSHAP guidance**: demonstrates that for rough Heston models, input can be substantially compressed while maintaining calibration accuracy—a practical route to simpler, faster inference.
4. **Architectural robustness finding**: dominant attribution structure is stable across model architectures despite differences in accuracy and parameters, suggesting learned mappings capture fundamental problem structure.

## Practical Applications
- **Faster derivative pricing pipelines**: once calibration is reduced to key volatility surface regions, inference systems need fewer inputs and lighter models, cutting latency for real-time pricing desks.
- **Risk model explainability for regulators**: banks can now explain *why* their neural calibration chose specific volatility curves, aiding model validation and audit trails.
- **Parameter recovery in unstable markets**: understanding which maturities/strikes dominate inference helps traders focus on the most liquid, reliable data points during stress periods.
- **Transfer learning in volatility models**: identifying redundancy guides which features to include when adapting calibration models to new underlying assets or market regimes.

## Technical Limitations
- **Limited to smooth, well-behaved inputs**: Shapley methods assume continuous feature spaces; may struggle with discrete market microstructure events or market dislocations.
- **Computational cost of explanations**: SHAP and νSHAP require many forward passes; computing full explanations for every calibration in production may be prohibitive on high-frequency desks.
- **Model-dependent results**: attribution structure reflects learned architecture and training data; different architectures or data regimes may expose different feature importance, limiting universality of findings.
- **Rough Heston focus limited**: empirical findings (e.g., short maturities dominate) may not generalize to other volatility models (SABR, Dupire, local volatility) or multi-asset calibration.
- **No temporal dynamics**: static analysis of implied vol surface; doesn't capture how parameter sensitivity evolves with calendar time, maturity rotation, or volatility regime shifts.
- **Assumes calibration is well-posed**: method assumes market prices are noise-free and model is correctly specified; breaks down under bid-ask bounce, stale data, or model misspecification.

## Open Questions
- **How do explanations degrade under market stress?** Do νSHAP-selected features remain sufficient during crashes or regime breaks?
- **Can Shapley insights transfer across asset classes?** Are short maturities always dominant, or is this Heston-specific?
- **How to handle temporal dimension?** What if we track attribution over rolling windows—does feature importance vary with vol regime or maturity rotation?
- **Comparison with other explainability methods**: how do Shapley-based attributions compare to integrated gradients, attention weights, or saliency maps for this problem?
- **Optimizing for explainability during training**: can we regularize networks to have sparse, interpretable Shapley patterns *by design*, rather than post-hoc analysis?

## Key Takeaway
Neural volatility model calibration is interpretable and compressible via Shapley analysis, revealing which market data regions truly matter for parameter recovery.
