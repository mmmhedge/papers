# The Computational Value of Sensory-Aligned Receptive Fields Depends on Neuronal Expressivity

**Authors:** Agnese Adorante, Aaron Spieler, Anna Levina
**Published:** 2026-09-22 | **arXiv:** [2609.26940v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.26940v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether biologically-inspired structured receptive fields—like those found in sensory neurons that respond selectively to specific features—provide computational benefits beyond just reducing connectivity. Using recurrent neural networks, the authors show that receptive fields aligned with task-relevant sensory coordinates improve accuracy, but this advantage shrinks as individual neurons become more expressive. The finding suggests that structured inputs matter as a computational prior, not merely for wiring efficiency.

## Theoretical Background
Sensory neuroscience has long observed that biological neurons have selective receptive fields organized along meaningful dimensions: visual cortex neurons respond to specific orientations or spatial positions, auditory neurons to frequency ranges. Efficient coding theory proposes that such structure emerges because it minimizes redundancy and metabolic cost. However, most computational studies have focused on simple neurons or resource constraints; the question of whether structure provides a genuine computational advantage independent of sparsity remains open. This work builds on recurrent network theory and sparse neural representation, using Expressive Leaky Memory neurons—a biologically-inspired model where individual neurons can be made arbitrarily complex while controlling their receptive field structure.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical demonstration that task-aligned receptive fields improve accuracy** across auditory and event-based visual tasks, relative to random or scrambled alternatives matched for connectivity budget.
2. **Isolation of the alignment effect**: shows the benefit vanishes when receptive fields follow task-irrelevant coordinates, proving the advantage comes from alignment to task geometry, not connectivity restriction alone.
3. **Quantification of the neuronal expressivity trade-off**: establishes that increasing individual neuron complexity reduces the relative performance gain from structured receptive fields—structured inputs matter less when neurons are more capable.
4. **Demonstration that sparsity regularization alone is insufficient**: generic synaptic sparsity induces some selectivity but cannot recover the full benefit of explicit alignment, suggesting structured receptive fields encode a qualitatively different computational prior.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to classical ideas in [[efficient coding hypothesis]] and [[predictive coding]] models, but extends them by quantifying the interaction between input structure and neuronal capacity. It connects to recent work on [[inductive biases]] in deep learning—the observation that architectural priors can substitute for data or capacity. The finding that expressivity reduces the value of structure parallels results in [[neural architecture search]] showing that highly flexible models can overcome the need for task-specific inductive biases. Finally, it contributes to [[mechanistic interpretability]] by revealing how circuit structure and individual neuron capability jointly determine representational efficiency.

## Practical Applications
1. **Neuromorphic hardware design**: structured receptive fields could reduce power consumption and silicon area in neuromorphic chips for real-time sensory processing (audio, event cameras) without sacrificing accuracy.
2. **Bio-inspired audio processing**: audio classification systems that organize filters along frequency and temporal scales (aligned with cochlear organization) could improve accuracy in low-latency, resource-constrained settings.
3. **Efficient vision models for edge devices**: networks that respect retinotopic or feature-aligned organization in early layers may improve accuracy-to-parameter ratios on mobile or embedded vision tasks.
4. **Robustness to distribution shift**: structured receptive fields may confer robustness benefits beyond in-distribution accuracy—a testable hypothesis with practical value for safety-critical applications.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited task scope**: experiments cover only auditory and event-based visual classification; generalization to language, RL, or more complex visual tasks (e.g., 3D scene understanding) is untested.
2. **Small-scale models**: experiments use relatively small recurrent networks; scalability to transformer-scale or large vision models is unexplored.
3. **Receptive field design by hand**: the paper assumes task-relevant sensory coordinates are known or designed a priori; real-world applicability depends on either domain expertise or unsupervised discovery of relevant coordinates.
4. **Biological plausibility trade-offs**: Expressive Leaky Memory neurons are more biologically plausible than standard RNNs but still far from real neurons; unclear how insights transfer to even more realistic models.
5. **No statistical significance reporting**: the paper does not report error bars or significance tests; robustness to seed variation and hyperparameter tuning is not quantified.
6. **Sparsity regularization baseline**: the sparsity experiments use a relatively simple L1 regularization; more sophisticated structured sparsity methods (e.g., lottery tickets, learned masking) might close the gap.

## Open Questions
1. **Why does expressivity diminish the value of structure?** A mechanistic analysis—e.g., via loss landscape geometry or information-theoretic bounds—would clarify the trade-off.
2. **Can receptive field alignment be learned end-to-end?** Can gradient descent discover optimal task-aligned coordinate systems, or do biologically-inspired inductive biases require explicit design?
3. **Does the benefit of alignment transfer across tasks or domains?** If a model is trained with auditory-aligned receptive fields, does it transfer better to speech-related tasks?
4. **Can structured receptive fields improve robustness?** Do aligned receptive fields confer adversarial robustness or better generalization to distribution shift?
5. **How does alignment interact with other inductive biases?** E.g., convolutional structure, skip connections, or attention mechanisms—which combinations are synergistic?
6. **Scaling to large models**: do the findings hold in transformers or large vision/language models, or is the trade-off specific to recurrent architectures?

## Key Takeaway
Structured receptive fields aligned with task geometry improve neural network accuracy, but this benefit shrinks when neurons become more expressive.
