# From Isolated Tasks to Structured Capabilities: A Multilayer Taxonomy for Large Language Models

**Authors:** Shixin Fang, Jiachen Wo, Wenjuan Qin, Sihang Jiang, Yanghua Xiao
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22182v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22182v1)

## Summary

This paper organizes LLM evaluation around capabilities rather than isolated tasks by introducing a multi-layer taxonomy of 14 capability domains and 91 subskills. The authors analyzed over 15,000 recent LLM papers and mapped them to this taxonomy, revealing significant research gaps and dominance patterns—for example, language and reasoning skills receive 43% of research attention while theory of mind and social reasoning get less than 2%. This shift from task-centric to capability-centric thinking makes it easier to identify what LLMs can actually do, compare studies across the field, and spot where evaluation is sparse.

## Theoretical Background

LLM evaluation traditionally organizes knowledge around benchmarks and tasks—datasets designed to test specific abilities. However, this creates fragmentation: the same underlying capability may be tested by many different benchmarks, and it's unclear whether progress on one task transfers to another. The paper draws on human cognitive science (developmental psychology, cognitive ability frameworks) to structure capabilities hierarchically. The taxonomy uses three layers based on developmental precedence: Primitive skills (foundational—like language understanding), Constructed skills (built from primitives—like reasoning about sequences), and Integrative skills (combining multiple capabilities—like planning complex multi-step actions). This echoes frameworks like Bloom's taxonomy in education, adapted here for observable model behavior rather than human performance.

## Core Contributions

1. **Multi-layer taxonomy design**: 14 domains × 91 subskills organized by cognitive precedence (Primitive → Constructed → Integrative), grounded in cognitive science rather than LLM architecture alone.
2. **Large-scale empirical mapping**: Systematic annotation and analysis of 15,934 papers from 2023–2025, revealing which capabilities dominate research and which are neglected.
3. **Coverage asymmetry quantification**: Explicit evidence that Language-Semantic Competence and Reasoning account for 43.6% of focus, while six domains appear in <2% of papers; median within-domain imbalance shows one subskill receives 97.9% of attention.
4. **Co-occurrence patterns**: Identification of high-lift skill pairs (e.g., Theory of Mind + Social Reasoning appear together 30.84× more often than random).
5. **Operational framework**: A tool for audit, diagnosis, and research planning that shifts from task-centric to capability-centric analysis.

## Connections to Other Work

This work complements existing evaluation frameworks like [[SuperGLUE]], [[MMLU]], and [[BIG-Bench]] by providing a meta-level organizational schema. It relates to capability emergence studies (e.g., scaling laws research) by grounding "capabilities" as a formal construct. The hierarchical layer design parallels work on [[transfer learning]] and [[few-shot learning]]—understanding what skills support what other skills. The paper also bridges [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (understanding model internals) and evaluation methodology by suggesting that capability-aware evaluation may reveal more about model structure than task-specific benchmarks. It's upstream of work on [[alignment]] and model auditing, as capability taxonomies enable systematic assessment of safety-relevant skills (e.g., social reasoning, theory of mind).

## Practical Applications

1. **Benchmark design & validation**: Researchers building new LLM benchmarks can explicitly target underrepresented capabilities, ensuring balanced evaluation suites rather than redundant coverage.
2. **Model selection & procurement**: Organizations can audit LLMs against a structured capability profile, matching model strengths to real-world needs (e.g., which models excel at planning vs. semantic understanding).
3. **Curriculum & training planning**: Teams fine-tuning or pre-training LLMs can use the taxonomy to design multi-stage training that builds primitive skills before integrative ones, improving sample efficiency.
4. **Diagnosis of failure modes**: When an LLM fails at a task, the taxonomy enables root-cause analysis—is the problem primitive language competence, reasoning, or integration of multiple skills?
5. **Cross-domain transfer studies**: The hierarchy suggests which capabilities should transfer (e.g., will strong reasoning help planning?) and enables controlled experiments to test these hypotheses.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **LLM Capability Audit Platform**: SaaS tool for enterprises to evaluate off-the-shelf or proprietary LLMs against the taxonomy, generating a standardized capability scorecard and identifying gaps for fine-tuning or model selection.
2. **Benchmark-as-a-Service with Structured Coverage**: Managed evaluation suite that dynamically generates tests targeting underrepresented subskills, ensuring balanced coverage and flagging emerging blind spots in LLM development.
3. **Capability-Aligned Fine-Tuning Service**: Automated training pipeline that accepts a target capability profile, maps existing datasets to the taxonomy, and recommends synthetic data generation or curricula to strengthen weak domains.
4. **Research Funding/Grant Allocation Tool**: Analyze research proposals against the taxonomy to identify which capability gaps are ripe for investment, helping funding bodies allocate resources strategically.
5. **LLM Chatbot/Agent Quality Assurance**: Continuous monitoring system that maps real-world user interactions to capability subskills, alerting teams when the model underperforms in specific domains (e.g., social reasoning) and triggering retraining or model rotation.

## Technical Limitations

1. **Human cognitive science fit**: The taxonomy is anchored to human cognition, but LLMs may recruit capabilities in fundamentally different ways or possess emergent abilities not present in humans—the forced alignment may obscure unique model behavior.
2. **Annotation scalability & consistency**: Mapping 15,934 papers required multi-model consensus and arbitration; the process doesn't clearly scale to newer papers, and inter-annotator agreement metrics are not reported (reliability unknown).
3. **Subskill granularity**: 91 subskills is large; within-domain imbalance (one subskill at 97.9% prevalence) suggests either over-definition or severe measurement gaps—refinement needed to avoid rendering the taxonomy noisy.
4. **Task-to-capability inference**: Assigning a benchmark to a capability domain assumes clear mapping, but many tasks recruit multiple capabilities simultaneously; the paper doesn't quantify multi-capability co-recruitment within single datasets.
5. **Temporal dynamics**: Analysis spans 2023–2025 only; no diachronic view of how research attention to capabilities evolves, limiting predictive power for future work.
6. **Generalization beyond English NLP**: Taxonomy derived from English-language papers and models; applicability to multilingual or non-language modalities (vision-language, audio) unclear.

## Open Questions

1. **Transfer learning within the taxonomy**: Do models strong in Primitive capabilities reliably excel at Constructed and Integrative ones? Can this be measured quantitatively, and what are the exceptions?
2. **Optimal capability balance for real applications**: What is the ideal profile of capabilities for specific use cases (e.g., customer support vs. code generation)? Can you predict task performance from capability strengths?
3. **Emergence of new capabilities**: As LLMs scale, do genuinely new capabilities emerge that fall outside the current 14 domains, or do all gains concentrate in existing subskills?
4. **Causal role of capabilities in performance**: Does training explicitly on underrepresented subskills (e.g., theory of mind) improve downstream task performance, or is the taxonomy merely descriptive?
5. **Cross-model variation**: How stable is the taxonomy across different model architectures, sizes, and training methods? Do open-source and proprietary models cluster differently?
6. **Socio-technical dynamics**: Why do certain capabilities receive disproportionate attention? Is it driven by publication incentives, benchmark availability, or genuine utility? Can taxonomy visibility shift research attention?

## Key Takeaway

LLM research is fragmented by tasks; organizing around shared capabilities reveals stark research gaps and enables systematic evaluation.
