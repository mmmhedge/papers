# Instruction-Tuned Models Locally Reuse Human Syntax More Than Humans Do

**Authors:** Zandi Eberstadt
**Published:** 2026-07-28 | **arXiv:** [2607.26015v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.26015v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether instruction-tuned large language models exhibit syntactic convergence—the tendency to match the grammatical patterns of their conversation partner—similar to humans. Using a substitution-paradigm approach on 16 Llama and Gemma models, the authors find that instruction-tuned models actually *over-reuse* syntax from preceding human turns compared to human baselines, though they paradoxically show weaker convergence to actual context versus random primes after instruction tuning.

## Theoretical Background
Syntactic convergence (also called linguistic accommodation) is a well-established phenomenon in psycholinguistics and sociolinguistics—humans unconsciously adapt their grammar, word choice, and phonology toward their conversation partners. This adaptation operates largely below conscious awareness and is studied using substitution paradigms, where researchers replace one speaker's turn in real dialogues with model generations and measure overlap in formal linguistic features. The paper uses context-free grammar (CFG) rules—a formal notation for hierarchical sentence structure—as a quantitative lens for measuring syntactic overlap. Prior work on neural language models' linguistic properties exists, but the specific question of whether instruction-tuning (a post-pretraining alignment step using human feedback) changes convergence behavior is largely unexplored.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical breadth**: First systematic measurement of syntactic convergence across 16 models at scale (1,901 matched dialogue positions per model), spanning multiple sizes and families.
2. **Instruction-tuning paradox**: The key finding—instruction-tuned models show *greater* absolute syntactic overlap with preceding turns than humans, yet *weaker* actual-versus-random differentiation (smaller increment) and lower conditional rule-reuse odds when controlling for rule-set size. This suggests instruction-tuning amplifies mimicry of unrelated primes, not selective convergence.
3. **Multi-level analysis**: Extends beyond syntax to lexical and semantic similarity, revealing instruction-tuned models exhibit higher semantic similarity overall but more heterogeneous lexical patterns.
4. **Mechanistic insight**: Demonstrates that instruction-tuning doesn't simply "make models more human-like" in dialogue behavior; it shifts the pattern of reuse in ways that diverge from human pragmatic selectivity.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader efforts in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] to understand how alignment procedures (like [[RLHF]]) change model behavior beyond the intended surface goals. It connects to dialogue understanding and conversational AI literature, which often assumes models should mimic human pragmatics. The paper implicitly challenges the assumption that "more human-like" outputs (higher semantic similarity) automatically mean more appropriate dialogue behavior. It also touches on the debate about whether neural models learn genuine linguistic structure or statistical patterns—the CFG analysis provides evidence that models do learn formal syntactic rules, but their application diverges from human pragmatic intuitions.

## Practical Applications
1. **Dialogue system evaluation**: Better metrics for assessing whether conversational AI systems exhibit appropriate (not excessive) linguistic accommodation, reducing uncanny or overly-mirroring behavior.
2. **Chatbot fine-tuning**: Understanding which instruction-tuning recipes lead to more natural dialogue—avoiding excessive syntactic mimicry that can feel manipulative or inauthentic.
3. **Accessibility in conversational interfaces**: Calibrating how much models should adapt to user syntax (important for non-native speakers or those with atypical language patterns) versus maintaining consistency.
4. **Linguistic analysis tools**: Using model-based substitution paradigms to study human dialogue corpora where speaker roles are incomplete or ambiguous.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Dialogue Quality Auditor**: SaaS tool that analyzes customer support chatbot transcripts to flag excessive or inappropriate linguistic mimicry that could harm user trust or seem manipulative.
2. **Instruction-Tuning Optimizer**: Service that tests instruction-tuning recipes on curated dialogue datasets to identify which variants produce the most "naturally human" (i.e., pragmatically selective) convergence.
3. **Language Accommodation Profiler**: API that measures a user's linguistic style and recommends model configurations or prompts that achieve appropriate (not excessive) accommodation for accessibility or engagement goals.
4. **Conversational AI Compliance Tool**: Platform for enterprises to audit whether their LLM-driven systems exhibit unwanted linguistic manipulation patterns (e.g., overly mirroring customer syntax to build false rapport).
5. **Dialogue Corpus Researcher**: Cloud platform that uses model substitution paradigms to automatically analyze large corpora of human dialogue, extracting fine-grained syntactic and semantic patterns.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Substitution paradigm assumption**: Replacing human turns with model generations assumes the model's context (the preceding turn) is identical, but real dialogue flow changes with model output. The substitution paradigm measures potential convergence, not actual in-context behavior.
2. **CFG as grammar measure**: Context-free grammar is a simplification of natural language structure. It may miss important syntactic nuances (long-range dependencies, pragmatic structures) that models and humans actually track.
3. **Human baseline confound**: The matched human responses come from original dialogues, so human participants saw different preceding context depending on the conversation flow. Direct comparison may not isolate pure accommodation effects.
4. **Generalization across domains**: All data appear to be conversational; unclear how findings extend to other genres (technical writing, formal instruction, etc.) where syntactic accommodation norms differ.
5. **Causal interpretation**: The paper shows correlations between instruction-tuning and convergence patterns, but doesn't isolate which aspect of instruction-tuning (RLHF, supervised fine-tuning, etc.) drives the effect.
6. **Semantic similarity metrics**: Lexical and semantic overlap are measured post-hoc; the causal relationship between semantic adaptation and syntactic behavior is not established.

## Open Questions
1. **Which instruction-tuning component matters?** Do supervised fine-tuning, RLHF, or specific reward signal choices drive the convergence change? Ablation studies needed.
2. **Why over-convergence?** Is excessive rule-reuse with unrelated primes a side effect of instruction-tuning's optimization landscape, or does it serve some emergent goal (e.g., perceived politeness or engagement)?
3. **In-context dialogue behavior**: How do these findings change when models generate consecutive turns in a real back-and-forth (not just substitution)? Does the accumulated context change the pattern?
4. **Human-model asymmetry**: Why do humans show selective convergence (higher actual-versus-random gap) while instruction-tuned models don't? Is this a fundamental difference in how LLMs process context versus human dialogue pragmatics?
5. **Cross-lingual effects**: Do these patterns hold in non-English languages, or are they artifacts of English syntactic structure or training data characteristics?
6. **Downstream impact**: Does excessive syntactic mimicry actually harm user satisfaction, trust, or task success in real dialogue? User studies needed.

## Key Takeaway
Instruction-tuned models mimic human syntax more indiscriminately than humans do, overriding the selective, contextual accommodation that makes human dialogue pragmatically effective.
