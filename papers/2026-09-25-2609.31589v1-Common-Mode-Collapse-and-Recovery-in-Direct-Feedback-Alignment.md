# Common-Mode Collapse and Recovery in Direct Feedback Alignment

**Authors:** Varun Reddy, Bernardo L. Sabatini, Houman Safaai
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31589v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31589v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies and explains a training failure in direct feedback alignment (DFA) — a biologically plausible learning algorithm — where networks get stuck predicting constant outputs. The authors trace this "common-mode collapse" to a mathematical property of how error signals propagate through fixed random feedback connections, then show how simple interventions like adjusting the readout baseline or subtracting batch-mean errors can recover learning. The work combines theory, simulation, and mechanistic analysis to understand a subtle but widespread failure mode.

## Theoretical Background
Direct feedback alignment is an alternative to backpropagation that uses fixed random projections to send error information to hidden layers, making it more biologically plausible since it doesn't require weight symmetry or backpropagation. This paper builds on prior DFA work by applying mean-covariance decomposition — a technique from linear algebra that separates signals into their averaged component and fluctuating residual — to understand training dynamics. The authors analyze how tanh activations and sigmoid readout layers interact with stochastic gradient descent, showing that when error signals contain a strong "common mode" (a component constant across all inputs), hidden units can saturate and learning stalls. The reduced model they develop is a dimensionality-reduction technique: it predicts which activations will concentrate sensitivity without fitting parameters, essentially distilling the network's behavior to its essential dynamics.

## Core Contributions
1. **Identification and mechanistic explanation** of common-mode collapse in DFA: the paper proves that a rank-one component of the error (the mean teaching signal ⊗ mean presynaptic activity) drives hidden units toward saturation, and random feedback provides no systematic correction on average.
2. **Exact mean-covariance decomposition** that separates update rules into interpretable rank-one and higher-rank components, providing an analytical lens for understanding DFA dynamics.
3. **Reduced model without fitted parameters** that accurately predicts activation sensitivity concentration across 48 experimental settings, demonstrating that collapse follows from network structure rather than random variation.
4. **Practical interventions** (calibrating readout baseline to class prior, subtracting batch-mean errors, using adaptive optimizers like Adam) that suppress collapse and accelerate learning, validated on MNIST and CIFAR-10.
5. **Generalization analysis** showing the phenomenon persists in deeper and convolutional architectures, linking severity to readout choice, optimizer type, and input statistics.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper advances [[mechanistic-interpretability]] of learning algorithms by providing a rigorous explanation of a training failure, similar to how prior work analyzes loss landscape geometry or mode connectivity. It relates to broader questions about why [[reinforcement-learning]] and biologically plausible algorithms (like those used in neuroscience models) train differently than backpropagation. The mean-covariance decomposition echoes techniques used in understanding neural collapse in supervised learning and feature learning dynamics. The paper's interventions (e.g., adaptive optimization, batch normalization-like preprocessing) connect to a large literature on optimization tricks that empirically improve learning without clear theory — here the theory illuminates why they work in this setting.

## Practical Applications
1. **Neuromorphic computing**: improving training algorithms for brain-inspired hardware that cannot implement backpropagation, where DFA is a natural choice.
2. **Spiking neural networks**: DFA is commonly used in spiking networks (event-driven, biologically realistic); this work provides diagnostics to prevent training collapse.
3. **Federated or private learning**: DFA-like algorithms avoid storing full weight matrices; understanding and fixing collapse modes could enable efficient private training.
4. **Robotics with local learning rules**: embodied agents using biologically plausible learning; the identified interventions could speed up on-board learning in resource-constrained systems.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scope to specific architectures**: analysis focuses on tanh + sigmoid; behavior with ReLU, batch norm, or other modern components is not fully characterized, limiting generalizability.
2. **Small-scale experiments**: MNIST and CIFAR-10 are relatively simple; collapse severity and intervention effectiveness on larger, high-dimensional datasets (ImageNet, language tasks) remain unclear.
3. **Single-layer or shallow networks emphasized**: while deeper networks are mentioned, the theoretical analysis centers on 2-3 layer cases; scaling to very deep networks may reveal new failure modes or different collapse signatures.
4. **Fixed random feedback assumption**: the paper assumes DFA's random projections are truly fixed and truly random; structured or learned feedback (which some recent work explores) may exhibit different collapse behavior.
5. **No comparison to other biologically plausible algorithms**: DFA is compared to standard backprop and Adam, but not to other bio-plausible schemes (e.g., feedback alignment variants, local learning rules), so it's unclear if common-mode collapse is unique to DFA or a general phenomenon.
6. **Initialization-dependent**: the reduced model's accuracy and interventions' effectiveness may depend heavily on initialization schemes, which are not systematically explored.

## Open Questions
1. **Does common-mode collapse occur in other biologically plausible or federated learning algorithms?** If so, is there a unifying geometric or information-theoretic principle?
2. **Can the reduced model be extended to non-tanh activations and modern components** (ReLU, layer norm, residual connections) to predict collapse in contemporary architectures?
3. **How do network width and depth trade off in determining collapse severity?** Is there a scaling law predicting critical regime size or collapse timescale?
4. **Can collapse be provably prevented or avoided with architecture design** (e.g., symmetric weight structure, specific feedback kernel properties), or is intervention during training necessary?
5. **What is the relationship between common-mode collapse and generalization?** The paper notes class decodability survives collapse; does this mean collapsed networks still learn useful representations, or do they memorize?
6. **Can adaptive variants of DFA (learning the feedback matrix or its structure) avoid collapse more naturally**, and what is the biological plausibility cost?

## Key Takeaway
Fixed-feedback learning algorithms can get stuck when error signals share a common component; simple preprocessing and tuning fixes it.
