# Communicating Credit Risk with Large Language Models: Evaluation of Explanations from Standard and Alternative Data-Based Models

**Authors:** Sahab Zandi, Noah Kostesku, Christophe Mues, María Óskarsdóttir, Cristián Bravo
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.17715v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.17715v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models (LLMs) can translate technical model explanations into plain-English narratives for credit risk decisions. Using real mortgage data, the authors compare three explanation pipelines (standard tabular, network-based, and hybrid) with three LLM variants, finding that the quality of input evidence matters more than which LLM is used, but that LLMs struggle reliably to communicate the *direction* of risk factors—a critical gap for regulatory compliance.

## Theoretical Background
Credit risk modeling has traditionally relied on logistic regression and tree-based models (like XGBoost) because they're interpretable and regulators understand them. Modern approaches trade interpretability for accuracy: Graph Neural Networks (GNNs — neural networks that learn patterns from network-structured data like loan applicant networks) and ensemble methods push predictive power higher, but their decisions become opaque. Post-hoc explanation methods like SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations — a game-theory-based approach to measure each feature's contribution to a prediction) help, but their output is still technical: lists of numbers and coefficients. LLMs have shown promise at translating technical content into natural language, but credit decisions trigger strict regulatory oversight (adverse-action laws require lenders to explain denials in comprehensible terms). This paper asks: can LLMs bridge the gap between model sophistication and regulatory communication requirements?

## Core Contributions
1. **Three-pipeline comparison**: Operationalizes alternative data (network effects via GNNs, bimodal tabular+network fusion) alongside standard approaches, showing how evidence *representation* shapes explanation quality downstream.
2. **LLM-as-explainer layer**: Demonstrates that small fine-tuned models (Gemma 3 4B) perform comparably to large models (DeepSeek R1 70B) and commercial zero-shot LLMs (Gemini 2.5) when bounded by evidence quality—a practical cost-reduction finding.
3. **Direction-of-influence gap**: Identifies a systematic failure mode: LLMs reliably name influential factors but unreliably convey whether those factors increase or decrease risk, undermining regulatory sufficiency.
4. **Human-expert benchmarking**: First empirical study comparing credit professionals' and non-professionals' standards for explanation quality, revealing stricter evidentiary demands from domain experts.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Explainable AI]] and [[AI for Finance]]. It relates to prior work on [[SHAP]] and other post-hoc methods, but reframes the explanation problem: the bottleneck isn't explainability algorithms, it's *communication*. The finding that evidence representation dominates model choice parallels results in mechanistic interpretability suggesting that the quality of intermediate representations constrains downstream behavior. The regulatory framing (adverse-action compliance) echoes concerns in [[Algorithmic Fairness]] about high-stakes decisions. The comparison of fine-tuned vs. zero-shot LLMs aligns with recent observations that domain-specific fine-tuning may be unnecessary when evidence is well-structured, but contradicts the assumption that larger models always outperform smaller ones in safety-critical settings.

## Practical Applications
1. **Mortgage/auto lending platforms**: Automatic generation of adverse-action notices (regulatory requirement when a loan is denied) that communicate risk factors in borrower-friendly language, reducing legal exposure and customer complaints.
2. **Credit union compliance teams**: Explainer-as-a-service to audit loan decisions post-hoc, flagging when narratives mismatch model outputs—catching errors before they trigger lawsuits.
3. **Risk officer dashboards**: Real-time narrative summaries of portfolio concentrations (e.g., "15% of loans have elevated unemployment risk in this zip code") to support board reporting and stress-testing.
4. **Fair lending monitoring**: Systematic generation of explanations for denied applications enables auditors to detect disparate-impact patterns (e.g., "female applicants are denied for different reasons than male applicants with similar profiles").

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"ExplainCredit"**: SaaS platform wrapping legacy credit models (FICO, bank scorecard) with LLM explainer layers, generating regulatory-compliant adverse-action letters on-demand; sell to mid-sized lenders without in-house AI teams.
2. **"RiskNarrative for Enterprise"**: Real-time narrative dashboards for credit portfolio management, translating GNN-based concentration risk into Board-ready summaries; target insurance companies and asset managers facing climate/geopolitical concentration stress.
3. **"FairLend Audit"**: Compliance tool that auto-generates explanations for every denied loan and flag suspicious patterns (e.g., direction-of-influence inconsistencies that might signal bias); sell to regulators and large issuers for internal audit.
4. **"Explainer-as-Code"**: Open-source/hosted inference service (fine-tuned Gemma 3B) specifically for credit, trained on regulatory guidance; license to fintechs as a drop-in layer between their model and user interface.
5. **"Credit NLP for Compliance"**: Consulting service: audit a lender's current explanations (manual or auto-generated), re-train a domain-aligned LLM on their loan book + compliance history, deploy fine-tuned model in-house to reduce regulatory risk.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Direction-of-influence failure**: The paper identifies that LLMs reliably fail to communicate risk direction. The fix (better prompting, stronger fine-tuning signals) is not solved here; this is a hard blocker for regulated deployment.
2. **Freddie Mac data only**: Results come from a single dataset of single-family mortgages. Generalization to auto loans, credit cards, or emerging geographies is untested.
3. **Limited scale of human study**: The study compares professionals and non-professionals but doesn't specify sample size, inter-rater agreement, or potential selection bias in recruitment. Statistical power for some findings may be weak.
4. **Explanations as post-hoc only**: The paper treats LLM narrative generation as a layer *after* the model decision. It doesn't explore whether LLMs could influence the decision itself (e.g., through in-context learning) or be used *during* training to constrain model behavior.
5. **No adversarial robustness**: Does the LLM's narrative break down under distribution shift (e.g., a recession, new lending guidelines)? No sensitivity analysis is provided.
6. **Regulatory gap unclear**: The paper identifies that professionals demand higher evidentiary standards but doesn't specify what those standards are or whether they align with formal regulatory requirements (e.g., Regulation Z, Fair Credit Reporting Act).

## Open Questions
1. How can LLMs reliably communicate the direction of influence? Is this a prompt-engineering problem, a fine-tuning architecture problem, or fundamentally harder?
2. Does the "evidence dominates model choice" finding hold across other high-stakes domains (healthcare, hiring, insurance)? What properties of evidence make this true?
3. Can a single fine-tuned LLM (Gemma 3B) be trained on *multiple* lending products (mortgages, auto, credit card) without degradation, or must each product have its own explainer?
4. What happens when the model's prediction is correct but unintuitive (e.g., a borrower denied because of a rare interaction between variables)? Can LLMs generate *persuasive* explanations that don't oversimplify?
5. How do narratives perform in an adversarial setting—e.g., when a denied applicant's attorney challenges the explanation in court? Which aspects of the paper's explanation quality metrics predict legal defensibility?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs can explain credit decisions clearly, but only if the underlying model evidence is structured well—and they still struggle to communicate whether risk factors increase or decrease the denial probability.
