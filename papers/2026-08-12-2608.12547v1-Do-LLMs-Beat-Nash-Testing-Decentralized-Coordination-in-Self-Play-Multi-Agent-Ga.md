# Do LLMs Beat Nash? Testing Decentralized Coordination in Self-Play Multi-Agent Games

**Authors:** Deborah Sinishaw, Qile Zhu, Edwin Meriaux, Gregory Dudek
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12547v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12547v1)

## Summary
This paper tests whether large language models can coordinate with each other in games without communication, asking if they can reason about their counterparts well enough to beat the Nash equilibrium — the standard game-theory baseline for uncoordinated play. The researchers find that frontier models sometimes exceed Nash equilibrium outcomes in two-player games but struggle in larger multi-agent teams, suggesting LLMs have limited ability to scale coordination reasoning beyond simple settings.

## Theoretical Background
Game theory provides the conceptual foundation: a Nash equilibrium is a strategy profile where no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing their action, given others' choices. In one-shot games without communication, players cannot coordinate explicitly and must instead reason about what others will do based on symmetry, salience, or shared rationality. The paper treats independent LLM instances as agents playing these games and measures whether their implicit reasoning surpasses Nash performance. Prior work on LLMs as agents (agents playing structured tasks) and multi-agent systems (systems where multiple actors interact) suggests language models might encode strategic reasoning, but this benchmark directly tests that hypothesis under zero-communication constraints.

## Core Contributions
1. **Benchmark Design**: A systematic evaluation framework testing 13 LLMs across seven game archetypes (coordination, anti-coordination, mixed-motive, etc.) in two-player matrix games with 2–10 actions per player.
2. **Empirical Finding on Frontier Models**: Two frontier-hosted models (GPT-4 class) consistently exceed Nash equilibrium, sometimes approaching Pareto-optimal (joint-best) outcomes, demonstrating genuine coordination reasoning without explicit communication.
3. **Scaling Failure Diagnosis**: Open-weight models show inconsistent, structure-dependent gains; performance degrades sharply in four+ agent team games, suggesting coordination ability does not generalize beyond dyadic (two-player) settings.
4. **Mechanistic Insight**: The results imply LLMs leverage implicit reasoning about symmetry and shared rationality in small games but lack abstractions to handle larger coalition problems.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[large-language-models]] as agents, [[multi-agent-systems]] coordination, and game-theoretic reasoning. It relates to prior studies of LLM reasoning in logic puzzles and strategic scenarios (e.g., Sudoku, code generation under constraints), but applies that reasoning to a decentralized, zero-communication setting. It echoes questions in [[reinforcement-learning]] about emergent coordination in multi-agent RL, though here the "learning" is implicit in the model's weights, not explicit gradient updates. The Nash equilibrium framing connects to mechanism design and social choice theory, which ask how individual incentives aggregate into collective outcomes.

## Practical Applications
1. **Autonomous Vehicle Fleets**: Coordination at intersections or merges where communication is unreliable; LLMs could help vehicles predict each other's behavior and avoid collisions without explicit messaging.
2. **Decentralized Robotics Teams**: Swarms or teams (search-and-rescue, warehouse logistics) operating in degraded-communication environments, relying on shared reasoning to avoid conflicts and inefficiency.
3. **Negotiation and Auction Systems**: Automated agents bidding or haggling without pre-coordination; LLMs could reason about opponent preferences to reach efficient (near-optimal) deals.
4. **Financial Market Simulation**: Testing whether LLM traders can find mutually beneficial trades or avoid herding without real-time information sharing.
5. **Supply Chain Coordination**: Independent suppliers and manufacturers inferring each other's actions (production, pricing, inventory) to reduce waste and deadlocks.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scaling Failure**: The paper shows performance degrades in four+ agent teams; the underlying mechanism is unclear, limiting applicability to realistic multi-party systems.
2. **One-Shot Games Only**: Real-world coordination often involves repeated interaction, learning, and reputation effects; this benchmark captures only instantaneous reasoning.
3. **No Communication Channel Tested**: The paper assumes zero communication by design. In practice, adding even a cheap-talk (non-binding messaging) phase might qualitatively change outcomes, and the paper does not explore this relaxation.
4. **Matrix Games Only**: Results may not transfer to continuous action spaces, asymmetric games, or settings with incomplete information (players unsure of payoffs).
5. **Model Heterogeneity**: All frontier models tested are transformers; results may not generalize to other architectures. Open-weight models show high variance, but the paper does not deeply diagnose why (prompt sensitivity, training data, model size).
6. **No Interpretability**: The paper does not explain *how* LLMs reason about Nash equilibrium or symmetry; understanding might unlock better scaling.

## Open Questions
1. **Mechanism of Coordination**: What activations, attention patterns, or learned features enable frontier models to exceed Nash? Can mechanistic interpretability (probing model internals) identify the coordination reasoning?
2. **Scaling to N Agents**: What architectural or fine-tuning changes allow coordination reasoning to scale beyond two players? Is the problem fundamentally about abstract coalition reasoning?
3. **Repeated Games**: How do LLMs perform in iterated games where history is visible and reputation matters? Does explicit memory or reinforcement learning from rollouts help?
4. **Cheap Talk**: If agents can send costless messages before playing, do LLMs use them effectively to coordinate? How does this compare to no-communication baselines?
5. **Asymmetric Games**: How do LLMs handle games where players have different payoffs or roles (e.g., one player is a "leader" with first-mover advantage)? Does the symmetry assumption break?
6. **Training Data Confounds**: Are frontier models' Nash-beating gains due to implicit game-theory knowledge in training data, or genuine reasoning? Pre-training on strategy guides or game theory texts?

## Key Takeaway
Large language models can reason about each other to beat Nash equilibrium in two-player games without talking, but fail to scale this reasoning to larger teams.
