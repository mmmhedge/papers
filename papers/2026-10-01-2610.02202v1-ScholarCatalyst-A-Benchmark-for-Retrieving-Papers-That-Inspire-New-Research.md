# ScholarCatalyst: A Benchmark for Retrieving Papers That Inspire New Research

**Authors:** Sohyeon Kim, Yoonho Lee, Bo Liu, Dayoon Ko, Rulin Shao et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02202v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02202v1)

## Summary
ScholarCatalyst is a benchmark dataset where 184 computer scientists annotated which prior papers inspired their recent research projects, creating a retrieval task: given a research question, find the papers that could advance it using only literature available at project start. The paper shows that current retrieval methods—including agentic search using Claude and embedding-based systems—perform poorly (R@20 ≈ 0.42–0.51), suggesting that AI systems lack the "expert intuition" scientists use to spot relevant prior work in large research archives.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two foundational areas. First, information retrieval research has long studied how to rank documents by relevance; traditional approaches use keyword matching or learned embeddings (dense vector representations that encode document meaning). Second, recent work on agentic AI systems—autonomous agents that use tools like search and reasoning iteratively—suggests that giving language models access to external tools might improve their ability to solve complex tasks. The paper implicitly draws on research in [[scientific knowledge graphs]] and [[literature-based discovery]], which examine how to surface meaningful connections across research domains. The benchmark framework mirrors established IR evaluation methods like TREC, where human judgments create ground truth for assessing retrieval quality.

## Core Contributions
1. **ScholarCatalyst dataset**: A new benchmark with 207 papers, each annotated by its lead author with rationales explaining which prior works inspired the project. This is the first large-scale benchmark that captures *author-validated* inspiration links rather than citation proximity alone.
2. **Temporal realism**: The task enforces that only literature available at project start can be retrieved, avoiding the common pitfall of evaluating on papers published after the research began.
3. **Agentic retrieval evaluation**: The paper systematically compares embedding-based retrieval, agentic search, and even in-distribution agents (Claude 3.5 Sonnet, which may have seen completed papers), revealing that agency and model scale do not translate to strong performance on this task.
4. **Evidence of a capability gap**: Results show current models lack the intuition to navigate broad literature corpora for inspirational relevance—a task expert scientists perform naturally.

## Connections to Other Work
ScholarCatalyst relates to several research directions:
- **[[Citation Recommendation]]** systems, which predict citations but typically rank only recently-published papers rather than capturing inspirational influence.
- **[[Semantic Scholar]]** and similar scientific search tools, which use embeddings and keyword search but lack explicit supervision on what makes a paper useful for a given research goal.
- **[[Agentic AI systems]]** and tool use—the paper shows that agentic architectures alone do not solve sparse-relevance retrieval, suggesting that [[RLHF]] or other training methods need domain-specific adaptation.
- **[[Scientific reasoning]]** in AI, related to work on mathematical problem-solving and theorem-proving, where models must search a large space of possible ideas.
- **Information needs in science**, studied in HCI/CSCW literature on how scientists discover and contextualize prior work.

## Practical Applications
1. **AI research assistant for early-stage ideation**: A tool that, given a research proposal outline, surfaces papers that could strengthen or inspire the project—directly helping PhD students and new researchers.
2. **Literature review acceleration**: Automatically generate comprehensive background sections by retrieving inspirational papers in a researcher's domain, reducing time spent in initial research discovery.
3. **Grant proposal writing**: Suggest prior work to cite and build upon when drafting grant applications, improving competitiveness by ensuring proposals are well-grounded.
4. **Cross-disciplinary innovation**: Help researchers outside a domain discover foundational papers that could inspire novel approaches (e.g., pointing an ML researcher to biology papers relevant to their problem).
5. **Journal editor support**: Assist editors in identifying potentially overlooked prior work when reviewing novel submissions, improving editorial decision-making.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ResearchMuse** — A subscription IDE for researchers that watches their project notes/documents and proactively recommends papers that could strengthen their work, powered by ScholarCatalyst-trained retrieval models.
2. **FoundationMap** — A marketplace where senior researchers or domain experts manually curate "inspiration maps" (structured collections of papers that led to breakthroughs) that junior researchers can search and learn from.
3. **Grant Intelligence** — B2B SaaS for research institutions: automatically suggest relevant citations and prior work for grant proposals, improving success rates and ensuring compliance with reviewers' expectations.
4. **PaperOrigins** — A browser extension that, when a researcher reads a paper, shows papers that likely inspired it (via reverse-engineering of citations + ScholarCatalyst-style linking), surfacing hidden intellectual ancestry.
5. **Scientific Serendipity Engine** — A serendipitous discovery tool that bridges distant research communities by retrieving papers from outside a researcher's typical domain that share inspirational patterns with their work.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scale and bias**: Only 207 papers and 184 authors from computer science—unclear if findings generalize to other fields (biology, physics, engineering) with different citation and inspiration practices.
2. **Annotation quality & coverage**: Author rationales are subjective; papers marked as not inspirational may actually have had indirect influence. Single-author labeling (no inter-rater agreement reported) limits robustness.
3. **Temporal leakage risk**: Even "recent" papers may have overlapping training data with large models; true out-of-distribution evaluation requires fresh data or models trained on cutoff-locked corpora.
4. **Shallow retrieval baseline**: The paper evaluates standard embedding and agentic search but does not explore hybrid methods (e.g., combining keyword search, citation graphs, and learned ranking), leaving room for simpler baselines to improve performance.
5. **Causality vs. correlation**: Author annotations show which papers *could* have inspired a project, but cannot distinguish causation—a paper might be inspirational in hindsight while the actual research path was independent.
6. **Query formulation**: The task assumes a clear "initial research question," but real ideation is messier; the paper does not address how well models perform with vague or evolving problem statements.

## Open Questions
1. **Cross-domain generalization**: Does a retrieval model trained on ScholarCatalyst generalize to biology, physics, or engineering papers? How do domain-specific citation patterns affect performance?
2. **Fine-tuning efficiency**: What is the minimum amount of author-annotated data needed to train a strong retrieval model? Can transfer learning or few-shot learning help?
3. **Explainability & trust**: Can retrieval models provide rationales (similar to author annotations) for why a paper is inspirational? Would explanations improve usability for researchers?
4. **Graph-aware retrieval**: How much does incorporating citation graphs, co-author networks, or semantic similarity improve performance compared to text-only embeddings?
5. **Iterative refinement**: Can a researcher engage in multi-turn dialogue with a retrieval agent, refining the query or feedback, to improve results over time?
6. **Inspiration taxonomy**: Are there recurring patterns in what makes a paper inspirational (e.g., methodological contribution, problem formulation, empirical result)? Can models learn to recognize these?
7. **Real-world deployment**: How do models perform in live scenarios where researchers use ScholarCatalyst-based tools during actual research projects?

## Key Takeaway
Current AI systems struggle to identify papers that inspire new research, even with agent-based search—a capability gap that requires new training approaches for scientific discovery.
