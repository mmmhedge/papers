# Microscopic dynamics of consensus formation in multi-agent LLM Naming Games

**Authors:** Cristiano De Nobili, Vijayasri Iyer, Alessandro Codello, Raffaella Burioni
**Published:** 2026-08-03 | **arXiv:** [2608.02178v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02178v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how decentralized populations of LLM agents spontaneously reach agreement on shared naming conventions, despite the randomness baked into their token generation. The authors show that a single parameter—decoding temperature—controls whether consensus happens, and they derive mathematical conditions predicting when ordering emerges from microscopic agent interactions. This matters because it connects physics-style emergent phenomena to practical LLM behavior in distributed systems.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on the **Naming Game** — a classical model from statistical mechanics and social dynamics where agents iteratively agree on labels for objects through pairwise interactions. In the deterministic version, agents succeed if a word is "in their inventory" and fail otherwise; consensus emerges reliably. Here, the authors replace the binary inventory check with a stochastic LLM token prediction at temperature $T$ (a parameter controlling randomness in language models). They then apply **mean-field theory** — a standard approximation that treats a large population as governed by average interaction rates — to derive when macroscopic ordering (consensus) emerges from microscopic randomness. The key insight is decomposing listener decisions into two conditional channels: agreement on known words (rate $π(T)$) and accidental agreement on unknown words (rate $φ(T)$). Their framework connects **decoding temperature** (a hyperparameter controlling how "random" the model's output is) to phase transitions familiar from statistical mechanics.

## Core Contributions
1. **Quantitative mapping of LLM stochasticity to consensus dynamics**: Shows how a single LLM hyperparameter (temperature) tunes the balance between two competing interaction rates, analogous to controlling an order-disorder transition in physics.

2. **Mean-field analytical condition**: Derives a critical line in the $(π, φ)$ parameter space that predicts consensus thresholds, generalizing prior work on deterministic Naming Games to the stochastic regime.

3. **Architecture-dependent listener regimes**: Identifies three distinct behavioral modes across open-weight models (permissive, near-deterministic, conservative), revealing how model capacity or training affects consensus dynamics.

4. **Temperature-sensitivity scaling laws**: Quantifies how convergence time scales with system size and temperature across architectures, showing temperature sensitivity $α$ ranges from ~0.67 to ~0, indicating architecture-dependent control.

## Practical Applications
1. **Decentralized LLM-based naming/tagging systems**: Autonomous labeling of objects across distributed systems (robotics fleets, sensor networks) where agents must converge on shared nomenclature without a central authority.

2. **Fault-tolerant multi-agent dialogue**: Swarms of LLM agents that must reach consensus on facts or interpretations in real-time without explicit synchronization protocols.

3. **Distributed knowledge base curation**: Multiple LLM instances autonomously building and reconciling a shared knowledge graph or taxonomy by local interactions.

4. **Temperature-tuned deployment of LLM swarms**: Using decoding temperature as a knob to trade off consensus speed vs. response diversity in production systems (e.g., faster agreement on safety-critical decisions by lowering temperature).

## Technical Limitations
1. **Mean-field approximation assumes infinite population**: Finite-size effects are observed empirically, but the theory only predicts the thermodynamic limit; small teams may deviate significantly.

2. **Single-token decision model is oversimplified**: Real-world consensus often requires multi-turn negotiation or reasoning; replacing it with a one-shot classifier is a strong abstraction that may not capture richer communication.

3. **Temperature as sole control parameter**: The paper doesn't explore how other LLM hyperparameters (top-k, top-p sampling, presence penalties) interact with temperature or whether they enable finer control over consensus dynamics.

4. **Limited to open-weight models**: No experiments on closed commercial models (GPT-4, Claude); results may not generalize to different architectures or training procedures.

5. **No explicit misalignment or adversarial analysis**: Paper assumes all agents are well-behaved; doesn't address Byzantine agents, poisoning, or deliberate subversion of consensus.

6. **Scalability untested**: Largest experiments are on population sizes typical of multi-agent RL; true "population of thousands" scenarios remain unexplored.

## Open Questions
1. **How do richer decision mechanisms (multi-token reasoning, chain-of-thought) change the phase diagram?** Does analytical theory still apply?

2. **Can agents learn to adaptively adjust their own temperature to accelerate or stabilize consensus?** Is there a reinforcement learning signal that would emerge naturally?

3. **What happens in heterogeneous populations** where agents have different temperatures or architectures? Does a single effective temperature emerge?

4. **How robust is consensus to Byzantine agents** (those that deliberately deviate)? What's the breakdown threshold?

5. **Can this framework predict consensus formation in real LLM multi-agent systems** (e.g., agent debate, multi-model ensembles) beyond the minimal Naming Game?

6. **Does the ordering-disordering transition have a connection to mode collapse** or diversity loss observed in large LLM populations?

## Key Takeaway
Decoding temperature controls whether decentralized LLM agents reach consensus, following universal statistical-physics principles that generalize across architectures.
