# Spatial Pattern Formation from Multi-Agent Learning in Public Goods Dilemmas

**Authors:** Yefei Zhang, Yuxuan Zhao
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.12321v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.12321v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how spatial patterns emerge when multiple agents learn movement strategies in public goods games — settings where individual incentives don't align with collective welfare. The authors show that learning rates fundamentally shape whether agents cluster, disperse, or form traveling bands, and that these learned patterns often harm overall welfare despite helping individuals find resources.

## Theoretical Background
Public goods dilemmas are game-theoretic scenarios where contributing to a shared resource benefits everyone, but individuals gain more by free-riding. In spatial versions of these games, agents occupy locations and interact locally, which can promote cooperation through spatial clustering. The paper builds on established work showing that prescribed (non-learned) movement toward rich areas can generate organized spatial structures. Here, the authors extend this by using Q-learning — a foundational reinforcement-learning algorithm where agents learn action-value functions from experience — to let agents *discover* movement policies themselves rather than follow fixed rules. Learning rates (how quickly agents update their beliefs) become a key control variable that shapes emergent behavior.

## Core Contributions
1. **Learning-rate dependence of spatial patterns**: Demonstrates that cooperator and defector learning rates independently control whether clusters form, dissipate, or reorganize into traveling bands.
2. **Welfare paradox**: Shows that when cooperators learn effectively, spatial clustering actually *reduces* collective welfare below random movement because crowding externalities exceed resource-location benefits.
3. **Training-history sensitivity**: Reveals that traveling-band patterns depend on training history rather than converging to a stable attractor — a dynamic instability absent from prescriptive models.
4. **Crowding-charge intervention**: Proposes internalizing crowding costs during learning as a practical mechanism to recover welfare losses, with empirical validation.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper bridges several literatures: spatial game theory (building on work on cooperation in spatial structures), multi-agent reinforcement learning (where agents co-adapt and create emergent dynamics), and mechanism design for externalities. It relates to broader concerns in [[Alignment]] — when individual-level optimization (each agent maximizing its own reward) diverges from group welfare — and echoes work on how learning rate schedules shape emergent behavior in systems with multiple learning agents. The finding that patterns depend on training history connects to non-stationary multi-agent learning and the instability of Q-learning under co-adaptation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Urban resource planning**: Predicting how residents naturally redistribute themselves toward amenities (jobs, parks, transit) and designing congestion pricing to offset overuse.
2. **Wildlife management**: Understanding how learned migration or grazing patterns form in multi-species populations and where to impose restrictions to preserve ecosystem welfare.
3. **Fisheries management**: Modeling how fishing vessels learn to concentrate at productive grounds and designing dynamic quota systems to prevent overharvesting.
4. **Wireless network optimization**: Agents (drones or access points) learning to position themselves toward demand hotspots while avoiding interference and congestion.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Spatial-learning simulator for urban planners**: SaaS tool that simulates how agents (residents, workers, visitors) naturally cluster under different incentive structures and recommends pricing or zoning interventions.
2. **Congestion-internalization platform**: Real-time system that charges users (vehicles, package delivery, workers) proportional fees for crowding externalities they impose, optimized via learned policies.
3. **Cooperative resource allocation for gig economy**: Marketplace that learns when and where to incentivize work (food delivery, rideshare) and applies dynamic surge pricing that accounts for crowding harm to prevent overconcentration.
4. **Ecosystem monitoring via multi-agent simulation**: Software for conservation organizations that predicts and mitigates overuse of natural sites (hiking trails, fishing zones) by modeling tourist or harvester learning.
5. **Data-center resource scheduling**: System that learns how to distribute compute jobs across locations while charging for contention externalities to minimize total latency and energy.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Tabular Q-learning scalability**: Requires discretized state spaces; breaks down in continuous or high-dimensional environments typical of realistic spatial settings.
2. **Myopic local observation**: Agents only see nearby areas; real agents often have information networks or global knowledge that change strategic reasoning.
3. **Offline learning assumption**: Training happens in isolation before deployment; co-adaptation during actual use may produce different patterns and welfare outcomes.
4. **Small, homogeneous populations**: Experiments use fixed numbers of cooperators and defectors; real populations are heterogeneous and dynamic (birth, death, entry, exit).
5. **Linear welfare aggregation**: Assumes welfare is simply resource benefit minus crowding cost; real preferences are non-linear and heterogeneous.
6. **Stationary environment**: Resource locations are fixed; realistic settings have dynamic supply and demand.

## Open Questions
1. **Scalability to continuous space**: Can the same pattern-formation mechanisms emerge with function approximation (neural networks) in continuous environments, and how sensitive are they to hyperparameters?
2. **Heterogeneous learning**: What happens when agents have different learning rates, exploration strategies, or initial beliefs? Do stratified learning-rate distributions lead to different equilibria?
3. **Dynamic populations**: How do entry/exit and heterogeneous types affect pattern stability and welfare recovery from crowding charges?
4. **Real-world validation**: Do empirical spatial games (online platforms, field experiments) show similar learning-rate dependence and welfare trade-offs?
5. **Optimal intervention design**: Can you characterize the *best* crowding-charge schedule as a function of learning rates, and does it have simple, implementable structure?
6. **Information structure**: How does agents' observational capability (local vs. global) or access to others' learned policies affect pattern formation?

## Key Takeaway
Individual learning in spatial competition creates crowding that harms collective welfare; charging for congestion during learning can recover losses.
