# Higher-Order Molecular Grammars for Generative and Foundation Models in Chemistry

**Authors:** Yiming Huang, Yujie Zeng, Vijay Prakash Dwivedi, Simone Foti, Jianmin Wang et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02186v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02186v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Higher-order Grammar Representation (HGR), a new way to represent molecules that captures their complex topology—like ring systems and recurring structural patterns—in a form that standard sequence models can process efficiently. The key innovation is converting molecules into combinatorial complexes and then serializing them as rule sequences, which maintains structural expressiveness while avoiding the computational overhead of explicit higher-order encodings. On molecular generation benchmarks, HGR-based models achieve 100% validity by construction and rank first in distributional alignment metrics; on representation learning tasks, they outperform existing baselines significantly.

## Theoretical Background
Molecular learning relies heavily on representation—how you encode a molecule shapes what a model can learn. Traditional approaches use either sequences (strings that describe molecules) or graphs (nodes for atoms, edges for bonds), but these struggle to explicitly capture higher-order topology like ring systems and recurring motifs. Prior work on higher-order representations (using techniques like combinatorial complexes—mathematical structures that generalize graphs to capture relationships beyond pairwise connections) can encode these structures directly, but they are computationally expensive and hard to decode back into valid molecules. This paper builds on ideas from formal grammars and combinatorial topology, adapting context-free grammar parsing (a technique from programming languages where rules generate valid sequences) to the domain of molecular structure.

## Core Contributions
1. **Higher-order Grammar Representation (HGR)**: A principled framework that lifts molecules into combinatorial complexes and parses them using context-free higher-order grammar rules. This preserves topological expressiveness while serializing into compact rule sequences compatible with standard sequence models (like transformers).

2. **RingDiv benchmark**: A new ring-enriched dataset of 1.18 million molecules (with a curated 300k subset) designed to reduce bias toward simple ring systems in existing benchmarks, plus a ring diversity index (RDI) metric to quantify ring-system coverage.

3. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates 100% validity by construction in molecular generation (ranking first on FCD across five benchmarks) and achieves state-of-the-art representation learning performance (8.3 AUC improvement on probing, 3.3 on fine-tuning across MoleculeNet).

## Connections to Other Work
HGR relates to prior work on higher-order graph representations and combinatorial approaches to molecular modeling, but sidesteps their computational burden via grammar-based serialization. This connects to broader efforts in [[generative-models]] for molecules (like VAEs and diffusion models) that struggle with validity constraints. The use of context-free grammars parallels work in program synthesis and code generation, where grammar-based decoding ensures structural validity. The representation learning component aligns with foundation model approaches (similar to how [[BERT]]-style pre-training works for molecules), where transferable learned representations improve downstream task performance.

## Practical Applications
- **Drug discovery**: Generating novel, valid molecular candidates with desired properties (ring-enriched scaffolds, specific functional groups) without post-hoc filtering.
- **Materials science**: Designing polymers and crystalline materials where ring systems and motifs critically determine physical properties.
- **Chemical synthesis planning**: Representing target molecules and reaction pathways in forms that aid retrosynthetic analysis and synthetic feasibility prediction.
- **Molecular property prediction**: Using HGR-FM as a foundation model for downstream tasks like toxicity prediction, activity prediction, and ADMET property estimation.
- **Patent analysis and chemical informatics**: Indexing and retrieving molecules based on their higher-order structural patterns.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Molecular design SaaS for pharma/biotech**: Web platform where chemists specify constraints (ring diversity, functional groups, property ranges) and HGR-based models generate and rank novel compounds, with built-in synthesis feasibility scoring.

2. **AI-powered fragment library optimization**: Tool for chemical suppliers and CROs to automatically identify and expand their fragment/scaffold libraries using HGR to find underrepresented high-diversity regions.

## Technical Limitations
- **Grammar design and parsability**: The context-free grammar rules must be carefully designed and validated; incorrectly specified grammars may fail to parse valid molecules or generate invalid ones. The paper doesn't deeply explore sensitivity to grammar design choices.
- **Scalability to very large molecules**: While HGR avoids explicit higher-order computations, parsing complex molecules into rule sequences still has overhead; extremely large or highly interconnected structures might face practical bottlenecks.
- **Decoding efficiency**: Converting parsed rule sequences back into valid molecules requires deterministic grammar inversion; the paper doesn't provide detailed decoding latency or discuss edge cases where inverse parsing may fail.
- **RingDiv benchmark construction**: While the curated 300k subset is valuable, it may not fully capture the diversity of real synthetic/pharmaceutical chemical space, and ring diversity alone doesn't ensure other important structural diversity (e.g., stereoisomers, functional group variety).
- **Generalization beyond ring systems**: The framework excels at capturing ring topology, but it's unclear how well it handles other recurring higher-order patterns (e.g., nested scaffolds, bridged structures) or molecules with minimal ring content.
- **Comparison fairness**: Baseline comparisons may not fully account for computational cost trade-offs; HGR's efficiency gain versus other higher-order methods is not directly quantified.

## Open Questions
- How sensitive is HGR performance to grammar design and parameterization? Could there be a learned or adaptive grammar that tunes itself to datasets?
- How does HGR perform on out-of-distribution molecules or chemical spaces very different from training data (e.g., exotic ring systems, organometallic complexes)?
- Can HGR be extended to multi-molecular complexes or reaction sequences, not just single molecules?
- What is the actual wall-clock decoding speed and how does it scale compared to baseline methods?
- How well does HGR capture stereochemistry and 3D conformational information, which are crucial for drug efficacy?
- Could HGR be integrated with reinforcement learning or evolutionary algorithms for goal-directed molecular optimization?
- How does performance degrade on very small molecules or acyclic structures where higher-order topology is minimal?

## Key Takeaway
Grammar-based serialization of higher-order molecular topology enables valid generation and strong transfer learning while remaining computationally tractable.
