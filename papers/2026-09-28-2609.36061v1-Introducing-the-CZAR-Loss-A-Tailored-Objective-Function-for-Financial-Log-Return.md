# Introducing the CZAR Loss: A Tailored Objective Function for Financial Log-Return Predictions

**Authors:** Joel Pfeffer, J. M. Diederik Kruijssen, Florian Stecker, Steven N. Longmore
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.36061v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.36061v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies a fundamental problem in financial machine learning: standard loss functions (like mean squared error) treat zero as near-optimal for predicting stock returns, because returns naturally cluster around zero. The authors propose CZAR, a custom loss function that rewards models for getting the *direction* of returns right, even when noisy, and show it dramatically improves directional accuracy in real bitcoin trading data.

## Theoretical Background
Financial return prediction differs from typical regression because the signal is weak relative to noise — the conditional mean of log-returns is close to zero. Symmetric losses (MSE, MAE) penalize equally on both sides of the true value, which means predicting zero constantly becomes a hard-to-beat baseline. Under a Gaussian linear model, this paper proves that all symmetric monotonic losses share a universal "breakeven" directional accuracy threshold — the accuracy a model must exceed to beat the zero predictor. As noise increases, this threshold rises toward impossibility. The paper builds on standard convex optimization theory and uses properties of the Gaussian distribution to derive these theoretical bounds.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical characterization**: Proves that symmetric losses have a universal breakeven directional accuracy threshold that depends on prediction noise-to-signal ratio; threshold becomes unobtainable as noise grows.
2. **CZAR loss function**: A piecewise quadratic, convex loss designed specifically for return prediction. It is asymmetric (rewards correct direction), penalizes undershoots and wrong-direction predictions nearly linearly, and includes an adaptive floor for evaluation.
3. **Practical advantage**: Under CZAR, the breakeven directional accuracy stays near 50% (coin-flip level) even as noise increases, whereas symmetric losses' thresholds spike dramatically.
4. **Closed-form gradients and Hessians**: CZAR integrates into gradient-boosted frameworks (e.g., LightGBM) with minimal computational overhead.
5. **Empirical validation**: LightGBM trained with CZAR on intraday Bitcoin returns shows reduced bias toward zero and better directional accuracy on large-magnitude moves.

## Connections to Other Work
This addresses a known tension in quantitative finance between statistical loss functions and economic objectives. Related work includes [[risk-management]] frameworks that focus on directional accuracy rather than point prediction accuracy, and [[algorithmic-trading]] systems that prioritize correct-sign forecasts. The paper implicitly critiques the mismatch between training objectives and evaluation metrics common in [[deep-learning]] for finance. It relates to the broader optimization literature on task-aligned loss design.

## Practical Applications
1. **Intraday crypto trading**: Predict Bitcoin/Ethereum price direction for high-frequency strategies; CZAR reduces false-signal noise.
2. **Equity factor models**: Train directional alphas for long-short portfolios where being right on direction matters more than magnitude.
3. **Commodity futures**: Predict natural gas, crude oil, or grain price moves where data is noisy but directional bets have payoff.
4. **FX pair forecasting**: Train models to predict currency appreciation/depreciation, where sign matters for carry and momentum strategies.
5. **Risk-on/off regime prediction**: Use CZAR to flag whether markets are entering risk-on or risk-off phases for portfolio hedging.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: Although the paper claims four hyperparameters reduce to one choice via defaults, sensitivity analysis is sparse. Do those defaults generalize across asset classes, time horizons, or market regimes?
2. **Distributional assumptions**: Theoretical guarantees assume Gaussian linear models and Gaussian returns. Heavy-tailed validation is brief; real returns exhibit skew and kurtosis that may break the theory.
3. **Piecewise design**: The piecewise quadratic structure introduces non-smoothness at boundaries, which could slow optimization or cause convergence issues in non-convex neural network settings (tested only on LightGBM).
4. **Evaluation bias**: The "adaptive loss floor" for evaluation is underexplained — its design and sensitivity are unclear, yet it directly affects ranking of models.
5. **Limited empirical scope**: Only Bitcoin tested. No equity, FX, or commodity validation. Intraday focus may not generalize to daily or weekly prediction horizons.
6. **True label leakage risk**: No discussion of forward-bias, look-ahead bias, or proper train-test splits in the BTC experiment.

## Open Questions
1. How sensitive is CZAR performance to the choice and scaling of its four hyperparameters across different assets and frequencies?
2. Does CZAR work equally well in neural network architectures (RNNs, transformers) that use custom gradient descent, or is it fundamentally suited to tree-based models?
3. Can the theoretical breakeven analysis extend to non-Gaussian or heavy-tailed return distributions (e.g., stable Paretian models)?
4. What happens under regime shifts — does CZAR maintain its advantage when market microstructure changes (e.g., volatility regimes, liquidity events)?
5. How does CZAR compare to other asymmetric losses used in finance (e.g., pinball loss for quantile regression, or weighted MSE)?
6. Can CZAR be extended to multi-step ahead forecasting or sequence prediction, or does it only work for one-step-ahead returns?

## Key Takeaway
A custom loss function for stock returns that keeps directional forecasts competitive with zero baseline, even under high noise.
