# Where A Small Language Model Helps in Invoice Categorisation, Understood Through Embedding Geometry

**Authors:** Emma Ceccherini, Daniel Lawson, Anjulika Salhan
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18033v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18033v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that small language models (SLMs) like SBERT can accurately categorize invoices into accounting codes while remaining cost-effective and interpretable for in-house deployment. The authors analyze why this works by studying the geometric structure of sentence embeddings in financial text, finding that pre-trained embeddings naturally cluster by vendor identity and that these clusters correlate with classification performance.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two main areas. First, it extends research on embedding geometry—the observation that neural network embeddings often have specific geometric properties like anisotropy (unequal variance across dimensions). Prior work showed this at the token level in transformers (the neural architecture behind modern language models); this paper extends those findings to sentence embeddings. Second, it applies sentence transformers (SBERT)—models trained to produce meaningful embeddings of whole sentences rather than tokens—to a financial domain where text similarity and vendor context matter. The paper also compares against zero-shot performance from larger language models (LLMs) and simpler baselines.

## Core Contributions
1. **Embedding geometry in financial corpora**: The first systematic analysis showing that sentence embeddings in invoice text are globally anisotropic but locally isotropic (clustered), with clusters strongly tied to vendor identity—a finding that extends token-level geometric observations to the sentence level.
2. **SLM performance on invoice classification**: Demonstrates that SBERT achieves 0.96 accuracy with fine-tuning on a single GPU, outperforming zero-shot LLMs and vendor-only baselines, while generalizing to new clients with ~100 labeled examples per client (0.9 F1).
3. **Counterintuitive UI finding**: Reveals that structured invoice input (helpful to human accountants) does not improve SLM performance, suggesting models and humans extract different information.
4. **Practical economics**: Shows in-house SLM deployment is viable—cheaper, more interpretable, and more secure than cloud LLM APIs while maintaining accuracy.

## Connections to Other Work
This connects to broader work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] by analyzing *why* embeddings work rather than just measuring accuracy. It relates to [[Attention Is All You Need]] and transformer-based NLP by studying learned representations. The vendor-clustering finding parallels work on social biases and demographic clustering in embeddings. It also addresses the practical deployment problem that motivates [[RLHF]] and interpretability research—organizations need models they can trust and afford to run locally.

## Practical Applications
1. **Automated GL coding in accounting software**: Integrating SBERT into enterprise accounting platforms (QuickBooks, Xero, SAP) to auto-suggest or auto-assign invoice GL codes, reducing manual entry by accountants.
2. **SME financial compliance**: Small businesses and nonprofits using in-house models to ensure invoice categorization meets tax and audit requirements without monthly cloud API costs.
3. **Vendor intelligence**: Using vendor clusters learned by SBERT to flag unusual invoices or detect vendor fraud (sudden shift in spending patterns).
4. **Client-specific accounting**: Accounting firms deploying client-specific SLM instances to handle each client's unique vendor relationships and expense policies.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **FinanceGPT Lite**: A SaaS platform offering pre-trained SBERT models fine-tuned per accounting firm's GL codes, with on-premises deployment options for compliance-sensitive industries (healthcare, fintech).
2. **Invoice Autopilot**: A plug-and-play Chrome extension + API for accountants that auto-categorizes invoices in real time, learning from each user's corrections and storing models locally.
3. **Vendor Analytics Dashboard**: Uses SBERT clustering to visualize vendor spending patterns, flag outliers, and surface cost-savings opportunities without sending data to cloud.
4. **Embedded Finance SDK**: A lightweight library for accounting software (ERPs, accounting apps) to integrate accurate, on-device invoice classification with zero external API calls.
5. **Compliance Audit Bot**: A tool for tax firms and internal auditors that uses SBERT geometry analysis to detect misclassified invoices and potential audit risks, with explainability via embedding space visualization.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scope**: Tested on a single (unnamed) financial corpus; generalization to different invoice formats, languages, or regulatory regimes is unclear.
2. **Embedding geometry analysis**: The link between clustering and performance is correlational; causation is not proven. The paper doesn't test whether explicit clustering-aware loss functions would improve results.
3. **Structured input paradox**: The finding that structured input doesn't help is underexplored—it's unclear whether this is a SLM limitation or a training/architecture choice, limiting actionability.
4. **Scalability**: No analysis of performance at scale (millions of invoices) or with much larger SLMs; computational cost comparison to LLMs is absent.
5. **Generalization**: Requires ~100 labeled invoices per new client, which may be onerous for very small clients or novel vendor types.
6. **Explainability**: While geometry is analyzed, the paper offers limited guidance on how to *explain* a specific categorization to an accountant (which vendors are most influential? which words?).

## Open Questions
1. What causes the structured input paradox? Is it an architectural limitation of SBERT, or a training artifact? Would fine-tuning on structured + unstructured pairs resolve it?
2. Can geometric analysis (e.g., distance from a cluster centroid) be used as a confidence score or flag for human review?
3. How does SBERT compare to other lightweight transformer models (e.g., DistilBERT, MPNet) on this task?
4. Can vendor clusters be leveraged via few-shot or meta-learning to reduce the per-client labeled data requirement below 100?
5. Does the local isotropy observation hold across different financial domains (e.g., expense reports, purchase orders, contracts)?
6. How stable are vendor clusters over time? Do they shift when vendors change pricing, locations, or product lines?

## Key Takeaway
Small language models categorize invoices accurately and affordably in-house, and their success depends on embedding clusters that form around vendor identity.
