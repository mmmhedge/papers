# GEAR: Generative Expansion and Real Anchoring for Two-Stage Distillation of Tabular Foundation Models

**Authors:** Qi Qin, Jiajie Zhu, Dali Chen, Yuzhao Zhang, Jia-Xing Han et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.18849v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18849v1)

## Summary
This paper presents GEAR, a method to distill large tabular foundation models (neural networks trained on structured data) into tiny, fast models that run on standard computers. The key innovation is a two-stage process: first, generate synthetic data to expand training coverage while learning from the original model's predictions; second, re-anchor the student model to real data to avoid errors from synthetic examples. The result is 50–2800× faster inference with minimal accuracy loss.

## Theoretical Background
Tabular foundation models use in-context learning (treating each prediction as a learned task solved with examples in the input context), similar to how large language models work. However, this flexibility comes at a cost: every prediction requires running the full model, which is slow and memory-intensive. Model distillation — training a smaller "student" model to mimic a larger "teacher" model — is a standard compression technique. GEAR builds on two ideas: (1) synthetic data generation to augment training sets, and (2) two-stage optimization to prevent the student from memorizing errors from synthetic labels. The authors derive a risk certificate (a theoretical bound) relating generated data volume to model performance.

## Core Contributions
1. **Two-stage distillation framework**: Stage 1 trains on synthetic queries with soft teacher targets; Stage 2 re-anchors using real labels and out-of-fold predictions to prevent label leakage.
2. **Risk-theoretic characterization**: A formal bound on the trade-off between synthetic data volume and generator quality.
3. **Empirical gains across multiple student architectures**: MLPs and tree-based models (LightGBM, XGBoost) all benefit; performance gains of 1.8–2.2 AUC points on binary classification.
4. **Massive efficiency gains**: 57–2866× faster inference, 1.9–3.3× lower peak memory, enabling CPU deployment.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends [[knowledge distillation]] (training small models from large ones) by combining it with synthetic data augmentation and two-stage learning. It relates to recent work on foundation models for tabular data (e.g., SAINT, TabR, GBDT-based models) but focuses on *deployment efficiency* rather than just predictive power. The "out-of-fold" re-anchoring strategy parallels cross-validation techniques to avoid overfitting. The risk certificate connects to [[statistical learning theory]] and generalization bounds.

## Practical Applications
- **Real-time recommendation systems**: Replace slow foundation models with MLPs at inference time for sub-millisecond latency.
- **Mobile and edge deployment**: Bring tabular predictions to smartphones or IoT devices where large models are infeasible.
- **Cost-sensitive cloud inference**: Reduce compute costs for high-volume tabular prediction (e.g., fraud detection, credit scoring).
- **On-device analytics**: Privacy-preserving inference without sending data to servers.
- **Legacy system integration**: Deploy predictions on older hardware or embedded systems that cannot run modern deep learning.

## Technical Limitations
- **Generator quality dependency**: The method relies on high-quality synthetic data generation; poor generator output creates a performance ceiling.
- **Theoretical bounds may be loose**: The risk certificate characterizes trade-offs but may not be tight in practice.
- **Two-stage optimization overhead**: Stage 1 requires training on many synthetic queries, which adds upfront cost (though inference is faster).
- **Out-of-fold requirement**: Stage 2 needs non-overlapping train splits for the teacher and real labels, reducing effective training data.
- **Limited to tabular domain**: Approach is not obviously applicable to images, text, or other modalities.
- **Comparison baselines**: Strong non-TFM baselines (CatBoost) still compete on some tasks; unclear if gains over simpler methods justify complexity.

## Open Questions
1. How does GEAR scale to very large tabular datasets (millions of rows, thousands of features)?
2. Can the risk certificate be tightened, and does it predict observed performance trade-offs accurately?
3. Does the method work with other generator architectures (e.g., diffusion models, VAEs) beyond the paper's synthetic covariates?
4. How sensitive is Stage 2 re-anchoring to the ratio of real to synthetic training data? Is there an optimal split?
5. Can GEAR be extended to time-series tabular data or datasets with hierarchical structure?
6. How do distilled models perform under distribution shift (e.g., test data differs from training)?

## Key Takeaway
Distill large tabular models into tiny, fast ones by training on synthetic data in two stages, reducing inference time 50–2800× with minimal accuracy loss.
