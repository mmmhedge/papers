# The Like Trap: Multi-Stage Poisoning against Agents in Similarity-based Recommendation Systems

**Authors:** Yue Xing, Pengfei He, Zitao Li
**Published:** 2026-09-22 | **arXiv:** [2609.27155v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27155v1)

## Summary
This paper reveals how adversaries can poison similarity-based recommendation systems to manipulate LLM-based agents operating on social media platforms. Rather than directly exposing poisoned content to agents, the attack exploits the like-score feedback loop in recommendation algorithms, causing the system itself to surface malicious posts. The work matters because autonomous agents are increasingly managing user accounts, yet this vector of attack—attacking through the platform's own ranking mechanism—was largely unexplored.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two convergent research areas. First, recent work on [[large-language-models]]-based [[agents]] that autonomously interact with web platforms and social media on behalf of users. Second, a growing body of literature on poisoning attacks against ML systems, which traditionally assumed adversaries can directly expose poisoned content. The paper focuses on OASIS, a specific similarity-based recommendation system (likely using embeddings and like-score mechanisms common in social media platforms), and shows how its feedback loop can be weaponized. The like-score mechanism uses user engagement signals to rank posts in a user's feed—a design choice meant to personalize experience but which becomes an attack surface when agents passively consume that feed.

## Core Contributions
1. **Theoretical characterization**: Formal analysis proving the like-score mechanism in OASIS-style systems can be exploited, including conditions under which multi-stage poisoning chains succeed.
2. **Subtle attack surface**: Demonstrating that poisoned posts can be ranked above the system's own retrieval threshold by manipulating the like-score feedback loop—bypassing traditional content-similarity defenses.
3. **Practical algorithm**: An algorithm that generates realistic (not obviously fake) poisoned posts designed to accumulate likes and trigger the feedback loop, validated experimentally.
4. **Feedback loop exploitation**: Unlike prior work assuming direct adversary-to-agent exposure, this exploits the recommendation system's own amplification mechanism.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[alignment]] concerns in autonomous agents with adversarial ML. It extends poisoning attack literature (typically focused on training-data attacks or direct adversarial examples) to the novel domain of recommendation-system manipulation. Related to broader work on [[multi-agent-systems]] security and the failure modes of agents operating in partially-adversarial environments. Complements recent research on prompt injection and indirect agent manipulation, but targets a different layer (ranking mechanism vs. prompt input).

## Practical Applications
- **Account security**: Defending autonomous agents managing social media, financial, or email accounts against feeds being poisoned with malicious links, deepfakes, or social-engineering content.
- **Platform integrity**: Designing recommendation systems robust to like-farming and coordinated poisoning campaigns, especially as agents become mainstream users.
- **Fraud prevention**: Detecting multi-stage attack chains where accounts systematically like crafted posts to amplify them.
- **Agent monitoring**: Building agent sandboxes that check feed integrity before autonomous agents consume and act on social media content.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Agent Feed Firewall**: A middleware service that scrubs feeds for LLM agents, detecting poisoned content by identifying signatures of the multi-stage attack pattern.
2. **Recommendation Robustness Auditor**: B2B platform audit tool for social media and marketplace companies to test their like-score mechanisms against this attack.
3. **Autonomous Agent Bodyguard**: A verification layer for agents that validates feed content against external trust signals (verified accounts, reputation scores) before consuming it.
4. **Poisoning Detection Service**: Real-time detection of like-farming coordinated campaigns using anomaly detection on like patterns and post similarity.
5. **Agent-Safe Social API**: A wrapper API for platforms that sanitizes recommendations served to agents, filtering out low-confidence or anomalous posts.

## Technical Limitations
- **Realism assumption**: The algorithm generates "realistic" poisoned posts, but the definition and evaluation of realism are subjective and may not hold across diverse human raters or moderation systems.
- **Single platform focus**: Analysis assumes OASIS-style recommendations; generalization to other platforms (TikTok, Twitter algorithms) is unclear.
- **Temporal dynamics**: Assumes static like-score mechanisms; platforms may adapt ranking in response to detected attacks.
- **Agent behavior**: Assumes agents passively consume feeds; agents that verify sources, check timestamps, or compare multiple feeds may be harder to trick.
- **Scale**: Unclear whether the attack scales when platforms deploy robust anomaly detection or when competing coordinated poisoning campaigns interfere.

## Open Questions
- How do multi-agent scenarios affect the attack? (e.g., if multiple poisoned agents amplify each other's posts, can moderation systems detect the coordination?)
- What defenses can platforms implement without breaking benign recommendation quality?
- How does the attack perform against agents with built-in skepticism or source verification?
- Can the attack be extended to other recommendation signals beyond likes (shares, comments, dwell time)?
- What is the minimum number of coordinated accounts or stages needed for guaranteed success?

## Key Takeaway
Recommendation algorithms can be exploited to poison autonomous agent feeds without direct adversary-agent contact, leveraging like-score feedback loops.
