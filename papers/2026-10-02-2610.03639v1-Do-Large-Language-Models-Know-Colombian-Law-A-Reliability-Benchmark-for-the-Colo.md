# Do Large Language Models Know Colombian Law? A Reliability Benchmark for the Colombian Legal System

**Authors:** Rubén Manrique, Michelle Castellanos, Jorge Morales, Juan David Gutiérrez, Antonio Barreto Rozo et al.
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03639v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03639v1)

## Summary
This paper evaluates whether large language models reliably answer questions about Colombian law—a question almost never studied outside the US legal context. The authors built a 1,042-question benchmark with expert validation and tested 15 models across ten legal areas. They found a striking pattern: models sound confident and relevant but are frequently factually wrong, and free-text answers rarely exceed 45% correctness even for the best models.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two converging lines of research. First, the growing use of LLMs in professional domains—particularly law—where accuracy is non-negotiable. Second, recent work on evaluating LLM reliability through benchmarks (e.g., MMLU, HellaSwag), which typically focus on English-language knowledge. The paper also draws on legal reasoning frameworks, specifically IRAC (Issue, Rule, Analysis, Conclusion)—a standard method lawyers use to structure arguments. The core insight is that evaluating models on *non-English legal systems* reveals failure modes that English-centric benchmarks might miss, and that "sounding right" (relevance) is uncorrelated with "being right" (correctness).

## Core Contributions
1. **First expert-validated benchmark for non-US law**: A 1,042-item dataset covering Colombian law with multi-stage expert review and human-in-the-loop quality control.
2. **Dissociation discovery**: Empirical evidence that relevance and correctness are negatively correlated (ρ = −0.46)—models produce coherent, on-topic answers that are factually wrong.
3. **Citation hallucination quantification**: Shows that roughly half the legal norms cited by models are fabricated or non-existent.
4. **Reproducible evaluation pipeline**: Released the benchmark construction methodology to enable similar studies in other jurisdictions and legal systems.
5. **Complexity-correctness inversion**: Reliability follows an inverted-U across question complexity, peaking at moderate difficulty.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper sits at the intersection of [[Evaluating Language Models for Legal Document Analysis]] and general [[Benchmark|benchmarking]] work on LLM factuality. It extends critiques of [[Hallucination in LLMs|hallucination]] by showing that hallucination is *especially dangerous* in high-stakes domains like law, where incorrect-sounding answers can mislead non-experts. The work also connects to [[Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)]] and source-grounding literature—the paper suggests that grounding in authoritative sources (e.g., Colombian legal codes) may mitigate the false-confidence problem. It complements studies on [[Multilingual and Cross-Cultural LLM Evaluation|multilingual LLM evaluation]] by focusing on a specific non-Anglophone jurisdiction.

## Practical Applications
1. **Legal AI quality gates**: Law firms and legal tech companies can use this benchmark (or similar ones) to screen which LLMs are safe to deploy for Colombian legal research, training, or document review—and where human review is mandatory.
2. **Legal education risk assessment**: Law schools in Colombia and similar jurisdictions can use the findings to educate students about LLM limitations and when to rely on them (vs. primary sources).
3. **Regulatory compliance tools**: Government agencies in Colombia can use this work to set minimum reliability thresholds before permitting LLM-assisted legal document generation or review.
4. **Localized AI governance**: Non-US jurisdictions can use this methodology to audit whether commercial LLMs meet domain-specific accuracy requirements before adoption.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Legal AI auditor for emerging markets**: A SaaS platform that runs benchmarks like this one on LLMs before legal deployment, scoring reliability by jurisdiction and legal area. Charge law firms and legal tech companies per audit.
2. **Grounded legal research assistant**: An LLM + retrieval system that answers Colombian legal questions *only* when answers can be grounded in official legal codes and case law; flag uncertain answers to human lawyers.
3. **Jurisdiction-specific LLM fine-tuning service**: Offer fine-tuned models trained on translated/localized legal datasets for underserved jurisdictions (Colombia, Mexico, Brazil) with guaranteed minimum accuracy benchmarks.
4. **Legal LLM certification body**: Provide independent third-party certification for LLMs used in legal contexts, modeled on medical device approval—publish reliability scores by jurisdiction and legal domain.
5. **AI-assisted legal research for SME law firms**: Target small law firms in Colombia with a tool that uses retrieval-augmented LLMs to draft legal documents and research questions, but automatically highlights uncertain claims and requires human sign-off.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Benchmark size and scope**: 1,042 questions is substantial but may not cover rare legal scenarios or recent legislative changes; Colombian law is dynamic, and benchmarks become stale.
2. **Snapshot evaluation**: The paper evaluates models at one point in time; LLMs are updated frequently, so results may not reflect current versions.
3. **Expert availability bias**: The benchmark was built by experts in Colombian law, which may be geographically concentrated in Bogotá or other urban centers; rural or frontier legal practice may differ.
4. **Language effects unclear**: It's not clear whether low accuracy stems from knowledge gaps (models were trained on less Colombian law data) or language/translation issues, making fixes harder.
5. **Generalization to other jurisdictions**: Findings may not transfer to other Latin American or civil-law countries; each jurisdiction has unique legal codes and institutional structures.
6. **Automated judge unreliability**: While LLM judges reproduce human rankings (ρ ≥ 0.88), they may still have systematic biases in favor of certain reasoning styles—blind human expert scoring is gold standard but expensive.

## Open Questions
1. **Can retrieval-augmented generation close the gap?** Do systems that ground answers in Colombian legal codes and case law reach practical reliability thresholds (e.g., >80% correctness)?
2. **Why does relevance anticorrelate with correctness?** Is this a scaling law artifact, a training-data effect, or a fundamental limitation of attention mechanisms on specialized domains?
3. **Does fine-tuning on Colombian law help?** What if you fine-tune a base model on translated Colombian legal texts and case law—how much does accuracy improve?
4. **How do other Latin American legal systems compare?** Does Mexican, Brazilian, or Argentinian law show similar reliability patterns, or are findings specific to Colombian law?
5. **Can models cite accurately?** Is the 50% citation hallucination rate fixable through instruction-tuning, or do current architectures struggle with formal citation in any domain?
6. **What's the expert-efficiency frontier?** How much human expert review is *required* per legal task for safety? Can workflows be designed to minimize expert overhead while staying safe?

## Key Takeaway
Top LLMs sound confident answering Colombian law questions but are wrong almost half the time—lawyers and courts should not use them without expert review.
