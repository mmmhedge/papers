# Muon Learns Facts Better: Understanding the Role of Spectral Orthogonalization

**Authors:** Xuheng Li, Qiwei Di, Yuan Cao, Quanquan Gu
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.02798v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02798v1)

## Summary
This paper explains why the Muon optimizer—which applies spectral orthogonalization to neural network weight updates—trains faster and learns more structured representations than standard gradient descent. Using a tractable model of factual recall, the authors show that spectral orthogonalization fundamentally changes *how* and *in what order* a transformer learns different types of information, enabling exponential convergence instead of polynomial decay.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on continuous-time optimization theory, where gradient descent (GD), Muon, and Adam are studied as limits of differential equations called gradient flow (GF). Prior work (Nichani et al., 2025) showed that transformers learning "facts"—mappings from (subject, relation) pairs to answers—exhibit feature separation: they learn relation-dependent features before subject-dependent ones when subjects outnumber relations. This paper extends that framework by analyzing what happens when you apply spectral orthogonalization (the key innovation in Muon), which constrains weight matrices to have orthonormal singular vectors. The tractable factual-recall model isolates the learning dynamics of these components, making theoretical analysis feasible where full neural network analysis would be intractable.

## Core Contributions
1. **Quantified speedup from spectral orthogonalization**: Shows the learning-time ratio between subject and relation components drops from Θ̃(√S/R) under standard GF to Θ̃(1) under spectral GF—eliminating the feature-separation bottleneck.

2. **Exponential vs. polynomial convergence**: Proves that error under standard GF decays as 1/(T log T) but under spectral GF decays exponentially (exp(−poly(T))), a fundamental acceleration.

3. **Equivariance properties**: Establishes that standard GF and spectral GF are equivariant under orthogonal transformations of embeddings (invariant to rotations), whereas Sign GF (Adam's continuous limit) is not—meaning different random initializations can produce drastically different learning orders for Adam but not for Muon.

4. **Mechanistic explanation**: Provides the first rigorous characterization of *why* spectral orthogonalization reshapes learning dynamics, grounded in a concrete, analyzable model.

## Practical Applications
1. **Faster large-model training**: Muon already shows ~20% wall-clock speedups on billion-parameter models; this paper provides principled understanding to improve the method further.

2. **Interpretable fact-based systems**: Knowledge graphs and semantic databases can use this insight to design architectures that learn relations and entities in a predictable, decoupled way.

3. **Reliable feature learning in NLP**: Language models need stable learning of grammatical rules (relations) before lexical associations (subjects); spectral orthogonalization could improve sample efficiency.

4. **Initialization robustness**: Understanding equivariance properties helps practitioners choose stable optimizers and initializations for production systems.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Restrictive model**: The factual-recall task is highly idealized. Real transformers learn entangled, hierarchical features; the linear-recovery assumption may not hold for vision or complex reasoning tasks.

2. **Continuous-time analysis**: Results use gradient flow, not discrete SGD. Batch size, learning rate schedules, and noise effects are not captured.

3. **No empirical validation in paper**: Theoretical claims are not validated on the factual-recall task itself or on real LLM training. The connection between theory and practice remains empirical.

4. **Equivariance only for orthogonal transforms**: Non-orthogonal initialization or weight matrices (e.g., from pruning or quantization) break the equivariance guarantees.

5. **Scalability of spectral orthogonalization**: Computing singular vectors for very large matrices is expensive; the paper does not address computational cost trade-offs.

## Open Questions
1. **How do batch effects change the picture?** Does spectral GF maintain its exponential convergence under mini-batch SGD with realistic learning rates?

2. **Can we extend to nonlinear models?** The analysis uses linearity critically; does spectral orthogonalization accelerate feature learning in deeper or fully nonlinear architectures?

3. **What about other matrix structures?** Does this apply to low-rank factorizations, structured sparsity, or other parameterizations beyond dense orthogonal matrices?

4. **Can we predict when feature separation hurts vs. helps?** For some tasks, learning subjects before relations might be better; can we characterize when spectral GF's equalization is actually beneficial?

5. **How does this interact with regularization?** Weight decay, dropout, and normalization layers break some assumptions; how robust are the theoretical guarantees?

6. **Empirical verification**: Can the learning-time ratios and convergence rates be verified experimentally on synthetic factual-recall tasks and toy language models?

## Key Takeaway
Spectral orthogonalization makes transformers learn features exponentially faster by eliminating the bottleneck where one type of information must wait for another.
