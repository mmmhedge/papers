# Multi-Agent AI System for Radiology Report Structuring and Quality Assurance with Independent Radiologist Evaluation

**Authors:** Iryna Hartsock, Cesar Lam, Christopher Otteni, Aliya Qayyum, Robert Gatenby et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18072v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18072v1)

## Summary
This paper describes a locally deployed multi-agent AI system that automatically structures radiology reports into standardized anatomical sections and performs quality assurance checks to catch errors like section mismatches and undocumented critical findings. The system was evaluated on 638 real chest, abdomen, and pelvis CT reports, with independent radiologist validation showing 84% of reports achieved "excellent" or "good" quality ratings, suggesting that AI-assisted structuring could improve consistency and safety in clinical radiology workflows.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two key foundations: (1) **large language models (LLMs)** — neural networks trained on vast text corpora that can understand and generate human language — and (2) **multi-agent systems** — architectures where multiple specialized AI components work together to accomplish complex tasks. The paper uses local LLMs (deployed on-premise rather than via cloud APIs) combined with rule-based components (regex patterns) to parse unstructured medical text. Prior work in clinical NLP has explored report generation and information extraction, but combining multiple agents into a unified structuring-plus-QA pipeline in a regulated medical environment remains less common. The approach also draws on quality assurance best practices in radiology, where standardized reporting (e.g., structured templates) has long been recognized as improving communication and reducing errors.

## Core Contributions
1. **End-to-end structuring pipeline**: Sentences from radiology findings are automatically mapped into predefined anatomical categories while preserving original content — tested on 22,270 sentences across 638 reports.
2. **Integrated QA module**: The system simultaneously detects multiple error types (section mismatches, gender-anatomy conflicts, undocumented critical findings) within a single workflow rather than as a separate step.
3. **Local deployment validation**: Demonstrates feasibility of running LLM-based medical AI without cloud dependencies, addressing data privacy and compliance concerns in healthcare.
4. **Independent radiologist evaluation**: Two board-certified radiologists independently validated a 45-report subset, establishing human-grounded performance metrics (69% full agreement on correct restructuring, 100% agreement that no clinically important information was lost).

## Connections to Other Work
This paper sits at the intersection of [[Generative Models]] for medical NLP and [[Multi-Agent Systems]] for clinical decision support. It complements prior work on [[RLHF]]-based report generation (which focuses on creating new text) by instead focusing on parsing and quality control of existing reports. The structuring task relates to information extraction and semantic role labeling — classical NLP problems — but applies them to the high-stakes medical domain. The emphasis on local deployment and independent validation distinguishes it from purely academic benchmarking studies and brings it closer to the concerns of [[Alignment]] (ensuring AI outputs match human expert judgment) in clinical AI.

## Practical Applications
1. **Hospital radiology departments**: Automatic structuring of free-text reports into templates, reducing dictation time and standardizing communication for referrers and downstream care teams.
2. **Quality assurance workflows**: Flagging reports for human review before signing, catching typos, logical inconsistencies, or missed critical findings before they reach patients.
3. **Electronic health record (EHR) integration**: Extracting and populating structured data fields automatically, enabling downstream analytics and decision support systems.
4. **Training and peer review**: Highlighting common structuring errors and inconsistencies across a radiology practice to identify training needs or individual variability.
5. **Regulatory compliance**: Documenting that critical findings were communicated, generating audit trails for medical-legal purposes.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RadQA Pro**: A white-label SaaS platform for hospital radiology departments offering AI-driven report structuring + QA flagging, integrated into PACS (picture archiving and communication system) workflows. Monetize per-report or per-radiologist-license.
2. **Local LLM Radiology Appliance**: Hardware + software device that runs LLM report processing entirely on-premises, marketed to health systems with strict data-residency requirements. Recurring maintenance and model-update fees.
3. **Radiology Report Standardization Consulting**: Combine this system with change-management consulting to help practices implement structured reporting templates, using the AI as an enforcement layer. Revenue from implementation services and ongoing licensing.
4. **Critical Finding Alert Engine**: Focus the QA module into a standalone real-time alert system that flags high-risk miscommunications (e.g., critical finding not escalated), integrated into worklist management software.
5. **Teleradiology Quality Monitor**: Deploy the system as a backend service for teleradiology platforms to automatically QA reports from distributed radiologists, ensuring consistent quality across geographies and shifts.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited evaluation scope**: Only 45 reports (7% of dataset) received independent radiologist review; larger validation cohort needed to establish true clinical safety metrics and generalization.
2. **Modest anatomical coverage**: Evaluated on chest, abdomen, pelvis CT reports only; performance on other modalities (MRI, ultrasound, X-ray) and body regions unknown.
3. **Disagreement on edge cases**: 27% of validated reports had radiologist disagreement on correctness, suggesting the system produces ambiguous outputs that require human judgment anyway.
4. **No longitudinal follow-up**: Study did not track whether structured reports actually improved downstream clinical outcomes, referrer satisfaction, or error rates in diagnosis or treatment.
5. **Regex + LLM hybrid dependency**: Reliance on both rule-based parsing and local LLMs means performance degrades if either component fails; fragility under distribution shift (e.g., unusual report formats or new terminology).
6. **Hallucination risk**: Even local LLMs can generate plausible-sounding but false anatomical mappings; the paper confirms no fabricated content was introduced in the validation set but doesn't quantify this risk systematically.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance vary across different radiologist dictation styles, report lengths, and anatomical specialties? Are there systematic failure modes?
2. Can the system generalize to other imaging modalities (MRI, ultrasound, PET) without retraining, or does each require custom configuration?
3. What is the clinical impact of structured reports on downstream care? Does standardization reduce errors in diagnosis, referral, or treatment planning?
4. How does the system handle rare or novel findings not seen during development? Can it gracefully degrade or flag uncertainty?
5. What is the user experience for radiologists using this system? Does it reduce cognitive load and dictation time, or create friction?
6. How does performance scale with larger, more diverse datasets? Is there a plateau in accuracy or continued improvement?
7. Could the QA module be extended to detect more subtle quality issues (e.g., incomplete differential diagnoses, missing follow-up recommendations)?

## Key Takeaway
AI multi-agent system successfully structures radiology reports and flags QA issues in real clinical workflows, with radiologist-validated performance at 84% "good or excellent" standard.
