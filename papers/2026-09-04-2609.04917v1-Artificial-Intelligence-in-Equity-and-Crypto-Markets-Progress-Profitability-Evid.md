# Artificial Intelligence in Equity and Crypto Markets: Progress, Profitability Evidence, and the Limits of Automated Investing

**Authors:** Linsen Zhu, Mengqing Cai
**Published:** 2026-09-04 | **arXiv:** [2609.04917v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04917v1)

## Summary
This review examines whether AI systems can reliably generate investment profits in equity and crypto markets. The authors find that while AI has made real progress in prediction, text analysis, and portfolio design, evidence for *sustained net returns* (after costs and real-world constraints) remains weak — and no AI approach is proven to work consistently across different market conditions and asset scales.

## Theoretical Background
The paper frames AI investing through an **alpha-translation chain**: raw information → predictive signal → tradable positions → executable orders → risk-adjusted returns net of costs. This conceptual framework spans multiple AI subfields: [[deep-learning]] for feature extraction, [[transformers]] and [[large-language-models]] for financial text processing, [[reinforcement-learning]] for dynamic portfolio optimization, and [[agents]] that coordinate research and execution workflows. The review builds on decades of quantitative finance literature examining market efficiency, transaction costs, and the persistence of predictive edges — while acknowledging that modern foundation models and RL have vastly expanded the toolkit.

## Core Contributions
1. **Systematic taxonomy** of where AI has and hasn't succeeded: clear upstream wins in prediction and NLP, but sparse evidence for downstream profitability.
2. **Concrete failure mechanisms**: temporal contamination (data leakage), repeated selection bias, survivorship bias, weak benchmarks, implementation friction, and capacity constraints that break the chain from prediction to net alpha.
3. **Separate treatment of crypto markets**: distinguishes spot, perpetual futures, and on-chain mechanics, showing crypto adds signal but requires bespoke evaluation.
4. **Audited results gap**: highlights the gap between published research (often biased) and actual live-capital performance — most papers lack point-in-time data or prospective testing.
5. **Governance framework**: proposes concrete requirements for credible AI trading claims (point-in-time models, decision-aligned objectives, joint portfolio–execution evaluation, controlled adaptation, prospective tests).

## Connections to Other Work
This work critiques and synthesizes across multiple communities:
- **[[RLHF]]** and agent alignment: notes that portfolio objectives often misalign with actual risk preferences or market constraints.
- **[[Attention Is All You Need]]** spillover: highlights that foundation models excel at text but their edge rarely survives venue costs and capacity limits.
- **Market microstructure** literature: emphasizes that small prediction edges dissolve under execution costs, slippage, and liquidity constraints.
- **Traditional quantitative finance**: connects to decades of research on factor decay, regime change, and the limits of backtesting.
- **Mechanistic interpretability**: hints that explainability of AI predictions is orthogonal to profitability — a model can be interpretable and still lose money after costs.

## Practical Applications
1. **Due diligence tooling**: Software that applies the alpha-translation chain to audit AI trading claims — surfaces temporal contamination, selection bias, and cost leakage in published backtests.
2. **Crypto trading edge validation**: Point-in-time backtesting frameworks specifically for spot, perpetual, and decentralized venues, with separate cost models and regime filters.
3. **Risk oversight dashboards**: Real-time monitors that flag capacity constraints, regime shifts, and predictor decay in live AI trading systems — alerts when a model's edge is likely decomposing.
4. **Prospective testing services**: Managed platforms for AI trading strategies that enforce point-in-time data, prospective evaluation, and third-party governance — reducing bias in published results.
5. **Institutional AI trading audits**: Consulting services that certify live-capital AI systems against the paper's governance framework, improving credibility with regulators and LPs.

## Technical Limitations
1. **No persistent winner identified**: The review finds no AI architecture (RL agents, LLMs, time-series models, etc.) proven to reliably outperform after costs and adaptation — a null result that some practitioners will dispute.
2. **Sparse live-capital data**: Most credible evidence is from proprietary firms; public research is systematically biased toward success, making generalizations hard.
3. **Crypto orthogonality**: Crypto's decentralized and high-throughput nature breaks many equity-market assumptions; results may not transfer.
4. **Regime non-stationarity**: Market regimes shift (correlations, volatility, vol-of-vol, liquidity); no model shown to adapt robustly across all regimes — adaptation itself introduces selection bias if not prospectively tested.
5. **Capacity and feedback loops**: A profitable AI strategy may degrade as it scales or as competitors learn; the paper cannot quantify this decay without live experiments.
6. **Governance asymmetry**: Institutional traders can hide live-capital results; this review only examines published work, which is positively biased.

## Open Questions
1. **Prospective long-term studies**: Can we see 3–5 year prospective backtests (point-in-time data, no look-ahead) on a standardized suite of assets? Most published results are 1–2 years.
2. **Cross-regime robustness**: Do any models persist across bull, bear, high-vol, and crisis regimes without retraining or parameter adjustment? How much decay occurs?
3. **Capacity frontier**: At what AUM does each AI approach's edge erode? What is the functional form of capacity drag?
4. **LLM + RL hybrids**: Do combined financial LLMs (for news/earnings) plus RL agents (for execution) outperform either alone after costs? Published evidence is scarce.
5. **Crypto on-chain signals**: Which on-chain metrics (flow, whale moves, liquidation cascades) yield durable edges? How do they interact with spot and perpetual markets?
6. **Adaptation governance**: What automated or human-in-the-loop retraining schedules minimize overfitting to recent data while preserving edge detection?
7. **Audit standards**: Should regulators mandate point-in-time data, prospective evaluation, and third-party certification for AI trading claims in public research?

## Key Takeaway
AI predicts markets better than before, but sustained profit after costs remains unproven — and governance, not just algorithms, is the bottleneck.
