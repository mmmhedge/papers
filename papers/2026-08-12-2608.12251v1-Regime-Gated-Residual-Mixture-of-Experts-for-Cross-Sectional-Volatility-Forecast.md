# Regime-Gated Residual Mixture-of-Experts for Cross-Sectional Volatility Forecasting

**Authors:** Junyi Ye, Gargi Vijay Borde
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12251v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12251v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes RG-ResMoE, a neural network architecture that forecasts stock volatility by using market regime information strategically—routing expert predictions rather than feeding regime data directly into the model. The key finding is that *where* regime information enters the network matters as much as *that* it enters: using it only for expert routing improves both forecasting accuracy and training stability, while naive direct input degrades performance.

## Theoretical Background
Volatility forecasting in finance traditionally relies on models like GARCH (Generalized Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity), which assume volatility clusters and is regime-dependent—meaning its behavior shifts with market conditions (bull/bear markets, crisis periods, etc.). Cross-sectional volatility forecasting extends this to many assets simultaneously, predicting asset-level volatility from stock features and market state.

Mixture-of-experts (MoE) architectures — neural networks where multiple expert subnetworks specialize on different input regions and a gating network routes inputs to appropriate experts — have recently gained traction in deep learning. Residual connections (skip paths that help gradient flow) address training instability in deep networks. The paper combines these: a base predictor captures stock-level volatility signals, while regime-based gates route *corrections* to that base forecast through different expert pathways.

The tension the paper identifies is fundamental: regime information is predictive but introducing it naively can destabilize training, creating conflicting signals or gradient issues.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical demonstration that information integration pathway is decisive**: The same regime variables degrade performance when concatenated to inputs but improve it when used only for expert routing. This is a concrete architectural finding, not obvious a priori.

2. **RG-ResMoE design**: A specific architecture combining residual connections with soft-gated MoE, tailored for volatility forecasting where regime nonstationarity is endemic.

3. **Rigorous empirical protocol**: Walk-forward backtesting on 1,027 U.S. equities with matched hyperparameters, capacity, and random seeds across architectures—avoiding common pitfalls in ML-for-finance papers (data leakage, overfitting to backtest).

4. **Cross-validation on independent data**: Japanese equity panel confirms findings generalize, not just U.S.-specific.

5. **Soft vs. hard routing analysis**: Shows soft routing (weighted expert combination) outperforms hard routing (one-hot expert selection), with implications for how gating should work in regime-aware models.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several threads:

- **[[GARCH]] and regime-switching models**: Classical finance theory; this paper shows neural nets can improve on them by learning nonlinear feature interactions, but only if regime information is carefully integrated.
- **Mixture-of-experts in deep learning**: Recent work (e.g., sparse MoE in large models) focuses on scaling model capacity; this paper argues MoE's value for volatility is *not* capacity but rather **information architecture** — how to gate nonstationary signals.
- **Neural networks for financial forecasting**: Prior work often struggles with nonstationarity and training instability; this paper identifies a concrete cause and solution.
- **Walk-forward backtesting rigor**: Aligns with best practices in quantitative finance to avoid overfitting bias.

## Practical Applications
1. **Volatility-targeting hedge funds**: Dynamic position sizing based on next-period volatility forecasts; more accurate cross-sectional volatility means better risk budgeting across a portfolio.
2. **Options market makers**: Improved volatility surface estimation for pricing and Greeks calculation; more stable forecasts reduce hedging costs.
3. **Risk management systems**: Banks and asset managers use volatility forecasts for Value-at-Risk (VaR) and stress testing; the paper shows RG-ResMoE improves VaR calibration.
4. **Algorithmic execution**: Optimal execution algorithms use volatility forecasts; better predictions reduce market impact costs.
5. **Index rebalancing**: Low-latency regime detection and volatility prediction can improve timing of portfolio rebalancing.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Rolling walk-forward is computationally expensive**: Retraining the model every 5 days across 1,000+ assets limits real-time deployment and hyperparameter exploration; unclear how results scale to high-frequency or minute-level forecasting.

2. **Regime definition is external and manual**: The paper uses unspecified "regime state variables" (likely VIX, yield curves, etc.); results may be sensitive to regime definition. No ablation on what regime features matter.

3. **Cross-sectional only**: Forecasts individual asset volatility but not portfolio volatility or correlation; doesn't capture how volatility co-moves across stocks.

4. **Limited to linear stock features**: The paper doesn't say what the base predictor's input features are; if they're simple (e.g., lagged returns, turnover), the model may underperform on richer feature sets (alternative data, sentiment, graphs).

5. **Regime routing vs. feature interaction**: The routing gate learns linear combinations of regime variables; unclear if this is expressive enough for complex regime-dependent dynamics, or if more sophisticated gating (e.g., attention) would help.

6. **Data leakage risk in walk-forward**: Hyperparameter tuning procedure not fully detailed; if tuned on recent data, results may be optimistic.

7. **No transaction costs**: Backtests assume costless trading; in practice, frequent rebalancing based on volatility forecasts incurs fees that could erase alpha.

## Open Questions
1. **What exactly are the regime variables?** The paper is vague; ablation showing which regime features drive the routing decision would strengthen claims.

2. **How does RG-ResMoE perform on longer horizons** (20-day, monthly volatility)? Does regime matter more at longer scales?

3. **Can attention-based gating outperform the simple linear gate?** Could a learned, query-dependent gate (like in transformers) route more intelligently?

4. **Generalization across asset classes**: Do results hold for bonds, FX, commodities? Regime nonstationarity may be different.

5. **Why does hard routing underperform soft routing?** Mechanistic analysis (e.g., gradient flow, training dynamics) would clarify the information-theoretic reason.

6. **Can regime information be learned end-to-end** rather than provided exogenously? Would a latent regime variable learned from data work?

7. **Transfer learning**: Can a model trained on U.S. equities be fine-tuned to other markets, or does regime structure differ enough to require retraining?

8. **Extreme regimes**: How does the model perform during financial crises (2020 COVID crash, 2008 GFC)? Does regime-aware routing help or hurt in tail events?

## Key Takeaway
Neural volatility forecasts stabilize and improve when regime information gates expert routing rather than feeding directly into predictions.
