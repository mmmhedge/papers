# Language Has Two Parameters: Narrative-Induced Semantic Plasticity and Phase-Sensitive Interpretation

**Authors:** Hollis Robbins
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18041v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18041v1)

## Summary

This paper proposes that language understanding requires two parameters: amplitude (word co-occurrence strength, what embeddings capture) and phase (how coactivated meanings relate and combine). The author argues that current transformers and interpretability work ignore phase—a relational parameter indexed to individuals and dyads—which is essential for irony, allusion, and quotation. The work frames this formally using quantum probability notation and makes testable predictions about suppressed meanings and encounter order.

## Theoretical Background

Language models—especially [[Transformers]] (neural networks using attention mechanisms to process sequences)—typically learn word embeddings that capture *amplitude*: how strongly words co-occur in a corpus. The [[mechanistic-interpretability]] program has focused on finding "monosemantic" neurons (ones that fire for a single concept), treating polysemy (words with multiple meanings) as a defect to eliminate. This work builds on classical semantic theory distinguishing between stable word meanings and their pragmatic use in context. It borrows the *phase* concept from quantum mechanics as a mathematical formalism for representing how multiple active meanings interfere with one another—a signed, relational parameter separate from their individual strengths.

## Core Contributions

1. **Two-parameter model of semantics**: Distinguishes amplitude (familiar from embeddings) from phase (novel), where phase determines *how* coactivated meanings combine, potentially reversing contributions without eliminating them.

2. **Agent and dyad indexing**: Claims phase is not a corpus-level property but indexed to individuals and pairs—averaging across a population erases phase relations, so corpora cannot supply it.

3. **Critique of monosemanticity**: Argues the interpretability goal of finding single meanings per neuron optimizes against the coexistence required for language phenomena like irony and quotation.

4. **Six testable predictions**: Including whether suppressed meanings remain active, whether encounter order matters, whether signal marking changes interpretation, and whether models with history are changed versus merely informed by it.

5. **Architectural requirement**: Proposes language models must have agent-indexed, phase-bearing semantic states, not just frozen embeddings.

## Connections to Other Work

This work critiques core assumptions in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] (especially the monosemanticity objective) and extends classical work on polysemy and pragmatics. It relates to multi-agent communication and context-dependent interpretation in [[multi-agent-systems]], though not developed here. The use of quantum probability echoes work in cognitive science (e.g., Aerts, Gabora) but the author explicitly disclaims commitment to quantum processes in the brain—it's a mathematical notation. The emphasis on history and encounter order connects to temporal dynamics in language, underdeveloped in standard [[transformers]].

## Practical Applications

- **Irony and sarcasm detection**: Systems that track phase relations could disambiguate when a suppressed literal meaning is actively invoked by irony.
- **Machine translation**: Preserving phase-bearing meanings across languages, not just amplitude, could improve translation of allusive or quoted material.
- **Dialogue systems**: Chatbots that maintain agent-indexed phase states could track shared history and intra-dyadic context, avoiding contradictions across turns.
- **Literary analysis tools**: Automated systems that detect allusion and quotation by identifying when meanings coexist despite surface form.
- **Psycholinguistic modeling**: More faithful simulation of how humans reactivate suppressed meanings in context.

## Technical Limitations

- **No empirical demonstration**: The paper makes a theoretical case but provides no trained models, benchmark results, or quantitative validation of the six predictions.
- **Unclear how to compute phase in practice**: Quantum probability notation is suggestive but the paper does not specify a concrete algorithm for learning or representing phase from data.
- **Corpus-level impossibility asserted but not proven**: The claim that averaging erases phase is intuitive but not rigorously demonstrated; the inference problem of recovering agent-indexed phase from population data is not solved.
- **Transformer compatibility unclear**: While the paper identifies frozen inference as an obstacle, it does not show how to modify attention, layer normalization, or other mechanisms to carry phase.
- **Encounter order predictions untested**: The strong claim requires deriving an encounter-order constraint the author admits has not yet been done.
- **Philosophical gap**: Relying on quantum probability metaphor risks conflating mathematical elegance with cognitive reality; the explanatory power over simpler context models is not demonstrated.

## Open Questions

1. Can you train a transformer variant to learn and maintain phase-bearing states? What loss function would work?
2. Do the six predictions hold empirically? Design controlled experiments isolating each one.
3. How does phase interact with attention? Should it be in the attention weights, key–value pairs, or a separate mechanism?
4. Can you recover individual or dyadic phase from a corpus using latent variable inference, or is it truly inaccessible?
5. Does phase improve performance on tasks requiring irony, allusion, or sarcasm detection compared to amplitude-only models?
6. How do agent-indexed states scale to large populations and real-world dialogue without intractable personalization?

## Key Takeaway

Language interpretation requires a second relational parameter beyond word-association strength; current transformers lack it and interpretability work may be optimizing against it.
