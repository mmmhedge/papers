# Two Models of Event Finality: Functional Alignment, Contestability, and Empirical Comparability on Polymarket and Kalshi

**Authors:** Maksym Nechepurenko
**Published:** 2026-09-17 | **arXiv:** [2610.00183v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00183v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental infrastructure problem in decentralized prediction markets: different platforms (Polymarket and Kalshi) define "finality" — when an event contract is economically settled — through incompatible institutional mechanisms, making cross-venue comparison and aggregation impossible. The authors develop a formal framework to identify what conditions must align for duration comparisons to be meaningful, then empirically map the finality pathways on both platforms and identify where exact matching fails.

## Theoretical Background
Prediction markets depend on oracles — trusted entities or mechanisms that resolve events and trigger payouts. Traditional finance has long studied settlement procedures and finality; in blockchain and decentralized systems, finality (the point at which a transaction becomes irreversible) is a critical design concern. This paper extends finality concepts to event contracts by recognizing that "finality" is not a single moment but a sequence of functional stages: determination (oracle declares outcome), endpoint (contract records that outcome), and settlement (funds transfer). Prior work on market microstructure examines order execution and clearing; this paper applies similar precision to the oracle-to-settlement pipeline.

## Core Contributions
1. **Mechanism-aware comparison certificate**: Proposes that payoff semantics, endpoint functions, calendar support, censoring rules, observation grades, and dependence units must all align before duration comparisons across venues are valid—showing that naive label matching ("resolved," "settled," "finalized") obscures fundamental differences.

2. **Formal taxonomy of finality stages**: Maps Polymarket's five-stage path (oracle adjudication → adapter consumption → payout recording → technical redeemability → optional redemption) and Kalshi's four-stage path (determination → public finalization → lifecycle messages → REST settlement) as distinct institutional routes.

3. **Large-scale empirical inventory**: Documents exact finality paths across 108,638 Polymarket conditions and 7,611,594 Kalshi MVE (multi-valued event) objects, identifying what fraction of markets have complete, matched finality records (71,657 ordinary Kalshi markets have exact public endpoints).

4. **Identification of measurement gaps**: Reveals that REST endpoint completeness can be high even when exact lifecycle traces are sparse, and that current Kalshi timer fields do not preserve historical version-consistent rules.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to broader concerns in [[market-microstructure]] about **settlement risk** and **operational finality**. In traditional finance, settlement finality (e.g., T+2 in equities) is standardized; here, the decentralized nature of oracle-based contracts creates multiple finality definitions. The paper echoes concerns in distributed systems about consensus finality (e.g., Nakamoto vs. absolute finality in proof-of-stake). It also connects to [[risk-management]] literature on counterparty and liquidity risk — if two platforms have different finality horizons, hedging across them becomes risky. The empirical methodology mirrors efforts to standardize **comparable endpoints** in cross-exchange venue analysis.

## Practical Applications
1. **Cross-venue portfolio hedging**: Traders holding the same event bet on both Polymarket and Kalshi need to know when both legs settle identically; misaligned finality creates unwanted duration mismatch and liquidity drag.

2. **Market aggregation and pricing**: AMM (automated market maker) or prediction aggregation platforms that pool liquidity from multiple venues must reconcile finality differences to avoid orphaned liquidity or settlement disputes.

3. **Smart contract automation**: Bots that automatically settle derivative or hedge positions when a prediction resolves need a unified finality definition across platforms, or they risk transacting at the wrong time.

4. **Regulatory reporting**: Compliance teams reporting to regulators need consistent event-to-settlement timelines; incompatible finality definitions complicate position reconciliation and audit trails.

5. **Oracle service comparison**: Users selecting which oracle service to use for their own event contracts can use the framework to understand true settlement speed and risk.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Cross-venue finality router**: A middleware service that detects finality alignment across Polymarket, Kalshi, and other platforms, alerting traders when paired bets are ready to settle and capturing arbitrage from finality mismatches.

2. **Prediction market data warehouse**: Standardized API normalizing finality across venues, selling clean time-series and settlement metadata to quants and compliance teams.

3. **Smart contract finality validator**: Automated on-chain oracle that monitors both Polymarket and Kalshi and only triggers downstream smart contract actions when both venues have reached aligned finality, reducing settlement risk for derivatives.

4. **Finality-aware prediction aggregator**: A DAO or protocol that rebalances liquidity pools only when true finality is achieved across constituent markets, reducing impermanent loss from late or disputed settlements.

5. **Institutional settlement reconciliation tool**: SaaS for hedge funds and traders to automatically reconcile positions across venues, flag finality mismatches, and generate audit-ready settlement reports.

## Technical Limitations
1. **No numerical cross-venue comparison yet**: The paper explicitly states cross-venue duration comparison is blocked pending future work (Paper 7.3); this makes it impossible to say whether Polymarket actually settles faster than Kalshi in wall-clock time.

2. **Sparse exact lifecycle data**: REST endpoint completeness does not guarantee you know what happened *between* finality stage transitions; gaps in recorded lifecycle messages mean inferred durations may be biased.

3. **Timer field ambiguity**: Kalshi's MVE timer fields do not record historical version changes, so if the platform's settlement logic changed mid-observation period, measured durations are confounded.

4. **Event matching incomplete**: The definition of "the same event" across venues is unresolved; two markets labeled identically may have different payoff semantics (e.g., rounding, tie-breaking), invalidating paired comparisons.

5. **Causality and censoring unmeasured**: The paper identifies censoring and observation grades as confounders but does not quantify their impact; markets that fail to resolve or are delisted are not systematically characterized.

## Open Questions
1. **Blind decidability clocks**: What properties must a finality timestamp have to be comparable across venues that may use different time references or clock sources? Can you design a vendor-neutral finality marker?

2. **True latency distributions**: Once exact finality paths are matched, what are the actual duration distributions? Do they differ by event type, market size, or dispute likelihood?

3. **Settlement disputes and reversals**: How often does a finality endpoint get revised after initial declaration? What fraction of markets undergo multiple settlement cycles, and how should that factor into latency estimates?

4. **Semantic equivalence of outcomes**: Can you automatically verify that Polymarket and Kalshi markets are measuring the same underlying event, or are manual event registries required?

5. **Incentive alignment**: Do platform incentives (e.g., faster settlement to reduce counterparty risk) drive observable differences in finality stage duration, and can you quantify the trade-off between speed and accuracy?

## Key Takeaway
Different prediction markets settle events through incompatible institutional paths, making accurate comparison impossible until finality stages are mechanically aligned.
