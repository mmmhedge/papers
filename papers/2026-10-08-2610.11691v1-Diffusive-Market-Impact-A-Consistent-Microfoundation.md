# Diffusive Market Impact: A Consistent Microfoundation

**Authors:** Julius F. Bonart
**Published:** 2026-10-08 | **arXiv:** [2610.11691v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.11691v1)

## Summary
This paper provides a rigorous theoretical foundation for how large trades move market prices — a phenomenon called market impact. The author shows that when many arbitrageurs compete to eliminate predictable returns, a trader's price movements follow specific mathematical patterns (including the empirical "square-root law"), and develops a model where impact decays over time in predictable ways tied to the trader's own trading schedule.

## Theoretical Background
Market impact refers to the permanent price change caused by executing a large trade. Empirically, practitioners have observed that price impact follows a square-root relationship with trade size — a pattern called the "square-root law" — but the theoretical reasons have been unclear. This paper builds on structural price diffusivity, a framework treating price changes as a stochastic process, and microfounds it by modeling multi-agent competition. The key innovation is explicitly modeling arbitrageurs (market participants who profit from price discrepancies) who each observe partial information about fundamental value and progressively eliminate predictable profit opportunities. The trader's impact is then characterized through a causal all-pass filter — a signal processing concept meaning the filter preserves signal energy but shifts phase relationships — ensuring that price impacts are "white" (unpredictable residuals) even though the underlying trading pattern is predictable.

## Core Contributions
1. **Microfoundation of the square-root law**: Derives the empirical square-root market impact relationship from first principles — a large trader plus many partially-informed arbitrageurs optimizing against predictable returns.

2. **Information-neutral impact characterization**: Shows that when arbitrageurs collectively possess complete information (as their numbers grow), a trader's impact becomes white noise with a specific impulse response function (the all-pass filter U).

3. **Impact decay bounds**: Quantifies impact relaxation (how quickly prices revert after a trade), predicting a decay confined to 50%–60% under simple flow models, and shows this is consistent with some (though not all) empirical estimates.

4. **Economic interpretation of the impact phase**: Argues that the all-pass filter with minimum distance from the underlying trade flow generator is economically meaningful because it minimally distorts the flow while guaranteeing positive execution costs.

5. **Long-term mean reversion**: Proposes that real markets deviate slightly from the ideal all-pass case, permitting full relaxation and weak long-term mean-reversion in the impact state.

## Practical Applications
1. **Optimal execution algorithms**: Trading desks can use the predicted impact decay rates to better schedule large order execution, knowing how quickly prices will revert and thus minimizing total costs.

2. **Risk assessment for institutional traders**: Portfolio managers executing large positions can estimate realistic price impact costs before entering trades, improving profit forecasts.

3. **Market impact prediction models**: Quants can build real-time impact estimators calibrated to the model's predictions of decay rates and white noise behavior, improving execution.

4. **Liquidity provision strategies**: Market makers can use impact decay predictions to better set bid-ask spreads when large trades hit the market, knowing how long the distortion persists.

5. **Regulatory circuit breaker design**: Regulators can use impact predictions to set appropriate trading halts and order size limits by understanding how impact evolves and when mean-reversion begins.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Model assumptions may not hold universally**: The paper assumes arbitrageurs have progressively better information and operate rationally. In real markets, some arbitrageurs may be irrational, undercapitalized, or risk-averse, violating these assumptions.

2. **Biexponential flow specification**: The simplest model (biexponential trade flow $L$) produces specific decay rates (50–60%), but the paper acknowledges more complex flows can yield different results. Empirically determining which flow model applies is non-trivial.

3. **Stationary limit assumption**: The analysis requires convergence to a stationary state, which assumes markets are not in regime shifts, stress periods, or structural breaks — questionable during crises.

4. **Partial information structure**: The model specifies that each arbitrageur observes only one component of fundamental return privately. Real information revelation is messier and less structured.

5. **Limited empirical validation**: The paper predicts impact decay bounds but acknowledges these are "lower than some empirical estimates." The mismatch suggests the model may miss important real-world friction or feedback mechanisms.

6. **No feedback on arbitrageur behavior**: The model does not endogenize how arbitrageur strategies adapt if they observe the trader's impact pattern, potentially creating strategic game-theoretic complications.

## Open Questions
1. **Can the model predict impact decay on individual stocks and assets?** The paper shows general bounds, but does calibration to specific assets' microstructure (tick size, typical trade size, volatility) improve prediction accuracy?

2. **How does the all-pass filter respond to regime changes or vol clusters?** The stationarity assumption may break during stress; does the model extend to time-varying impact?

3. **What determines the "distortion" of the impact phase in real markets?** The paper speculates that long-term tails are distorted, permitting mean-reversion, but provides no mechanism. Can this be measured or modeled?

4. **How does this framework extend to multi-asset or correlated trading?** Most real execution involves multiple securities; does the single-trader model scale?

5. **Can arbitrageur learning speed be endogenized?** How fast arbitrageurs reduce information asymmetry likely depends on incentives and technology; can the model jointly determine learning rates and impact decay?

6. **What is the empirical distribution of flow generators $L$ across real markets?** If different markets have different flow models, can we predict which will have 50–60% decay vs. other rates?

## Key Takeaway
Large trades move prices through a theoretically predictable filter-like mechanism that arbitrageurs' collective information structure creates.
