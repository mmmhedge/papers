# Reconstruction: A Blind Benchmark for Recovering Research Ideas from Pre-Publication Bibliographies

**Authors:** Shaolong Chen, Yanlin Fei, Nazhou Liu, Xinmiao Yu, Lei Li et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.16645v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16645v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces Reconstruction, a benchmark that tests whether language models can infer a published paper's core research idea by reading only its bibliography—without access to the paper itself or future literature. The authors find that single models struggle (3–15% success), but a multi-agent approach combining cross-model debate and tournament selection nearly triples performance to 23–42%, demonstrating that collaborative model reasoning can extract latent research intent from citation patterns alone.

## Theoretical Background
The work sits at the intersection of bibliometrics, information retrieval, and language model reasoning. It assumes research ideas are recoverable signals encoded in citation choices—a principle rooted in citation analysis literature. The benchmark builds on the observation that bibliography structure often encodes an author's intellectual genealogy and problem framing. The multi-agent component draws from recent work on [[ensemble methods]] and [[multi-agent debate]] where independent LLM perspectives are combined through structured voting or consensus mechanisms. The "Swiss tournament" selection method is borrowed from competitive bracketing—a way to rank hypotheses by repeated head-to-head matching without a single global judge.

## Core Contributions
1. **Reconstruction benchmark itself**: A large-scale, rigorously anti-leakage benchmark across 643 papers in six domains with temporal citation cutoffs and anonymous reference IDs—addressing a genuine gap in how we evaluate LLM reasoning over scholarly content.
2. **Multi-agent pipeline design**: A reference-only approach (no external web search) that chains cross-model review with Swiss tournament ranking, showing 2.4× improvement over single-model baselines without additional training.
3. **Empirical finding on collaborative reasoning**: Demonstration that LLM ensembles with structured tournament selection outperform individual frontier models, suggesting architectural patterns for collaborative inference.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[chain-of-thought prompting]] and [[self-consistency]] methods that improve reasoning through multiple passes, but applies them to inter-model collaboration rather than intra-model sampling. It connects to bibliography-driven scientific knowledge extraction (e.g., SPECTER embeddings, citation-graph neural networks) but tests a more challenging inverse problem: inferring direction from structure. The multi-agent debate component echoes recent work on LLM-based scientific review and [[Constitutional AI]], where multiple model voices negotiate toward consensus. The anti-leakage protocol contributes methodologically to benchmarking rigor—a concern increasingly central to generative model evaluation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Literature review automation**: Auto-generate initial research hypotheses from a reading list to accelerate grant proposal drafting or literature positioning.
2. **Research direction inference in preprints**: Flag emerging research directions from early-stage citations to alert communities to shifting trends.
3. **Citation policy & integrity**: Detect unusual citation patterns by comparing inferred ideas to stated abstracts—a signal for citation manipulation or over-citation fraud.
4. **Interdisciplinary research discovery**: Match research ideas inferred from domain A's bibliography to unsolved problems in domain B, suggesting novel collaboration opportunities.
5. **Peer review support**: Generate baseline interpretations of a paper's contribution from its references alone, supporting reviewers unfamiliar with the subfield.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ResearchLens** (B2B SaaS): Upload a bibliography or citation list; get AI-generated research hypotheses and lit-review summaries. Target: PhD students, grant writers, industry R&D.
2. **Citation Intelligence API**: Real-time inference of research direction from live citation patterns—for preprint servers, journals, or institutional repositories to surface novelty and cross-domain opportunity.
3. **ArXiv Trend Scout** (Subscription service): Weekly digest of inferred emerging research ideas across domains from new preprint bibliographies, with multi-agent consensus scoring for confidence.
4. **Academic Matchmaking Platform**: Use bibliography-to-idea inference to match researchers with complementary interests or to connect industrial problems to academic solutions without explicit abstracts.
5. **Funding Agency Tool**: Analyze grant proposal bibliographies to validate consistency between cited work and claimed novelty; flag over-citations or under-grounded claims as risk signals.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Modest absolute performance**: Even multi-agent pipelines achieve only ~42% at best; false negatives (ideas not recovered) remain high and could mislead downstream users.
2. **LLM judge dependency**: The "held-out ground-truth" is matched by a frozen LLM judge, which introduces its own biases and failure modes; no human validation of matches is reported.
3. **Bibliography-only signal**: Many important research ideas emerge from negative results, external constraints, or serendipity rather than pure intellectual genealogy—these are invisible in citations alone.
4. **Domain & temporal bias**: Performance likely varies sharply by field (e.g., better in ML/NLP where citations are explicit; worse in applied domains with tacit knowledge). No per-domain ablation is shown.
5. **Scalability of multi-agent pipeline**: The Swiss tournament requires O(n²) pairwise comparisons; computational cost at scale (thousands of papers) is not discussed.
6. **Frozen bibliography assumption**: Real-world papers are often written with incomplete draft bibliographies; the anti-leakage protocol may not generalize to live prediction scenarios.

## Open Questions
1. How much does performance improve with human-in-the-loop validation of LLM judge matches? Are the 23–42% figures stable under human spot-checking?
2. Can per-domain fine-tuning or domain-specific judge models raise recovery rates further, or is there a hard ceiling imposed by citation signals alone?
3. What is the causal contribution of each component (cross-model review vs. tournament selection) to the 2.4× lift? Would a simpler ensemble (e.g., majority vote) reach 80% of the gain?
4. How does performance degrade with smaller or noisier bibliographies (e.g., conference papers with 10–20 refs vs. dissertations with 200+)?
5. Can the benchmark be extended to infer *which citations matter most* for the core idea—a form of citation rationalization that could aid understanding?
6. How do results change if the LLM judge is replaced with a human panel, or with a learned ranking model trained on ground-truth idea–bibliography pairs?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs alone struggle to reverse-engineer research ideas from citations, but multi-agent debate and ranking achieve 2.4× improvement—showing structured collaboration over isolated reasoning.
