# Towards Computational Provenance: Carrying Causal-State Evidence in Generated Text

**Authors:** Benjamin Belay
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.16868v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16868v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether neural networks can embed hidden evidence of their internal computational states into generated text — evidence that remains detectable even when the final answer stays the same. The authors design controlled experiments where feed-forward networks and transformers must route through specific intermediate states to solve arithmetic tasks, then embed subtle statistical signatures of those states into their outputs. The work matters because it addresses a foundational question: can we verify *how* a language model arrived at an answer, not just *what* answer it gave?

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] — the project of understanding what happens inside neural networks — and causal inference. It relies on the observation that neural networks learn distributed representations in their hidden layers; the question is whether information about causally important internal states can be preserved in (and extracted from) the final generated text. The work treats language generation as a sequential process where each token emerges from internal computation, and asks whether that computation leaves detectable fingerprints. This connects to broader work on neural network transparency and the invertibility of model outputs — whether we can reason backwards from outputs to internal states.

## Core Contributions
1. **Proof of concept for computational provenance**: Demonstrates experimentally that causally relevant internal states can embed detectable statistical signatures into generated text, even when the semantic output is identical across different internal paths.
2. **Controlled architecture experiments**: Tests the idea in two settings — a modular feed-forward network (fully transparent) and transformers (more realistic) — using arithmetic as a domain where internal states are verifiable.
3. **Reproducibility across training runs**: Shows the effect holds across five independently trained feed-forward models and three independently trained transformers, suggesting it's not a quirk of single model instances.
4. **Negative result**: Linear probes fail to recover naturally learned intermediate states (without explicit embedding), clarifying that the effect requires deliberate architectural design or training intervention.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to several threads:
- **[[Mechanistic Interpretability]]**: Shares the goal of understanding internal computation, but focuses on embedding verifiable evidence *into outputs* rather than just probing hidden layers.
- **Causal abstraction and circuit analysis**: Complements work on identifying causal mechanisms inside networks by asking whether those mechanisms leave statistical traces in behavior.
- **AI transparency and alignment**: Connects to [[alignment]] concerns — if we can embed and verify internal states in outputs, this could support auditing and [[Mechanistic Interpretability]] of deployed models.
- **Contrast with [[RLHF]] and behavioral fine-tuning**: While RLHF shapes outputs to match preferences, this work asks whether outputs can carry deeper cryptographic/statistical evidence of internal computation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Model auditing and regulatory compliance**: Verify that a language model used a specific, approved reasoning pathway (e.g., safety check, calculation method) before generating sensitive outputs, useful in finance or healthcare.
2. **Debugging and failure analysis**: When a model generates an incorrect answer, embedded state evidence helps investigators trace which internal computation failed.
3. **Attribution and intellectual property**: Embed cryptographic proof that generated content came from a specific model or training run, useful for detecting model theft or unauthorized reuse.
4. **Chain-of-thought verification**: For reasoning tasks, embed evidence of intermediate reasoning steps so downstream systems can verify the logic is sound, not just the answer correct.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Model audit platform**: SaaS tool that instruments LLMs to embed computational provenance signatures, then audits generated outputs to verify they used approved internal pathways — target: regulated industries (finance, healthcare, law).
2. **Debugging-as-a-service for LLMs**: Offer forensic analysis of failed LLM outputs by recovering embedded state evidence and replaying the internal computation path that led to the error.
3. **Certified reasoning API**: A wrapper around language models that guarantees each output carries verifiable proof of which reasoning method was used (e.g., step-by-step vs. intuitive), with cryptographic verification.
4. **Model provenance blockchain**: A decentralized ledger recording embeddings of model identity and training lineage in every output, preventing model cloning and enabling provenance tracking at scale.
5. **Interactive debugging IDE for LLMs**: Developer tool that shows not just what the model outputted, but embedded evidence of *why* (which internal state was active), with visual traces of the computation path.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Requires architectural control**: The approach needs either access to model internals (to deliberately inject signatures) or fine-tuning on tasks with explicit intermediate states. It doesn't work on black-box off-the-shelf models.
2. **Scales poorly to complex, realistic tasks**: Tested only on arithmetic with two discrete, verifiable states. Real-world language understanding has many continuous, hard-to-authenticate internal states.
3. **Detection vs. robustness**: The embedded signatures are "subtle statistical patterns" — it's unclear how robust they are to paraphrasing, distillation, or adversarial output perturbation.
4. **Linear probes limit**: Linear probes only recovered signals when explicitly embedded; they failed on naturally learned intermediate states, suggesting the effect may not transfer without explicit training.
5. **Computational overhead**: Embedding and detecting provenance likely adds latency and memory cost; real-time applications may be impractical.
6. **Assumption of verifiable ground truth**: The method assumes you can independently verify which internal state "actually" occurred — this is hard for interpretable subcomponents in real models.

## Open Questions
1. **Scalability to natural language**: Can provenance embeddings survive in open-ended text generation, or do they only work in constrained domains like arithmetic?
2. **Adversarial robustness**: Can an adversary strip or forge embedded signatures through paraphrasing, distillation, or prompt injection?
3. **Continuous vs. discrete states**: Does this work for continuous intermediate representations (typical in modern transformers) or only for discrete, manually imposed states?
4. **Authentication mechanisms**: How would you authenticate which internal state "really" occurred in a deployed model without access to internals? Can this be made cryptographically sound?
5. **Interaction with RLHF and fine-tuning**: Do standard fine-tuning procedures (e.g., [[RLHF]]) destroy embedded provenance? How stable is it across multiple training regimes?
6. **Detection in real deployments**: How would you detect signatures in a real-world API response without explicit cooperation from the model provider?

## Key Takeaway
Subtle statistical patterns in text can reliably encode hidden information about which internal computational path a neural network took.
