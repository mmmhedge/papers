# MARC v1: An Open-Source Multi-Agent Framework for Clinical AI Reasoning and Coordination

**Authors:** Saisha Shetty, Satvik Tripathi, Austin Lin, Colin Zhao, Theodore Kim et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-13 | **arXiv:** [2608.13476v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13476v1)

## Summary
MARC is an open-source framework that replaces single-prompt LLM interactions with coordinated teams of specialized AI agents for clinical reasoning tasks. Instead of asking one model to do everything, it routes medical questions through extraction, reasoning, generation, and evaluation agents with traceable outputs—making it easier to find where things go wrong and giving clinicians more interpretable, auditable AI decisions.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on two foundational ideas: (1) **multi-agent orchestration** — the principle that complex reasoning tasks benefit from decomposition into specialized agents rather than monolithic models, and (2) **interpretability through modularity** — the observation that explicit intermediate outputs and role separation make AI decision-making more transparent than end-to-end black boxes. The framework assumes that clinical reasoning naturally decomposes into discrete stages (information extraction, reasoning logic, answer synthesis, quality checking) and that deterministic task routing (not learned policies) is appropriate for safety-critical domains. It also leverages the flexibility of large language models (LLMs) — neural networks trained on vast text data that can follow instructions across domains — while accepting their brittleness and opacity.

## Core Contributions
1. **Multi-agent orchestration for clinical AI**: A deterministic, stage-wise pipeline replacing monolithic prompting, with explicit context passing between agents and traceable failure attribution at each stage.
2. **Decomposer module**: Automated generation of task-specific agent prompts from natural-language task descriptions, eliminating manual prompt engineering—a significant usability win for non-ML practitioners.
3. **Full interpretability stack**: Intermediate outputs at every stage, role-specialized prompts, and YAML-based configuration that requires no code changes, making the framework accessible to clinicians without programming expertise.
4. **Deployment flexibility**: Support for both cloud (API-based) and on-device (local CPU) execution, addressing privacy and accessibility constraints in healthcare.

## Connections to Other Work
MARC relates to several threads:
- **Chain-of-thought prompting** and **step-by-step reasoning** in LLMs, but with explicit orchestration rather than relying on the model to organize its own thoughts.
- **Multi-agent systems** literature (e.g., agent coordination protocols), adapted here for synchronous, deterministic pipelines rather than asynchronous negotiation.
- **Interpretability and explainability** in clinical AI — compared to [[mechanistic-interpretability]] work on understanding model internals, MARC achieves interpretability through compositional design.
- **Prompt engineering** automation — similar spirit to work on [[in-context learning]] and meta-prompting, but domain-specific and human-centric rather than fully learned.
- **AI alignment** concerns in high-stakes domains: by making reasoning stages explicit and auditable, MARC reduces the risk of hidden model failures.

## Practical Applications
1. **Clinical decision support**: Diagnostic reasoning (e.g., differential diagnosis generation from patient histories) with traceable intermediate steps for clinician review and liability documentation.
2. **Medical record analysis and abstraction**: Extracting structured data (medications, procedures, risk factors) from unstructured notes with clear audit trails.
3. **Clinical trial matching**: Parsing patient records and matching against inclusion/exclusion criteria with verifiable reasoning steps rather than opaque similarity scores.
4. **Peer review and quality assurance**: Generating evidence-based summaries of clinical decisions (treatment recommendations, contraindication checks) with intermediate reasoning visible for human verification.
5. **Regulatory compliance**: Generating documentation and summaries (e.g., discharge notes, prior authorization justifications) with explicit traceability for auditors and payers.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Clinical reasoning audit platform**: A SaaS product wrapping MARC that hospitals use to validate and log AI-assisted diagnoses and treatment plans, generating compliance reports for regulators and insurers.
2. **No-code clinical AI builder**: A low-code interface (drag-and-drop agent workflows, templated prompts) for healthcare institutions to build their own specialized reasoning systems (radiology summaries, lab result interpretation, triage) without ML engineers.
3. **Interpretable AI scoring for insurance/underwriting**: Adapt MARC for claims processing or coverage decisions, where explainability is legally mandated and competitors offer opaque black boxes.
4. **Clinical documentation assistant**: A real-time tool that watches a clinician's workflows (EHR, imaging), runs MARC reasoning agents asynchronously, and suggests structured notes, diagnoses, and next steps—with all reasoning steps visible for editing.
5. **Multi-agent LLM hosting for regulated industries**: A managed platform (similar to Anthropic's Bedrock or AWS SageMaker) that bakes in multi-agent orchestration, audit logging, and interpretability by default—targeting finance, law, and healthcare where single-model solutions face adoption friction.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Deterministic routing assumes decomposability**: Some clinical reasoning is non-linear (iterative hypothesis refinement, sudden pivots based on new lab results). Hard-coded agent pipelines may be too rigid.
2. **Quality depends on prompt quality**: Automating prompt generation (Decomposer) still relies on good natural-language descriptions. Garbage in → garbage out; unclear task specs could yield useless agent prompts.
3. **No learning or adaptation**: The system is stateless across cases. It cannot improve from feedback or learn idiosyncratic hospital workflows unless manually updated.
4. **Scalability to very large contexts**: Clinical documents (years of notes, imaging reports, genomics data) can be massive. The paper doesn't address how the framework handles context-window limitations or retrieval-augmented generation at scale.
5. **Model-agnostic claim is partial**: While the framework can swap LLM backends, performance and safety vary widely by model. No principled method to ensure all model families preserve the reasoning structure.
6. **Evaluation is limited**: The abstract and excerpt don't detail benchmarks, clinical validation, or comparison to monolithic prompting on real cases—only framework design and usability claims.
7. **Role confusion in edge cases**: If a case doesn't neatly fit the extraction→reasoning→generation→evaluation pipeline (e.g., meta-questions about agent confidence or counterfactual reasoning), the framework may degrade to ask the agent to "figure it out."

## Open Questions
1. **How does MARC perform on adversarial or ambiguous cases** where the intended decomposition breaks down? Does it gracefully degrade, or fail loudly?
2. **Can the Decomposer learn from examples**, improving its prompt generation as it sees success/failure patterns across similar tasks?
3. **How does multi-agent orchestration compare to single-agent prompting in terms of latency, cost, and accuracy** on standardized clinical benchmarks (e.g., MedQA, USMLE exams)?
4. **Does explicit intermediate output reduce hallucinations or unsafe outputs**, or does it just make failures more transparent without preventing them?
5. **How should the framework handle real-time feedback or corrections from clinicians?** Can it update agent behavior mid-case or across cases without redeploying?
6. **What is the minimal level of domain expertise required to design effective agent roles** for a new clinical task? Can a clinician with no ML background do it?
7. **How does MARC integrate with existing EHR systems and clinical workflows** (latency, security, data residency)?

## Key Takeaway
Decomposing clinical reasoning into specialized agents and explicit steps makes AI explanations auditable and failures traceable, not just faster.
