# When Do Intrinsic Rewards Lead to Exploration?

**Authors:** Scott W. Viteri, Laura Gomezjurado Gonzalez, Clark Barrett
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02159v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02159v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates when intrinsic reward signals—like prediction error or empowerment—actually lead to good exploration in reinforcement learning agents. The authors show that maximizing common intrinsic rewards can produce suboptimal exploration, propose a formal criterion based on "counterfactual information" (how well an agent's history substitutes for experience under other policies), and identify conditions where existing rewards succeed or fail.

## Theoretical Background
Reinforcement learning agents learn to maximize rewards through trial and error. "Intrinsic rewards" are bonuses given for interesting experiences (not tied to the task)—examples include curiosity-driven learning (reward for prediction errors), empowerment (reward for gaining control), and information-gain objectives. The challenge is that maximizing any single intrinsic reward does not necessarily produce the most useful learning signal. This work grounds exploration quality in information theory: specifically, how well an agent's collected experience can inform decisions under counterfactual scenarios (what would happen if the agent followed a different policy instead).

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal exploration criterion**: A principled way to evaluate exploration policies using counterfactual information—comparing how well agent histories can substitute for direct experience under alternative policies, rather than just raw information gained.
2. **Failure cases**: Concrete counterexamples (a single constructed environment) where count-based exploration, prediction-error curiosity, empowerment, and information-gain objectives all produce Pareto-suboptimal policies under this criterion.
3. **Characterization of success conditions**: Identifies specific structural conditions under which existing intrinsic rewards do encourage optimal exploration.
4. **New objective**: Proposes an alternative intrinsic reward that assigns value precisely when exploration improves under the counterfactual information criterion.

## Connections to Other Work
This addresses fundamental tensions in exploration research (e.g., [[curiosity-driven learning]], [[empowerment]], [[intrinsic motivation]]) by formalizing what "good exploration" actually means. It relates to prior work on information-theoretic foundations of RL and the broader question of how to align intrinsic reward design with actual learning efficiency—similar in spirit to concerns in [[RLHF]] and [[alignment]] about proxy reward misspecification, but in the exploration rather than task-reward setting.

## Practical Applications
- **Sample-efficient RL in sparse-reward environments**: Better exploration guidance could reduce the number of trial-and-error steps needed to solve hard exploration problems (e.g., robotics, game-playing).
- **Curriculum learning design**: Improved understanding of which intrinsic rewards truly improve learning could inform how to structure learning curricula.
- **Intrinsic motivation in embodied AI**: Robots and agents operating in complex, partially observed environments would benefit from exploration that genuinely increases decision-making capability rather than just novelty.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Exploration-as-a-service platform**: A framework that automatically selects or tunes intrinsic reward objectives for RL agents based on domain characteristics, validated against the counterfactual information criterion.
2. **Data-efficient RL toolkit for robotics**: A library that applies principled exploration rewards to robot learning, reducing real-world training time and hardware wear.
3. **Game AI with adaptive curiosity**: Game AI systems that explore environments intelligently (not just chasing high prediction error), improving NPC behavior and procedural content discovery.
4. **Safety-aware exploration module**: Intrinsic reward system for autonomous systems that explores efficiently while respecting safety constraints—useful for autonomous vehicles or industrial automation.
5. **Research intelligence platform**: Tool that benchmarks RL exploration strategies against the counterfactual information criterion, helping researchers identify which curiosity mechanisms work for which problem classes.

## Technical Limitations
- **Counterfactual information is hard to compute**: The paper's criterion requires evaluating how well an agent's history would inform decisions under alternative policies—this is computationally expensive and may not scale to high-dimensional state spaces.
- **Single toy environment**: The failure cases are demonstrated in one constructed environment; it remains unclear how prevalent these failures are in realistic, complex domains.
- **No empirical comparison on standard benchmarks**: The paper does not compare the proposed objective to existing curiosity methods on standard RL benchmarks (Atari, continuous control, etc.), making practical impact unclear.
- **Assumes stationarity and access to counterfactuals**: The criterion assumes the ability to reason about alternative policies; in non-stationary or adversarial settings, this may not hold.
- **Limited scalability analysis**: No discussion of how the approach scales to large state/action spaces or high-dimensional observations common in modern RL.

## Open Questions
1. How does the counterfactual information criterion perform on standard RL benchmarks (e.g., Atari, MuJoCo) compared to count-based, curiosity, and empowerment baselines?
2. Can the criterion be approximated efficiently for large-scale problems, or is it fundamentally limited to tabular / small domains?
3. Do the success conditions identified in the paper apply broadly across problem classes, or are they problem-specific?
4. How does the proposed objective interact with task rewards? Does it remain beneficial when combined with extrinsic rewards, or do task signals override its exploration benefits?
5. Can the theory extend to multi-agent settings where agents' explorations interact?

## Key Takeaway
Intrinsic reward signals often fail at true exploration; a new criterion based on counterfactual information formalizes when they succeed.
