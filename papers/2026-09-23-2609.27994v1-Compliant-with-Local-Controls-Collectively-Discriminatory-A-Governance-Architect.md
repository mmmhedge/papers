# Compliant with Local Controls, Collectively Discriminatory. A Governance Architecture for Multi-Agent AI in Regulated Finance

**Authors:** Jose Manuel de la Chica Rodriguez, Juan Manuel Vera Diaz, Pablo Delgado Romero
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.27994v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27994v1)

## Summary
This paper identifies a critical governance gap in financial institutions deploying multi-agent AI systems: locally compliant components can collectively produce harmful outcomes like discriminatory lending or market abuse. The authors propose ARIA, a reference architecture that monitors and controls agent populations as a whole, not just individual components, and map it to regulatory requirements like fair-lending rules and the EU AI Act.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two conceptual pillars. First, **multi-agent systems governance** — the challenge of coordinating many autonomous agents whose individual behaviors are predictable but joint behaviors are not. Second, **constitutional non-compositionality** — a new term the authors introduce to describe the failure of local compliance to guarantee global compliance. This echoes long-standing concerns in financial regulation about systemic risk: individual institutions can be solvent while the system becomes fragile. The work also draws on fair-lending law (which targets disparate impact across populations, not individual decisions) and model-risk management frameworks, which typically audit models in isolation.

## Core Contributions
1. **Naming the problem**: Formalizes "constitutional non-compositionality" as the core governance failure — showing why component-level testing is insufficient.
2. **ARIA architecture**: A six-capability framework organized across three planes (normative-accountability, execution-control, assurance-learning) that treats agent populations as the unit of control.
3. **Population-level monitoring (M2)**: A specific contribution showing how to detect collective misbehavior via observed-versus-expected distributional analysis, with a concrete example catching drift earlier than component-level alerts.
4. **Regulatory mapping**: Explicitly connects ARIA controls to fair-lending rules, EU AI Act Articles, model-risk governance, and conduct supervision — making the proposal actionable for compliance teams.
5. **Simulation validation**: Two concrete scenarios (thin-file exclusion, constructed drift) demonstrate the method's ability to catch problems invisible to local controls.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent-systems]] governance and financial regulation. ## Practical Applications
1. **Credit decisioning oversight**: Banks deploying multi-agent pipelines (risk scorer + policy engine + final decision agent) can use M2 monitoring to catch unintended disparate impact across demographic groups in real time, before hitting regulators.
2. **Fraud detection governance**: Multiple fraud detection agents (transaction-level, behavioral, network-based) can be monitored collectively to ensure they don't collude to block legitimate high-risk customers systematically.
3. **Collections workflow control**: When debt collection agents operate across regions with different compliance regimes, bounded authority and runtime containment prevent local agents from collectively exceeding fair practice thresholds.
4. **Regulatory reporting**: Institutions can generate audit trails and evidence of control that satisfy EU AI Act Article 17 (high-risk AI governance) by showing population-level monitoring and human oversight were active.
5. **Compliance automation at scale**: As institutions deploy hundreds of agents, ARIA provides the framework to scale human oversight from component-by-component review to population-level exception management.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ARIA-as-a-service governance platform**: A SaaS tool that ingests audit logs from an institution's agent ecosystem, runs M2 monitoring across populations, alerts on collective drift, and generates regulatory evidence — comparable to modern compliance platforms but agent-native.
2. **Fair-finance auditing service**: Specialized consulting + software for financial institutions to validate that their multi-agent credit and lending systems comply with fair-lending law at the population level, with ongoing monitoring and quarterly evidence reports.
3. **Agent-population simulator for regulators**: A simulation tool (for central banks, financial regulators) to test stress scenarios on reported agent ecosystems and detect systemic risks before they manifest.
4. **Real-time agent behavior dashboard**: An observability product for risk & compliance teams showing agent population metrics (disparate impact, authority usage, containment breaches) with alerting and drill-down tracing.
5. **EU AI Act compliance accelerator**: Preconfigured templates and audit playbooks for high-risk financial AI systems, mapped to ARIA and EU AI Act requirements, sold to compliance consultancies and enterprises.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Simulation fidelity**: The two simulations are constructed scenarios; real-world agent interactions are messier and may exhibit emergent behaviors not captured by the M2 monitoring framework.
2. **Definition of "collective harm"**: The paper doesn't fully specify how to define or quantify acceptable collective behavior — "bounded disparate impact" is intuitive but operationalizing thresholds is left to institutions.
3. **Scalability of human oversight**: The architecture assumes human competence to interpret agent behavior and override policies — but as agent populations grow, meaningful human oversight becomes a bottleneck, not addressed here.
4. **Adversarial evasion**: No discussion of whether agents could learn to exploit gaps between local controls and collective monitoring, or coordinate to hide collective misbehavior.
5. **Generalization across domains**: The architecture is tailored to finance (fair lending, market integrity); generalization to other regulated sectors (healthcare, hiring) is unclear.
6. **Real-time performance**: M2 monitoring may require live statistical analysis of large populations; computational and latency constraints in high-frequency trading or real-time lending are not discussed.

## Open Questions
1. **How does M2 monitoring degrade under distribution shift?** The paper shows one constructed drift scenario; what happens when the baseline distribution changes fundamentally (e.g., during a recession or market shock)?
2. **Can ARIA handle agent learning and adaptation?** If agents improve over time via reinforcement learning or online updates, how does population governance evolve — does policy need real-time retuning?
3. **What are minimal sufficient statistics for population-level monitoring?** Can you monitor collective behavior with compressed summaries, or must you see individual transaction-level data?
4. **How to balance bounded authority with agent effectiveness?** Stricter containment reduces systemic risk but may degrade business outcomes; what is the Pareto frontier?
5. **Can this framework scale to cross-institutional agent networks?** Financial consortia, interbank markets, and fintech ecosystems involve agents from many institutions — how would ARIA apply?
6. **What evidence would a regulator actually accept?** The paper maps to regulations but doesn't test whether demonstrated ARIA compliance would satisfy a real exam or audit.

## Key Takeaway
Local AI compliance doesn't guarantee safe collective behavior in finance; monitor and control agent populations as wholes, not components alone.
