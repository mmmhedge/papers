# No One Wins in Nuclear War: A Social Simulation of Military Decision-making

**Authors:** Glenn Matlin, Isaac Song, Anthony Wen-Ming Zang, Mark Riedl
**Published:** 2026-08-03 | **arXiv:** [2608.01868v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.01868v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces WOPR, a social-simulation environment for studying how organizations make high-stakes decisions under uncertainty, using the card game Nuclear War as a testbed. The system combines a verifiable rules engine (ensuring reproducibility) with large language model agents that negotiate, form coalitions, and make strategic choices—focusing on military decision-making because of its safety implications and the need to better understand how AI systems behave in conflict scenarios.

## Theoretical Background
Social simulation—using computational models to study how groups of agents interact and make collective decisions—has roots in agent-based modeling and multi-agent systems research. Prior work in this space (e.g., Concordia, a framework for building believable agent personas) has emphasized realistic character behavior and synthetic opinion-formation, but typically lacks two things: (1) a verifiable, rule-respecting game engine that can be replayed and validated, and (2) structured diplomatic channels where agents negotiate with explicit commitments rather than just expressing opinions. This paper builds on game theory and organizational decision-making, using wargames as the scaffolding—wargames are human-run strategic simulations used by militaries to explore scenarios and test plans.

## Core Contributions
1. **WOPR engine**: A deterministic, replay-validated rules engine that enforces game rules and exposes a clean decision-point contract so agents can choose actions without breaking game logic.
2. **Diplomatic layer**: A four-rung escalation ladder (silence → private channels → structured commitments) that allows agents to negotiate with each other in verifiable ways, moving beyond simple opinion expression.
3. **Collective command-and-control modeling**: Rather than treating each faction as a single agent, the system models them as organizations with internal hierarchy, improving realism for military decision-making.
4. **Instantiation on Nuclear War**: A complete, rules-verified implementation of the published Nuclear War card game, with all code and replay data open-sourced.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent-systems]], [[alignment]], and [[agents|agent behavior]]. It extends prior social-simulation work (Concordia framework) by adding formal verification and negotiation. The emphasis on safety and studying how AI systems behave in adversarial, high-stakes scenarios connects to broader [[alignment]] research—understanding emergent behavior in multi-agent competitive settings is crucial for AI safety. The use of wargames echoes military simulation traditions but applies them to AI, creating a new testbed for studying emergent strategic behavior.

## Practical Applications
1. **Military training and red-teaming**: Running simulations with AI agents to explore novel strategy options or stress-test plans before deployment.
2. **Policy analysis**: Simulating diplomatic and military crises (e.g., escalation in regional conflicts) to understand decision-making dynamics and unintended consequences.
3. **Organizational decision-support**: Extending WOPR to corporate or government settings (supply-chain disruptions, crisis management) where high-stakes collective decisions matter.
4. **Crisis communication**: Testing how organizations communicate under pressure, with applications to emergency response and disaster recovery.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **AI Wargaming as a Service**: A SaaS platform offering military, government, and large enterprises access to WOPR-like simulations with their own rules and scenarios, trained on domain-specific data.
2. **Strategic Scenario Explorer**: A consulting tool that runs multi-agent simulations of geopolitical or competitive business scenarios, generating reports on emergent strategies and failure modes.
3. **Negotiation & Diplomacy Trainer**: Software that lets human players negotiate with AI agents in structured diplomatic games, training diplomats and business negotiators.
4. **Risk-Assessment for Multi-stakeholder Systems**: A platform for critical infrastructure operators (power grids, financial networks) to simulate cascading failures and organizational responses under pressure.
5. **Open Research Toolkit**: A commercial platform packaging WOPR's engine for academic institutions and think tanks to build and share custom rule-based simulations.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Fidelity of agent reasoning**: LLM agents may hallucinate commitments, misunderstand game rules, or fail to plan strategically at the depth human players achieve. No explicit mechanism for learning or updating strategy across game instances.
2. **Scalability**: The paper focuses on a specific card game; it's unclear how well the approach scales to continuous-time military simulations or larger numbers of agents.
3. **Verification gaps**: While the rules engine is deterministic and replay-validated, there's no proof that agent actions comply with strategic intent or that negotiated commitments are actually honored during gameplay.
4. **Limited adversarial robustness**: No discussion of how agents behave against jailbreaking or deceptive opponents, which matters for safety-critical applications.
5. **Persona stability**: The paper doesn't address whether agent "personalities" remain consistent across multiple games or under cognitive load from complex scenarios.

## Open Questions
1. How do these simulations compare to human decision-making in the same scenarios? Can we validate that emergent behaviors are realistic?
2. What happens when agents are given conflicting objectives or face incentives to deceive? Do they escalate uncontrollably?
3. Can the system be extended to continuous-time or partially-observed games (where agents have incomplete information)?
4. How sensitive are the results to LLM choice, temperature, and prompting strategy? Is there a "sweet spot" for realism?
5. Can we extract causal insights from replays—e.g., which decision points were pivotal in escalation or de-escalation?

## Key Takeaway
AI agents in multi-player conflict games show realistic strategic behavior when constrained by verifiable rules and diplomatic channels—a testbed for understanding AI safety in high-stakes decision-making.
