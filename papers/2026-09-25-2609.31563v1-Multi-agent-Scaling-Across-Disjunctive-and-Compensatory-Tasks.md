# Multi-agent Scaling Across Disjunctive and Compensatory Tasks

**Authors:** Carolina Fortuna, Blaz Bertalanic
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31563v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31563v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how teams of large language models scale in performance as team size grows, finding that scaling depends critically on task structure. The authors use Steiner's taxonomy to distinguish disjunctive tasks (where one correct answer succeeds) from compensatory tasks (where averaging helps), discovering that standard aggregation methods like plurality voting and averaging often fail to capture the theoretical benefits of larger teams.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on Steiner's taxonomy of group tasks — a framework from social psychology that classifies tasks by how group members' contributions combine. Disjunctive tasks require only one member to succeed (e.g., "is this statement true?"), while compensatory tasks benefit from pooling estimates (e.g., forecasting). The authors model LLM agents as conditionally independent given an item, allowing them to derive theoretical large-team limits: plurality voting converges to the model's most likely answer, and averaging converges to its systematic bias. This connects to classical [[aggregation]] and [[ensemble methods]] in machine learning, but applied to language model outputs rather than learned feature representations.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal framework**: Apply Steiner's task taxonomy explicitly to multi-agent LLM systems, providing a structured way to predict when scaling helps.
2. **Theoretical limits**: Derive closed-form large-team behavior for plurality voting and averaging under conditional independence assumptions.
3. **Empirical mismatch**: Show that on disjunctive tasks, the theoretical improvement from larger teams (5-20 percentage points in raw correctness) is almost entirely lost by plurality voting, which gains only ~0.5 points.
4. **Task-dependent scaling**: Demonstrate that Fermi estimation (compensatory) shows minimal gains from averaging because 87% of error comes from shared item-level biases, not variance.
5. **Limited revision gains**: Multi-round peer revision helps accuracy on disjunctive tasks but plateaus after ~1 peer, even with 29 teammates.

## Connections to Other Work
This research relates to [[ensemble methods]], [[voting systems]], and [[ensemble diversity]] in traditional ML. It also touches on [[mixture of experts]] and [[model merging]] when discussing heterogeneous model families. The conditional independence assumption echoes classical [[wisdom of crowds]] theory but exposes why it fails for LLMs. The paper implicitly addresses [[scaling laws]] for multi-agent systems, complementing recent work on single-agent LLM scaling.

## Practical Applications
- **Customer support teams**: Routing disjunctive questions (factual lookup) vs. compensatory queries (open-ended advice) to appropriately-sized agent teams.
- **Content moderation**: Using teams of classifiers on disjunctive decisions (flag or pass) where one correct detection matters; avoid averaging for binary judgments.
- **Forecasting platforms**: Designing Fermi estimation workflows where cross-model ensembles beat single-model averaging, since different model families may have different biases.
- **Collaborative coding assistants**: Structuring multi-agent code generation around task type — majority vote for bug detection, selective averaging for API design proposals.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **TaskType-Aware LLM Orchestration**: A middleware layer that auto-detects task structure and routes to the optimal aggregation strategy (plurality voting vs. averaging vs. revision loops), billing by efficiency gain over baseline.
2. **Bias-Aware Ensemble Forecasting**: Plug-and-play service for enterprises doing Fermi estimation (demand planning, capacity forecasting) that decomposes error into bias and variance and recommends model combinations known to have orthogonal biases.
3. **Smart Team Sizing Assistant**: SaaS tool for enterprises using LLM agent teams that recommends team size and aggregation method per task class, with ROI calculator (cost of extra agents vs. accuracy gain).
4. **Revision-as-a-Service**: Managed service optimizing multi-round peer revision workflows for knowledge work, discovering that 1 peer is usually enough and avoiding the expense of 30-agent setups.
5. **Cross-Model Ensembling Engine**: Platform combining different open-weight model families (Llama, Mistral, Qwen, etc.) for compensatory tasks, trained to predict which combinations beat individual models on domain-specific benchmarks.

## Technical Limitations
- **Conditional independence assumption**: Assumes agents' errors are independent given the item; LLMs may have correlated biases (e.g., all trained on similar data, all prone to the same factual gaps).
- **Limited task diversity**: Analysis focuses on two task types; real-world task structure may be more nuanced or hybrid.
- **Small team caps**: Experiments go up to 30 agents; scaling behavior at 100+ or 1000+ agents is unexplored.
- **Model-specific findings**: Results use 13 open-weight models; proprietary LLMs (GPT-4, Claude) may have different bias and error profiles.
- **No computational cost modeling**: Does not weigh accuracy gains against inference cost of larger teams.
- **Revision mechanism unclear**: Multi-round revision described only loosely; implementation details may matter for reproducibility.

## Open Questions
- Do the observed scaling limits hold for fine-tuned or instruction-aligned models, or only base models?
- Can task structure be automatically detected from a task prompt, enabling dynamic routing at inference time?
- What mixing of disjunctive and compensatory elements in real tasks (e.g., "summarize this OR generate alternatives") changes the scaling curve?
- Does reinforcement learning or fine-tuning on multi-agent feedback create correlation between agents, breaking the conditional independence assumption?
- How do scaling limits change with different revision strategies — e.g., debate-style protocols or structured reasoning steps rather than simple re-sampling?
- Can we design aggregation functions that recover more of the theoretical gain on disjunctive tasks (e.g., learned weighting or confidence-aware voting)?

## Key Takeaway
Task structure, not just team size, determines whether multi-agent LLM systems scale: voting helps find one correct answer, but averaging rarely beats the strongest model alone.
