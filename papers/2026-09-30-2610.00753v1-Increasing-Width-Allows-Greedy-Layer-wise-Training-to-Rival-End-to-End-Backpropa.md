# Increasing Width Allows Greedy Layer-wise Training to Rival End-to-End Backpropagation in Self-Supervised Learning

**Authors:** Syon Mansur, Joel Zylberberg
**Published:** 2026-09-30 | **arXiv:** [2610.00753v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00753v1)

## Summary
This paper challenges the conventional wisdom that end-to-end backpropagation is necessary for training deep neural networks. The authors show that in sufficiently wide networks, greedy layer-wise training—where each layer optimizes locally without coordinating with other layers—can match or even outperform standard backpropagation in self-supervised learning tasks. The finding suggests that network width can compensate for the lack of global error coordination, opening new possibilities for alternative training algorithms that may be more biologically plausible or computationally efficient.

## Theoretical Background
Deep learning has traditionally relied on backpropagation, an algorithm that propagates error signals backwards through a network to update all parameters in a coordinated way. However, the "credit assignment problem"—figuring out which parameters contributed to successes or failures—can also be solved through local learning rules that only use information available at each layer. Prior work on greedy layer-wise training and biologically-inspired local learning rules has explored whether these simpler mechanisms can work, but success has been inconsistent and conditions for success unclear. This paper specifically examines self-supervised learning (pretraining methods that learn useful representations from unlabeled data without external labels), which has become central to modern large-scale AI systems like vision transformers and language models.

## Core Contributions
1. **Width-dependent regime identification**: Demonstrates empirically that network width is a critical factor determining when greedy layer-wise training becomes competitive with backpropagation—a finding not well-characterized in prior work.

2. **Self-supervised learning focus**: Extends the investigation of local learning rules specifically to self-supervised learning contexts, which differ importantly from supervised learning.

3. **Representational geometry analysis**: Identifies that very wide greedy-trained networks develop representationally superior geometry (how features organize in representation space) compared to backprop-trained networks, providing mechanistic insight into *why* width helps.

4. **Quantitative comparison**: Provides direct empirical evidence on convolutional networks showing that in shallow, very wide settings, greedy layer-wise training can actually outperform end-to-end backpropagation.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several research directions:

- **[[Local learning rules]]** and **biologically plausible learning**: Previous work on alternatives to backpropagation that respect biological constraints or locality assumptions.
- **[[Width and generalization]]**: Growing literature on how network width affects learning dynamics, generalization, and implicit regularization in neural networks.
- **[[Self-supervised learning]]**: Connects to recent scaling successes in SSL methods (contrastive learning, masked autoencoders) but with focus on training algorithm rather than objective function.
- **[[Neural scaling laws]]**: Related to understanding how architectural parameters (width, depth) interact with learning dynamics.
- **[[Feature learning and representation learning]]**: The representational geometry analysis connects to understanding what kinds of representations different training procedures induce.

## Practical Applications
1. **Neuromorphic computing**: Greedy layer-wise training requires only local information flow, making it more feasible to implement on neuromorphic hardware that lacks global backpropagation infrastructure.

2. **Federated learning**: Local learning rules could reduce communication overhead in distributed training scenarios where sending gradients between layers is expensive.

3. **Continual learning**: Greedy training may enable more efficient continual/lifelong learning where networks must adapt to new tasks without forgetting—local updates might prevent catastrophic interference more naturally.

4. **Energy-efficient edge AI**: Training on resource-constrained devices might benefit from algorithms requiring less memory overhead and fewer backward passes.

5. **Real-time learning systems**: Reduced coordination overhead could enable faster training iterations in time-critical applications.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited scope**: Evaluated primarily on convolutional networks with self-supervised objectives; unclear if findings generalize to transformers, RNNs, or other architectures.

2. **"Very wide" regime unclear**: Paper doesn't precisely define how wide is "very wide"—the threshold may be impractical for many real applications or dataset sizes.

3. **Depth penalty unexplored**: Results suggest greedy training struggles in deeper networks; no clear solution offered for scaling to very deep architectures.

4. **Representation geometry as proxy**: Analysis of "favorable representational geometry" is correlative; causality between geometry and performance is inferred but not rigorously established.

5. **Self-supervised only**: Findings limited to unsupervised representation learning; performance on downstream supervised tasks (fine-tuning) not thoroughly examined.

6. **Computational cost not analyzed**: No wall-clock time or actual computational efficiency comparisons provided; greedy training may require more total compute despite avoiding some backprop costs.

## Open Questions
1. **Transformer compatibility**: Do these findings hold for attention-based architectures, which dominate modern pretraining? The inductive biases of transformers may fundamentally change the relationship between width and credit assignment.

2. **Downstream task performance**: How do representations learned via greedy training transfer to supervised fine-tuning tasks? Self-supervised performance is necessary but may not be sufficient.

3. **Depth scaling**: Is there an architectural modification (skip connections, normalization schemes, etc.) that could make greedy training effective even in very deep networks?

4. **Theoretical characterization**: Can we develop theoretical conditions on network width, depth, and data properties that predict when greedy training will match backprop? Current findings are empirical.

5. **Hybrid approaches**: Can we interpolate between greedy and end-to-end training (e.g., greedy with occasional global synchronization) to get practical benefits across depth ranges?

6. **Biological learning**: If wide greedy networks work, do they suggest learning mechanisms in biological brains? How do findings relate to neuroscience evidence?

## Key Takeaway
Very wide neural networks can learn well using only local layer-wise updates, challenging the necessity of global backpropagation for self-supervised learning.
