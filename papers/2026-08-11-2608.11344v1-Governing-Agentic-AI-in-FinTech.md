# Governing Agentic AI in FinTech

**Authors:** Henry Han
**Published:** 2026-08-11 | **arXiv:** [2608.11344v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.11344v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates how to govern AI agents making consequential financial decisions — a problem largely ignored despite rapid deployment. The core insight is that the real constraint isn't whether AI systems are capable, but whether their decisions can be verified, audited, and reproduced. The authors introduce the "Verifiability Gap" and test whether different AI models (local, hosted, and frontier systems) can consistently replay the same financial decisions, finding that reproducibility varies dramatically by architecture and capability level.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on governance and accountability frameworks from financial regulation, combined with recent work on agentic AI systems — autonomous agents that decompose complex goals, coordinate multiple models and tools, and execute decisions with minimal human intervention. It engages with concepts from explainability (how transparent are decisions?) and reproducibility (can the same decision be recovered later?), which are longstanding challenges in interpretable machine learning. The framework treats agentic systems as fundamentally different from single-model inference: orchestration (how components interact) becomes a hidden policy layer that shapes outcomes independent of individual component capability.

## Core Contributions
1. **The Verifiability Gap framework**: A formal definition of the gap between the verification authority demands and the explainability/reproducibility actually retained. This is indexed to *who* verifies (the verifier), *what standard* (evidentiary standard), and *when* (audit lag).

2. **Empirical discovery that reproducibility is not scalar**: Across nine model versions, the paper shows that:
   - Provider model releases alter historical financial actions (breaking backward compatibility).
   - Architecture choices (not capability) determine whether decisions can be replayed.
   - Frontier models sometimes reproduce better than local ones, but their audit records remain equally opaque.
   - Deterministic models can reproduce current decisions perfectly yet fail to recover historical ones.

3. **Governance profile concept**: Rather than treating reproducibility as a single number, the authors propose reproducibility as a context-dependent governance profile tied to evidence retention, enabling "evidence-contingent delegation" where authority is defensible only while supporting evidence exists.

## Practical Applications
1. **Regulatory compliance for AI-driven trading**: Brokerages and hedge funds deploying agentic systems for portfolio rebalancing need audit trails; this framework clarifies what "reproducible" actually means in practice.

2. **Credit and lending automation**: Banks using agentic systems for loan approval must justify individual decisions to regulators; the paper shows current systems cannot reliably replay past decisions.

3. **Algorithmic risk monitoring**: Real-time surveillance of whether agentic AI in market-making, pricing, or settlement is behaving consistently with its approved policies.

4. **Historical reconstruction for disputes**: When a client disputes a trade, confirm whether the agent's decision can be reproduced under the same parameters.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **FinTech Agentic Audit SaaS**: A monitoring layer that sits between agentic AI systems and execution, continuously testing whether orchestration produces reproducible decisions and flagging when reproducibility degrades — sold to compliance teams at trading firms.

2. **AI Decision Replay Engine**: Infrastructure that captures full execution logs (including latent model state, tool calls, and parameter values) and allows regulators to replay any past decision, with guarantees on fidelity; target: wealth management and institutional trading.

3. **Governance Attestation Protocol**: A third-party certification service that tests a financial institution's agentic AI system against the Verifiability Gap framework and issues time-bound attestations (e.g., "this system achieved 98% reproducibility under full control set as of Q3 2026").

4. **Dynamic Authority Delegation Platform**: Software that automatically adjusts what decisions an agentic system is allowed to make based on real-time reproducibility metrics — if reproducibility drops below a threshold, authority caps lower until root cause is investigated.

5. **Model Snapshot Versioning for Finance**: A tool that freezes not just model weights but the entire execution environment (provider API version, random seed, temperature, orchestration graph) so historical decisions become permanently reproducible for audit purposes.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Reproducibility ≠ Correctness**: The paper tests whether decisions repeat, not whether they are good decisions. A perfectly reproducible wrong decision is still wrong.

2. **Provider opacity**: The paper shows frontier models hide critical controls (temperature, seed), making reproducibility impossible no matter what the user does. This is a business decision, not a solvable technical limitation.

3. **Orchestration complexity**: The paper shows that architecture changes erase reproducibility across all scales and configurations. This implies reproducibility requires *exact* environment replication, which becomes fragile as systems grow.

4. **Time-bound governance**: The framework acknowledges that evidence degrades or is deleted; "historical" decisions become unverifiable after evidence windows close. This creates a tension with long-term accountability.

5. **Limited scope of study**: All three studies focus on small decisions (credit decisions, financial actions). It's unclear whether the framework scales to system-level decisions (e.g., portfolio-wide risk reallocation by an agentic system).

6. **No latency-accuracy trade-off analysis**: The paper doesn't examine whether tightening reproducibility controls (e.g., locking all hyperparameters) makes decisions slower or less effective.

## Open Questions
1. **Can reproducibility be decoupled from capability?** Is there an architecture that retains frontier-model performance *while* exposing full control and auditability, or is there a hard trade-off?

2. **What is the optimal audit lag?** The Verifiability Gap is indexed to audit lag, but the paper doesn't characterize how long after a decision audit should occur before reproducibility becomes unrecoverable.

3. **How do we handle model drift in reproducibility?** When a provider updates a model, should institutions be required to maintain frozen versions for historical replay, or is that operationally untenable?

4. **Can governance profiles be standardized?** Could regulators (SEC, FINRA, ECB) codify acceptable reproducibility profiles for different decision types (e.g., 99% for trade execution, 95% for credit)?

5. **Does human oversight improve verifiability?** Does adding a human loop at the orchestration level (before final execution) improve reproducibility, or does it introduce additional variance?

6. **How do we extend this to continuous decisions?** All three studies involve discrete, point-in-time decisions. What happens with agentic systems that continuously rebalance or monitor?

## Key Takeaway
AI agents controlling money must make reproducible decisions; most cannot, because capability and auditability are decoupled.
