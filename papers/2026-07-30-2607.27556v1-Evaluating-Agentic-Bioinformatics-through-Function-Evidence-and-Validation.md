# Evaluating Agentic Bioinformatics through Function, Evidence, and Validation

**Authors:** Phuc Pham, Truong-Son Hy
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.27556v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27556v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes the Function–Evidence–Validation (FEV) framework to rigorously evaluate AI agents used in biological research, arguing that scientific credibility depends on inspectable workflows rather than final outputs alone. The authors map 109 agentic systems across genomics, protein science, and drug discovery, finding that most systems excel at planning and tool execution but lag in reproducibility, transparency, and empirical validation—a gap they address by recommending workflow-correctness as the primary assessment criterion.

## Theoretical Background
Large language model (LLM) agents — systems that use neural language models to plan and execute multi-step tasks using external tools — have proliferated in bioinformatics. Prior work has reviewed these systems by application domain, architecture type, and capability level, but has not systematically evaluated the *scientific accountability* of agent-generated analyses. The paper builds on established ideas from scientific reproducibility (provenance tracking, replayability) and software testing, arguing that biological workflows require not just correct final answers but traceable reasoning. The key insight is that for high-stakes scientific work, the journey (workflow trajectory) matters as much as the destination (final result).

## Core Contributions
1. **FEV Framework**: A three-part evaluation scheme separating (i) Function—does the agent perform the intended operations correctly?, (ii) Evidence—are actions and claims traceable to data and literature?, and (iii) Validation—does the workflow meet domain-specific scientific standards and external benchmarks?

2. **Systematic mapping**: Comprehensive census of 109 agentic or agent-adjacent bioinformatics systems and 28 evaluation resources across six biological domains (genomics, omics, protein science, drug discovery, pathology, general automation).

3. **Gap analysis**: Empirical finding that planning and tool execution are mature, but replayability, provenance logging, robust scientific assessment, external validation, and prospective testing remain weak across the surveyed systems.

4. **Reframing of success metrics**: Argument that "workflow correctness" (intermediate steps are sound) should supersede or complement "final-answer correctness" for scientific credibility.

## Practical Applications
1. **Regulatory compliance**: Biopharmaceutical companies running AI-assisted drug discovery pipelines can use FEV to document and audit workflows for FDA or EMA submissions.

2. **Laboratory automation**: Research institutes can evaluate whether agent-based protocols for genomics or microscopy are reproducible enough for peer review and independent replication.

3. **Clinical decision support**: Hospitals integrating LLM-agents into pathology or diagnostic workflows can verify that intermediate steps are justified by evidence, not just final diagnoses.

4. **Open-science repositories**: Platforms hosting computational biology workflows (e.g., Galaxy, CWL registries) can adopt FEV as a quality standard for agent-generated pipelines.

5. **Grant review and publication**: Journals and funders can use FEV criteria to assess whether agentic bioinformatics claims are scientifically sound.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Agentic Workflow Audit Platform**: SaaS tool that logs, visualizes, and validates bioinformatics agent workflows in real-time, generating provenance reports for regulatory submission or publication.

2. **FEV-Compliant Agent Studio**: IDE for building and testing bioinformatics agents with built-in Function–Evidence–Validation checkers and automated workflow replay for debugging.

3. **Scientific Credibility Dashboard**: Dashboard that scores existing bioinformatics tools and agent systems against FEV criteria, helping researchers select reliable pipelines for their projects.

4. **Agent Validation-as-a-Service**: Third-party service offering independent empirical validation and external benchmarking of client bioinformatics agents against gold-standard datasets.

5. **Automated Provenance Middleware**: Lightweight middleware layer that wraps LLM-agent calls in bioinformatics toolchains, automatically capturing evidence (citations, data lineage, parameter logs) without requiring system redesign.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Framework operationalization**: While FEV is conceptually clean, the paper does not provide detailed metrics or thresholds for each component, leaving ambiguity in how to quantify "sufficient evidence" or "robust validation" in practice.

2. **Domain specificity**: Validation criteria will vary widely across genomics, protein folding, and drug discovery; the framework may be too abstract to guide implementation without substantial domain expertise.

3. **Workflow vs. output trade-off**: Focusing on workflow correctness can be computationally expensive (full provenance logging, replay testing) and may slow deployment in fast-moving research environments.

4. **LLM hallucination and stochasticity**: The paper does not deeply address how to handle non-deterministic agent behavior (LLMs produce variable outputs) in a rigorous validation framework.

5. **Scope**: The survey covers 109 systems but may miss proprietary or very recent agent-based tools; the landscape is moving quickly and the snapshot may be incomplete.

## Open Questions
1. **Quantitative validation metrics**: Can FEV be operationalized into concrete, automatable scoring systems (e.g., provenance completeness %) that allow standardized comparison?

2. **Cost of compliance**: What is the overhead (compute, latency, developer time) of enforcing FEV-level rigor in production bioinformatics pipelines?

3. **Stochasticity and reproducibility**: How should agentic systems be evaluated when LLMs are inherently non-deterministic? Should agents include confidence measures or uncertainty quantification?

4. **Cross-domain transfer**: Can validation findings from one domain (e.g., genomics) transfer to another (e.g., drug discovery), or must each domain develop custom FEV implementations?

5. **Human-in-the-loop evaluation**: How should FEV be adapted when domain experts must manually validate agent recommendations, and what are the failure modes?

6. **Empirical validation at scale**: Do current benchmarks sufficiently stress-test agent workflows, or do we need larger, more realistic prospective studies?

## Key Takeaway
Bioinformatics agents need inspectable, validated workflows, not just fluent outputs, for scientific credibility.
