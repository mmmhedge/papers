# When a Correct Reward Is Not Enough: Diagnosing and Guiding PPO in an Analytically Solved Broker-Trader Game

**Authors:** Siu Tung Wong, Carlo Campajola
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03598v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03598v1)

## Summary
This paper tests whether a reinforcement learning algorithm (PPO) can learn to trade in a financial market where the optimal strategy is mathematically known. The researchers find that even with a correct reward signal, PPO struggles to match the analytical solution when market conditions are complex—but they show that using the analytical strategy as a starting point helps PPO adapt when costs change. This matters because it reveals gaps in how RL learns financial control problems and suggests that known mathematical solutions can guide and diagnose RL training.

## Theoretical Background
The paper combines two literatures: reinforcement learning (RL) — where agents learn policies by maximizing cumulative rewards through interaction — and mathematical finance. Specifically, it uses [[PPO]] (proximal policy optimization), a policy gradient method that updates neural network policies to maximize expected returns. The financial setting is a broker-trader game with continuous-time dynamics, where a broker chooses trading speed while facing an informed trader and random order flow. The authors leverage existing analytical solutions from financial mathematics (the continuous-time control problem solved via optimal stochastic control or Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equations) as ground truth benchmarks. They discretize the continuous payoff into a finite-step reward and verify correctness through grid refinement — a numerical validation technique.

## Core Contributions
1. **Diagnostic framework**: Uses an analytically solved financial control problem to test whether PPO learns correctly even with accurate reward signals. This reveals that PPO's critic (value function) fails to rank nearby actions reliably in stochastic environments — a specific failure mode often hidden in problems without analytical reference solutions.

2. **Empirical findings under different conditions**:
   - Without stochasticity (zero uninformed flow): PPO+FFNN approaches the reference policy.
   - With stochastic flow: Both PPO+FFNN and PPO+LSTM underperform, even though supervised learning shows their networks can represent the optimal action.
   - Potential-based reward shaping does not reliably help.

3. **Partial information result**: A causal certainty-equivalent controller (a model-based baseline) stays closer to optimal than PPO under partial observability.

4. **Adaptation mechanism**: Freezing the analytical policy and fine-tuning it closes 2.22% of the optimality gap when execution costs change — demonstrating that analytical solutions can serve as warm-start policies for RL adaptation.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to:
- [[RLHF]] and [[reward shaping]] (the paper tests whether correct rewards suffice for learning).
- [[Mechanistic interpretability]] of RL agents — the Monte Carlo diagnostics diagnosing why critics fail mirror interpretability efforts in other domains.
- Model-based RL and certainty-equivalence control — the comparison to a causal controller anchors RL to control-theoretic baselines.
- [[Algorithmic trading]] and market microstructure RL — most RL trading work lacks analytical ground truth; this paper highlights that advantage.

## Practical Applications
1. **RL for optimal execution**: Traders need algorithms to execute large orders without moving the market; analytical solutions exist for simplified models, but PPO struggles with realistic stochastic flow. This paper's diagnostic approach could improve RL-based execution engines.

2. **Policy adaptation under regime change**: Sudden changes in trading costs, volatility, or spreads require rapid adaptation. Starting from an analytically solved policy and fine-tuning with RL (as shown in the paper) could speed up adaptation in live trading.

3. **Validation of RL financial agents**: Financial regulators and risk teams need to trust RL systems. Using analytical benchmarks as described could help certify that RL traders aren't learning spurious patterns.

4. **Hybrid control systems**: Combine analytical control (for well-understood market microstructure) with learned adjustments (for unexpected regimes), reducing RL's training burden.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RL-powered execution algorithm marketplace**: Sell adaptive execution algorithms that start from analytically solved baselines and learn cost/volatility regimes in real-time, marketed as "analytically-certified RL trading."

2. **Financial RL diagnostics platform**: SaaS tool for quant teams to benchmark their RL agents against analytical solutions and visualize critic failures — similar to debuggers for supervised learning.

3. **Warm-start policy as a service**: Provide pre-trained analytical policies (computed offline) for common financial problems (execution, market-making, hedging); clients fine-tune for their costs/preferences.

4. **Risk monitoring for RL traders**: Real-time system that detects when learned policies diverge from analytical benchmarks, flagging potential overfitting or distribution shift before losses occur.

5. **Causal decision support for trading**: Build a tool that pairs model-based certainty-equivalent control (which the paper shows works better under partial info) with RL, allowing traders to understand *why* their algorithm trades.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scalability to realistic markets**: The broker-trader game is simplified. Real markets have multiple assets, latency constraints, and non-stationary liquidity. It's unclear whether the diagnostic insights transfer.

2. **Critic reliability in stochasticity**: The paper identifies that critics fail under stochastic flow but does not fully explain *why* or propose robust fixes. Potential-based reward shaping (a standard technique) did not help.

3. **Partial observability gap**: Under partial information, PPO underperforms the certainty-equivalent baseline, but the paper does not deeply explore how to design RL algorithms that handle partial obs better in this setting.

4. **Limited architecture exploration**: Only FFNN and LSTM actors/critics tested. Newer architectures (e.g., transformers, attention for order flow modeling) might help but are not evaluated.

5. **Adaptation experiment is narrow**: Only one cost-change scenario (halving execution cost). Robustness to other cost regimes, volatility changes, or adversarial traders is untested.

6. **Assumes known analytical solution**: The approach requires having a solved model; for many financial problems (e.g., portfolio optimization with transaction costs and constraints), analytical solutions don't exist.

## Open Questions
1. Why does the critic fail to rank nearby actions in stochastic settings? Can we design critics with built-in uncertainty quantification?

2. Do other RL algorithms (SAC, TD3, natural policy gradient) solve the same problems more reliably? Is the issue PPO-specific or fundamental to policy gradient methods?

3. How does the analytical benchmark transfer to partial observability settings with richer state spaces (e.g., order book dynamics, volatility surfaces)?

4. Can we use the analytical solution not just as a warm start but as a regularizer during training — penalizing divergence from it — and would that improve sample efficiency?

5. For financial problems without analytical solutions, can we use approximate analytical solutions (from numerical PDEs or linear-quadratic approximations) to achieve similar diagnostic benefits?

6. How does performance scale with market regime changes (e.g., fat-tail events, regime switches)? Does the analytical baseline remain useful as a reference?

## Key Takeaway
Even correct rewards don't guarantee RL learns well; analytical benchmarks reveal and guide hidden learning failures in financial control.
