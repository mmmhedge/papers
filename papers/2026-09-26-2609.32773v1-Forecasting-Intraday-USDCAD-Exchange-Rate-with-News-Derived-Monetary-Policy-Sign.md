# Forecasting Intraday USD/CAD Exchange Rate with News-Derived Monetary-Policy Signals

**Authors:** Maya Kodeih, Aliaa Alnaggar, Mucahit Cevik
**Published:** 2026-09-26 | **arXiv:** [2609.32773v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32773v1)

## Summary

This paper develops a method to forecast intraday USD/CAD exchange rates by extracting structured monetary-policy signals from news using large language models. Rather than treating news sentiment as a black box, the authors decompose monetary-policy communication into interpretable channels and measure which ones actually predict price movements — finding that communication timing and targeted activity measures matter more than sentiment alone.

## Theoretical Background

The paper builds on two strands of research. First, the efficient market hypothesis and its behavioral critiques — the question of whether markets incorporate all available information, especially from unstructured sources like central-bank communications. Second, it draws on sentiment analysis in financial NLP, where prior work has shown that extracting emotional tone from financial text can improve forecasting, though the mechanisms remain opaque.

The authors employ large language models (LLMs — neural networks trained on massive text corpora that can understand and generate human language) to convert qualitative central-bank statements into quantitative features. They then use tree-based machine-learning models (like random forests) to identify which of these features predict exchange-rate movements, combined with rolling-window experiments (training on past data, testing on future data in sequential chunks) to ensure realistic backtesting.

## Core Contributions

1. **Attribution methodology under false-discovery-rate control**: The paper introduces a statistical framework to identify which monetary-policy communication channels actually drive forecasting improvements, controlling for the multiple-testing problem where some features appear predictive by chance alone.

2. **Decomposition of monetary-policy signals**: Rather than lumping all news into "sentiment," the authors extract multiple dimensions — communication timing, targeted activity measures, LLM-derived sentiment — and measure each one's incremental contribution independently.

3. **Empirical finding that communication timing matters most**: The results show that *when* a monetary-policy statement is released contains more predictive power than traditional sentiment, challenging the assumption that sentiment is the primary value driver.

4. **Practical quantification of signal value**: The paper moves beyond asking "does news help?" to answering "which news elements help, and by how much?" — a shift toward interpretability and actionability.

## Connections to Other Work

This work relates to several areas:

- **[[Attention Is All You Need]]** (transformers) and [[RLHF]]: Modern LLMs are transformer-based models. The paper uses LLM-derived features rather than manually engineering them, representing a shift toward learned representations in finance.

- **Market microstructure**: The finding that communication *timing* predicts prices connects to literature on information asymmetry and order-flow dynamics — how market participants process and react to news with different speeds.

- **Sentiment analysis in finance**: Prior work (e.g., Tetlock, Gentzkow & Shapiro) showed sentiment from news forecasts returns; this paper refines the question by asking *which aspects* of communication matter.

- **Causality and feature importance in ML**: The false-discovery-rate correction echoes advances in causal inference and feature attribution, ensuring findings are not spurious.

## Practical Applications

1. **Intraday FX trading algorithms**: Hedge funds and proprietary trading firms can embed these monetary-policy signals into execution algorithms to predict short-term USD/CAD price movements and optimize entry/exit timing.

2. **Central-bank communication optimization**: Central banks themselves can use attribution insights to understand which communication formats (timing, tone, specificity) most influence market expectations and design communications more effectively.

3. **Risk management for multinational corporates**: Companies with large CAD/USD exposures can use these signals for tactical hedging — adjusting hedge ratios when monetary-policy announcements are imminent.

4. **Quantitative macro funds**: Multi-asset funds can use these signals as a component in broader macro forecasting systems to predict currency moves relative to other central banks.

5. **Real-time news-to-trading pipelines**: Automated systems can parse central-bank releases in real-time, extract signals via LLM, and trigger algorithmic trades within milliseconds.

## Technical Limitations

1. **USD/CAD specificity**: Results may not generalize to other currency pairs with different market structures, liquidity profiles, or central-bank communication styles.

2. **LLM dependency**: The method relies on LLM feature extraction quality. If the LLM misinterprets nuance (e.g., conditionals, sarcasm, forward guidance), downstream predictions degrade. The paper does not analyze LLM failure modes.

3. **Rolling-window overfitting risk**: Even with forward-looking tests, subtle overfitting to past regimes is possible. The paper does not discuss out-of-sample degradation or regime changes (e.g., during crises).

4. **Causality claims**: The attribution shows correlation, not causation. Communication timing may be correlated with *other* factors (e.g., market conditions that prompt announcements) that actually drive prices.

5. **Limited data and time-period bias**: Intraday FX data is noisy. Results may be specific to the study period; monetary-policy effectiveness varies with economic conditions.

6. **Tree-based model interpretability**: While more interpretable than neural networks, tree models can still struggle with high-dimensional feature interactions and temporal dependencies in FX markets.

## Open Questions

1. **Does the effect persist out-of-sample and out-of-period?** Can the signals forecast returns in a later year, or do they decay as traders learn and arbitrage them away?

2. **Which LLM architectures work best?** Would a smaller, fine-tuned model outperform a larger general-purpose LLM? How sensitive are results to LLM version and prompt design?

3. **Cross-currency validation**: Do these signals transfer to EUR/USD, GBP/USD, or other pairs? Is the USD/CAD result driven by unique institutional factors?

4. **Causal mechanisms**: What experiments would isolate whether timing *causes* moves, or whether timing is merely a marker of underlying macro shifts (e.g., surprise inflation data that triggers announcements)?

5. **Real-world profitability**: When accounting for transaction costs, slippage, and market impact, do these signals generate alpha? How quickly is alpha arbitraged away?

6. **Integration with other data**: How do these LLM-derived signals combine with price-based features, other news sources, or central-bank guidance indices (like the Gentzkow uncertainty index)?

## Key Takeaway

Central-bank communication timing predicts exchange rates better than sentiment alone, measured via LLM-extracted signals and statistical attribution.
