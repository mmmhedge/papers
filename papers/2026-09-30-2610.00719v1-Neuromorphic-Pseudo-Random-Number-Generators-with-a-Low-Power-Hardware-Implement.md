# Neuromorphic Pseudo-Random Number Generators with a Low Power Hardware Implementation

**Authors:** Jafar Shamsi, Navid Akbari, Sonia Sennik, Aaron Gruber, Wilten Nicola
**Published:** 2026-09-30 | **arXiv:** [2610.00719v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00719v1)

## Summary
This paper shows how spiking neural networks—brain-inspired computers that mimic how neurons fire—can generate high-quality random numbers while using very little power. The researchers built a neuromorphic random number generator that runs on low-power hardware (FPGAs) and consumes only 3.24 milliwatts, matching or exceeding the quality of traditional random number generators while being much more efficient.

## Theoretical Background
Pseudo-random number generation is essential for cryptography, simulation, and gaming, but current approaches trade off between quality (how unpredictable the sequence is), power consumption, and speed. The brain generates unpredictable outputs through chaotic dynamics—complex patterns that arise from balanced networks of excitatory (activating) and inhibitory (suppressing) neurons. This paper leverages a computational model called leaky-integrate-and-fire neurons, which simulate how real neurons accumulate electrical charge and fire when a threshold is reached. The hypothesis is that the chaotic, balanced states in these spiking neural networks can produce sequences with the randomness properties needed for practical applications.

## Core Contributions
1. Demonstrates that balanced spiking neural networks can serve as effective pseudo-random number generators, validated against standard statistical tests (e.g., NIST, Diehard).
2. Achieves a practical hardware implementation on low-power FPGAs consuming only 3.24 mW while generating random bits at 120 kbps—a significant efficiency gain over conventional approaches.
3. Shows that the same model works reliably in both software and hardware instantiations, suggesting robustness and portability.

## Connections to Other Work
This bridges neuroscience and hardware engineering: it exploits biological principles (balanced excitation/inhibition, chaotic dynamics) to solve a practical engineering problem. Related work includes traditional LFSRs (linear feedback shift registers) and algorithmic RNGs, which are faster but often power-hungry or require more silicon area. The paper connects to broader neuromorphic computing efforts, which aim to build brain-inspired systems for efficiency gains. The use of spiking neural networks relates to event-driven computing paradigms that only consume power when neurons fire, contrasting with traditional always-on digital circuits.

## Practical Applications
1. **Embedded cryptography**: IoT devices and sensors that need random number generation for encryption but have strict power budgets.
2. **Secure communications**: Portable devices (smartphones, wearables) requiring on-chip randomness without draining batteries.
3. **Monte Carlo simulations**: Low-power edge devices running stochastic simulations for robotics or autonomous systems.
4. **Gaming and lotteries**: Hardware random number generators for casinos or online gaming platforms that need certified, unpredictable sequences.
5. **Scientific instrumentation**: Field-deployed sensors and data loggers requiring low-power randomization for sampling strategies.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Neuromorphic security chip startup**: Manufacture and license ultra-low-power random number generator ICs for smartphone OEMs and IoT manufacturers, positioned as a post-quantum cryptography enabler.
2. **Edge AI accelerator with built-in RNG**: Bundle neuromorphic RNG with existing FPGA-based inference accelerators, targeting edge ML applications that need stochastic methods (e.g., dropout, Bayesian uncertainty).
3. **Secure enclave firmware**: Develop a software stack and toolchain for deploying NPRNGs on commercial FPGAs, marketed to defense and financial institutions as a supply-chain-secure alternative to foreign-made RNG hardware.
4. **Low-power simulation platform**: Create a cloud service or edge appliance that offers Monte Carlo and stochastic simulation engines powered by neuromorphic RNG, targeting quantitative finance and climate modeling.
5. **Hardware security module (HSM) upgrade**: Partner with existing HSM vendors to integrate neuromorphic RNG as a higher-efficiency replacement for legacy RNG modules in data centers.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Bandwidth vs. power trade-off**: 120 kbps is slow for some cryptographic applications (modern CPUs can generate gigabits/second); the approach may not scale to high-throughput scenarios.
2. **Design complexity**: Tuning the balance between excitation and inhibition in spiking networks is non-trivial; reproducibility and robustness across manufacturing variations on real hardware is unclear.
3. **Temperature and process variations**: The paper does not discuss how real FPGA implementations perform under voltage, temperature, or manufacturing process drift—critical for deployed systems.
4. **Limited validation**: Testing was done on standard RNG benchmarks (NIST, Diehard) but not against adversarial or cryptographic attack scenarios; security claims would need crypto-specific validation.
5. **Scalability unknown**: How do multiple instances behave? Can you run many NPRNGs on a single FPGA without crosstalk or correlation issues?

## Open Questions
1. How does the NPRNG perform under extreme operating conditions (temperature ranges, voltage noise) and over long deployment periods?
2. Can the bandwidth be increased without proportionally increasing power, and what are the fundamental limits?
3. What is the minimum network size needed to maintain high-quality randomness, and how sensitive is quality to network hyperparameters?
4. Does this approach scale to neuromorphic chips like Intel Loihi or IBM TrueNorth, and what are the advantages/disadvantages compared to FPGA implementations?
5. Can adversarial analysis (e.g., side-channel attacks, predictability under partial state leakage) confirm cryptographic security?

## Key Takeaway
Brain-inspired spiking networks generate high-quality random numbers on ultra-low-power hardware, enabling efficient cryptography and simulation in battery-constrained devices.
