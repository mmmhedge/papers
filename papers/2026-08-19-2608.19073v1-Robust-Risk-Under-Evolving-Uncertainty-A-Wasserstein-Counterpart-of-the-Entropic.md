# Robust Risk Under Evolving Uncertainty: A Wasserstein Counterpart of the Entropic Value-at-Risk

**Authors:** Deep Kumar Ganguly, Jan Křetínský
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.19073v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19073v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes a new risk measure—the Wasserstein entropic value-at-risk—that helps agents make safer decisions under uncertainty, especially when learning about their environment. Unlike existing entropy-based approaches, this measure accounts for unlikely "catastrophic" scenarios that standard methods might miss, making it more suitable for safety-critical applications where learning gradually increases confidence.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two foundational ideas. **Value-at-risk (VaR)** is a standard risk metric in finance measuring potential losses at a given confidence level. **Entropic value-at-risk (EVaR)** extends this by using robust optimization: it hedges against alternative models within a relative-entropy ball (a region of similar probability distributions), and increases caution proportionally to uncertainty. However, relative entropy only connects nearby models—it cannot represent truly catastrophic scenarios the nominal model assigns zero probability to. **Optimal transport** (or Wasserstein distance) provides an alternative geometry for comparing probability distributions that can bridge gaps between models, enabling the measure to account for tail risks. The paper studies the **coherent risk measure** induced by an optimal-transport ball, which is a mathematical object with desirable consistency properties.

## Core Contributions
1. **Wasserstein entropic VaR**: A new coherent risk measure using optimal-transport (Wasserstein) balls instead of relative-entropy balls, provably reaching catastrophic scenarios ignored by standard EVaR.
2. **Variational dual formula**: A closed-form dual representation mirroring the entropic formula, where transport radius replaces entropy radius and acts like an inverse temperature or transport price.
3. **Risk hierarchy placement**: Formal proof that this measure occupies a well-defined position in the known hierarchy of risk measures.
4. **Adaptive robust dynamic programming**: When the transport radius is driven by belief entropy (confidence about the environment), the resulting Bellman operator has a closed form, contracts caution as learning occurs, and comes with certified safety guarantees (a "safety sandwich").
5. **Numerical verification**: Empirical validation of both theoretical dualities and the adaptive safety properties.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends the framework of [[entropic value-at-risk]] and sits at the intersection of robust optimization, [[risk-management]] in finance, and safe [[reinforcement-learning]]. It relates to:
- **Distributionally robust optimization (DRO)**: Using uncertainty sets to hedge against model misspecification; this paper replaces the entropy ball with a Wasserstein ball.
- **Coherent risk measures** (Artzner et al.): The paper proves formal membership in this axiomatic class.
- **Safe exploration in RL**: Agents learning online benefit from measures that tighten confidence bounds as belief sharpens—the "certified safety sandwich" formalizes this intuition.
- **Optimal transport in ML**: Growing body of work using Wasserstein distances for robustness; this applies it to risk measurement.

## Practical Applications
1. **Portfolio optimization under model uncertainty**: Investment managers learning asset correlations can hedge catastrophic tail events (e.g., regime switches) that correlation models assign near-zero probability to.
2. **Autonomous system safety**: Robots or control agents learning their dynamics can set risk budgets that tighten as confidence grows, avoiding catastrophic failures during exploration.
3. **Financial derivatives pricing and hedging**: Traders managing exposure to extreme market moves not captured by standard models (e.g., flash crashes, policy shocks).
4. **Insurance and actuarial risk**: Insurers pricing rare, high-impact events where historical data is sparse and model error is severe.
5. **Supply chain optimization**: Firms learning demand and disruption patterns can adaptively rebalance inventory safety margins as forecasts improve.

## Product & Startup Ideas

2. **Adaptive Portfolio Manager**: A robo-advisor that explicitly models catastrophic tail scenarios unreachable by EVaR, marketed to institutional investors seeking "black swan" protection.
3. **Risk Intelligence API**: A machine learning service that takes historical data, learns an uncertainty set via Wasserstein distance, and outputs tight risk bounds for derivatives pricing and hedging—competing on accuracy vs. legacy VaR/EVaR.

5. **Enterprise Decision Support**: B2B software for supply chain and operations teams to optimize production/inventory under evolving demand uncertainty, automatically tightening margins as demand forecasts converge.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational tractability**: The variational dual may be hard to solve in high dimensions; the paper does not thoroughly address scalability to realistic problems with thousands of variables.
2. **Belief entropy assumption**: The approach assumes belief entropy is a good proxy for true uncertainty; misspecified belief updates or overconfidence could degrade performance.
3. **Limited empirical validation**: Experiments are numerical (verifying dualities) rather than large-scale realistic benchmarks; it is unclear how much safer decisions truly are in practice vs. EVaR or other baselines.
4. **Nominal model dependence**: Like EVaR, the measure still relies on choosing a nominal model; if that model is fundamentally wrong, the transport ball may not capture relevant uncertainties.
5. **No convergence guarantees**: The paper does not prove convergence rates of the adaptive DP algorithm or sample complexity of learning the transport ball from data.

## Open Questions
1. **Scalability**: How does computation time grow with dimension? Can this be solved efficiently via convex relaxations or approximate methods for realistic portfolio or control problems?
2. **Empirical comparison**: On real data (financial returns, robot trajectories, supply chain), does Wasserstein EVaR produce meaningfully safer decisions than EVaR, and at what computational cost?
3. **Learning the transport radius**: How should practitioners set the transport radius from data? The paper assumes entropy drives it; can this be adapted if belief updates are biased?
4. **Sample complexity**: How many samples are needed to learn a reliable Wasserstein ball around the nominal model? Does this scale with dimension unfavorably?
5. **Multi-agent extension**: Can this framework extend to multi-agent settings where agents have heterogeneous beliefs and learn interactively—relevant for market models or distributed control?

## Key Takeaway
Wasserstein-based risk measures protect against catastrophic scenarios that entropy-based methods miss, tightening safety margins as learning progresses.
