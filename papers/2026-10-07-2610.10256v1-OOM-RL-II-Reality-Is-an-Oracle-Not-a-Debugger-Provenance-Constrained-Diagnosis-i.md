# OOM-RL II: Reality Is an Oracle, Not a Debugger Provenance-Constrained Diagnosis in Continually Evolving Agent-Engineered Systems

**Authors:** Kun Liu, Liqun Chen
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10256v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10256v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles a critical problem in production machine learning systems: when external outcomes tell us *what* happened, they often don't tell us *why* or *which* of many evolving system components caused it. The authors study this "outcome-diagnosis gap" in a live quantitative trading system that changed continuously over a year, showing how provenance records and structured identification protocols can help attribute results to specific decisions even when perfect causal inference is impossible.

## Theoretical Background
The paper sits at the intersection of several areas. Machine learning in production systems often relies on empirical feedback (realized outcomes) to validate or adjust decisions — a core idea in [[reinforcement-learning]] where agents learn from reward signals. However, unlike controlled experiments, real systems are not frozen: code, models, configurations, and data all evolve. This creates an identification problem familiar to econometricians and causal inference researchers: confounding between what changed and what caused the outcome. The paper draws on software engineering provenance (tracking the history of artifacts and processes), causal inference concepts (distinguishing correlation from causation), and quantitative finance practice (where trading decisions are evaluated against realized P&L). It distinguishes three development modes: frozen instances (immutable snapshots), pre-specified adaptive procedures (declared rules for change), and ad-hoc development (undeclared modifications).

## Core Contributions
1. **Formalizes the outcome-diagnosis gap**: Separates three distinct claims — outcome evidence (did the system gain?), evaluated-object identity (which system version?), and causal explanation (which decision caused the gain?). These are logically independent.

2. **Proposes a provenance-constrained identification framework**: Uses engineering records (version control, change logs, timestamps) to establish what versions existed, when they were active, and which decisions they contained. Shows how this structure enables factual binding (assigning a recommendation to its runtime instance) without requiring counterfactual estimation.

3. **Illustrates the outcome-diagnosis gap empirically**: A real trading system that outperformed its benchmark and gained money, yet had statistically zero alpha under retrospective specification — demonstrating that outcome success and diagnostic clarity are orthogonal.

4. **Proposes a prospective protocol**: Suggests how to design systems going forward (declaring frozen instances, adaptive rules, and change binding points) so future diagnostics can resolve which component caused observed outcomes.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to several research threads:

- **[[RLHF|Reinforcement learning from human feedback]]** and reward learning: assumes external feedback (outcomes) can guide agent behavior, but exposes the gap between observed reward and understanding what generated it.
- **[[Mechanistic interpretability]]**: concerned with understanding why a system produced an output; this paper argues mechanistic inspection alone cannot overcome confounding from system changes.
- **Causal inference and program synthesis**: the paper echoes debates about identifiability under model misspecification and changing environments.
- **Software engineering**: formal provenance tracking and reproducibility are longstanding concerns; this work shows their necessity for ML auditing.
- **Quantitative finance**: positions the outcome-diagnosis problem as central to strategy auditing and risk governance.

## Practical Applications

1. **Trading System Auditing**: Regulators and risk officers need to know whether a profitable fund's returns came from skill or luck. This framework lets auditors trace which strategy versions were active when, pinning down what actually drove returns.

2. **A/B Testing in Production ML**: When rolling out new models or decision rules in live systems (recommendation engines, ads, pricing), provenance binding lets teams attribute incremental changes in metrics to specific code or parameter updates, even if drift occurred.

3. **Compliance and Explainability**: Financial institutions face regulatory requirements to explain decisions (e.g., loan denials, trading activity). Provenance-constrained diagnosis enables truthful ex-post explanation of which decision rule applied.

4. **ML Model Monitoring & Governance**: Production ML systems continually retrain and deploy new versions. Provenance protocols help teams connect performance changes to specific model versions, preventing misattribution of performance to the wrong update.

5. **Counterfactual Analysis in Non-Experimental Settings**: When running experiments is infeasible (e.g., live trading, medical treatment), provenance records can replace some randomization by establishing which treatment/rule variant was in effect for each outcome.

## Product & Startup Ideas

1. **Provenance-Audit SaaS for Quant Funds**: A cloud platform that integrates with trading infrastructure (APIs, databases, version control) to automatically build a complete provenance record of trading systems and outcomes. Sells to hedge funds for compliance, risk reporting, and performance attribution.

2. **ML Experiment & Rollout Governance Tool**: Enterprise software that enforces prospective binding protocols for ML deployments. Teams declare frozen instances, adaptive rules, and change gates before deployment; the system logs actual runtime bindings and auto-generates audit reports linking metric changes to code versions.

3. **Regulatory Explainability Engine**: White-label SaaS for financial institutions to meet GDPR, Fair Lending, and SEC requirements. Ingests provenance logs and outcomes; generates defensible explanations of *which decision rule* applied to each transaction (not just what a black-box model output).

4. **Causal Attribution for Digital Products**: Analytics platform for e-commerce, ads, and SaaS companies. Instead of crediting the last touchpoint, uses provenance-constrained identification to disentangle which product changes, feature flags, or algorithm updates genuinely drove conversion/retention shifts.

5. **Forensic ML Diagnostics for Post-Mortems**: On-demand consulting + lightweight software for startups to conduct root-cause analysis after production incidents or unexpected metric drops. Uses provenance records to rule out confounding and isolate the actual culprit.

## Technical Limitations

1. **Incomplete Provenance**: The paper assumes access to full engineering records (version control, deployment logs, configuration snapshots). Real-world systems often have gaps, missing audit trails, or undocumented changes. If provenance is sparse, the protocol's power degrades.

2. **No Counterfactual Validation**: The framework can establish *which* system version produced an outcome, but not *whether an alternative version would have performed differently*. It resolves identity without answering "what if?"

3. **Ad-Hoc Development Unresolvable**: By definition, undeclared modifications leave no trace. The protocol helps formalize future development, but cannot retroactively recover causal attribution in legacy systems built without provenance discipline.

4. **Outcome Measurement Noise**: Assumes oracle feedback (realized outcomes) is accurate. In practice, accounting, attribution, or measurement error can confound outcome signals, undermining the starting premise.

5. **Not Prospectively Validated**: The authors explicitly note the protocol is proposed, not yet tested in live deployment. Real-world friction (engineers forgetting to log, ambiguous change timestamps, partial rollouts) may degrade utility.

6. **Computational Complexity**: Enumerating all compatible histories and resolving identifiability for large, modular systems could become computationally expensive as the system grows.

## Open Questions

1. **How does this scale to complex multi-component systems?** Trading systems are relatively simple (one decision at a time). How does provenance-constrained diagnosis handle systems with dozens of interacting models, microservices, and data pipeline stages?

2. **Can prospective binding protocols be automated?** The paper proposes them informally. Can compilers or runtime frameworks enforce provenance binding automatically, eliminating human error?

3. **How sensitive is attribution to measurement error and outcome noise?** Under what conditions does noise in oracle feedback break the identifiability guarantees? How robust is the protocol?

4. **Does this extend to counterfactual effect estimation?** The paper separates identity from causal effect. Can provenance + additional structure (e.g., randomization in a subset of decisions) enable counterfactual inference?

5. **What is the optimal trade-off between provenance overhead and diagnostic power?** Logging everything is expensive. Can adaptive provenance (logging more carefully around suspected confounders) reduce cost while preserving identifiability?

6. **How do humans interpret and act on provenance-constrained diagnoses?** Case studies in other domains (healthcare, autonomous systems) would clarify whether practitioners trust and use these diagnoses operationally.

## Key Takeaway
When evolving systems produce good outcomes, detailed change logs matter more than causal models for explaining which decisions actually caused success.
