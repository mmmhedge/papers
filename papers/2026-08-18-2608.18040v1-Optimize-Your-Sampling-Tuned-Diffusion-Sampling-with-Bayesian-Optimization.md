# Optimize Your Sampling: Tuned Diffusion Sampling with Bayesian Optimization

**Authors:** Travis Zhang, Christian Belardi, Justin Lovelace, Jin Peng Zhou, Saebyeol Shin et al.
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18040v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18040v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a simple but overlooked problem: how to choose the timesteps during diffusion model sampling to balance speed and quality. Instead of relying on fixed schedules or theoretical surrogates, the authors treat timestep selection as a black-box optimization problem and use Bayesian optimization to find better schedules directly. The result is dramatic efficiency gains—a 5-step schedule achieves 89–94% of the quality of 50 steps, cutting inference cost by 10x.

## Theoretical Background
Diffusion models generate images by iteratively denoising random noise over many timesteps. Sampling is computationally expensive because it requires many forward passes through a large neural network. Prior work has explored two approaches to speed this up: (1) designing more efficient solver algorithms (like DPM-Solver) that require fewer steps, and (2) learning better timestep schedules that reweight or reorder the denoising process. A recent approach called Align Your Steps optimized a theoretically derived surrogate for sample quality rather than the actual quality metric itself. Bayesian optimization is a principled method for optimizing expensive black-box functions by maintaining a probabilistic model (usually a Gaussian process) of the objective and using an acquisition function to decide which inputs to evaluate next.

## Core Contributions
1. **Direct metric optimization**: OYS optimizes the actual quality metric (e.g., CLIP score, FID) via Bayesian optimization, rather than optimizing a theoretical surrogate. This is simpler and more empirically effective.
2. **Model-agnostic and training-free**: The method requires no additional training and applies to any pretrained diffusion model, including distilled variants—making it immediately practical.
3. **Strong empirical results**: OYS outperforms both default and Align Your Steps schedules across multiple tasks (text-to-image, inpainting, general image generation) in both automatic metrics and human evaluation.
4. **Compatibility with advanced samplers**: The approach improves performance across both simple (Euler) and sophisticated (DPM-Solver++) sampling algorithms, showing broad applicability.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[generative-models]] and [[optimization]]. It complements the recent surge in diffusion model acceleration (faster solvers, distillation, pruning) by attacking a complementary angle—the schedule itself. It differs philosophically from [[Align Your Steps]] by avoiding surrogate objectives and directly optimizing the metric of interest. The use of Bayesian optimization echoes classical hyperparameter tuning approaches but applies them to a novel and time-sensitive problem. The findings suggest that the choice of timesteps is as important as the choice of solver algorithm.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Bayesian optimization cost**: Finding the optimal schedule requires evaluating many candidate schedules; the paper doesn't detail how many forward passes this initial search costs or how to amortize this across many users.
2. **Metric dependency**: OYS optimizes for a chosen metric (CLIP, FID, human preference). The optimal schedule may vary significantly across metrics, so practitioners must commit to one objective or run multiple optimizations.
3. **Limited generalization**: A schedule optimized for one model or task (e.g., text-to-image) may not transfer well to another (e.g., inpainting or different datasets). The paper shows improvements on multiple tasks but doesn't fully characterize when schedules generalize.
4. **Scalability to very long schedules**: The paper demonstrates 5–50 steps; unclear how well Bayesian optimization scales to much larger step spaces or how sample efficiency degrades.
5. **Computational overhead not fully detailed**: The inference savings are clear, but the paper doesn't quantify the wall-clock time for the initial optimization, making it hard to assess break-even for small-scale deployments.

## Open Questions
1. **Schedule transferability**: Can a schedule optimized on one model (e.g., Stable Diffusion 1.5) transfer to another (e.g., SDXL or Flux)? How much fine-tuning is needed?
2. **Human preference alignment**: The paper uses automatic metrics and human evaluation, but does optimizing for human-rated quality differ from optimizing for CLIP or FID? Which metric best predicts user satisfaction?
3. **Adaptive scheduling**: Could the sampling schedule adapt dynamically during inference (e.g., based on intermediate noise levels or learned uncertainty)? Could Bayesian optimization find such adaptive policies?
4. **Theoretical justification**: Why is direct metric optimization better than surrogates? Is there a principled understanding of when schedules matter most?
5. **Cross-task and cross-domain schedules**: Can a single schedule work well across diverse tasks (inpainting, super-resolution, style transfer) or must each task be optimized separately?

## Key Takeaway
Bayesian optimization directly finds better diffusion sampling schedules, cutting inference cost by 10x while retaining 90%+ quality with zero retraining.
