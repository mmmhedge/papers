# MELD: A Protocol for Merging Knowledge Across Distributed Agentic Memories

**Authors:** Lauri Lovén, Jaakko Sauvola, Jukka Riekki, Sasu Tarkoma
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.16357v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16357v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces MELD, a protocol that lets autonomous agents with separate memories share and reconcile knowledge without a central coordinator. When two agents learn the same fact stated differently, or discover conflicting claims, MELD automatically merges or flags these inconsistencies rather than silently losing information. The work matters because distributed AI systems increasingly need to collaborate while staying independent — and current approaches either lose knowledge or require centralized oversight.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on distributed systems theory, particularly eventual consistency models — the idea that separate databases can agree on state without real-time coordination. It uses knowledge graphs as its data model: directed networks where facts are nodes and relationships are edges. MELD leverages CRDTs (Conflict-free Replicated Data Types) — mathematical structures that automatically converge to the same state across disconnected copies, even under network delays or failures. The core insight combines three matching signals: exact key identity (does "Paris" match "Paris"?), embedding similarity (do two descriptions point to the same concept?), and natural-language inference (does one claim logically entail another?). These signals feed a five-outcome decision tree: insert (new fact), merge (same fact, same representation), relate (linked but distinct facts), conflict (contradictory claims), or reject (noise).

## Core Contributions
1. **Auditable knowledge reconciliation**: A deterministic five-outcome procedure with three signals that decides how to handle incoming claims — crucial for transparency in multi-agent systems.
2. **Status CRDT for coordination-free coherence**: Uses a conflict-free replicated data type for claim status, allowing agents to agree on "what we know" even under network partitions, without a central authority.
3. **Preservation of contradictions**: Rather than resolving conflicts heuristically, MELD flags contradictions for later human or algorithmic adjudication — avoiding silent data loss.
4. **Empirical validation on realistic infrastructure**: Testing spans from edge 5G networks to HPC clusters with real partition-heal cycles, showing 30/30 recovery vs. 11/30 for last-writer-wins.
5. **Semantic routing optimization**: Reduces message traffic by ~3× through intelligent claim distribution while maintaining recall.

## Connections to Other Work
MELD sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent-systems]] coordination, distributed consensus (related to Raft and Paxos but without requiring a leader), and knowledge graph completion. It extends [[CRDTs]] — typically used for text or lists — to structured knowledge. The embedding-similarity matching echoes work in entity resolution and knowledge graph alignment, but applies it in a real-time, streaming context. The contradiction-preservation stance differs from traditional knowledge-base completion systems (which try to infer a single "true" state); it aligns more with work in handling uncertain or conflicting information in semantic web contexts.

## Practical Applications
1. **Federated healthcare systems**: Multiple hospitals share patient data (diagnoses, treatments, allergies) across independent databases. MELD reconciles "patient has allergy to penicillin" (written three ways) and flags contradictions (e.g., conflicting diagnoses) for clinician review rather than losing either.
2. **Edge AI in autonomous vehicles**: A self-driving car's local knowledge store learns about road hazards from onboard sensors; it exchanges this with nearby vehicles' memories via 5G. MELD merges "pothole at GPS 40.7°N, 74.0°W" from car A with "road damage at coordinates (40.700, -74.000)" from car B without duplication.
3. **Scientific collaboration platforms**: Researchers at different labs maintain shared memory of experimental results. When lab A reports "compound X has melting point 156°C" and lab B reports "157°C," MELD flags the contradiction for review and potential re-measurement rather than silently choosing one.
4. **IoT sensor networks**: Thousands of distributed environmental sensors report on air quality, temperature, soil moisture. MELD de-duplicates overlapping readings, merges correlated measurements, and preserves sensor conflicts for anomaly detection.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Distributed knowledge backbone for AI agents**: A managed service (like a "vector database for multi-agent coherence") that enterprises subscribe to. Agents query it via simple APIs; the platform handles reconciliation, maintains audit logs, and exposes conflicts. Revenue: per-claim or per-agent-connection fees.
2. **Federated CRM for healthcare networks**: Independent clinics use MELD to maintain a shared patient record across organizational boundaries. Each clinic keeps its own database; MELD reconciles diagnoses, medications, and allergies across them. Compliance-ready (audit trails, never silent overwrites).
3. **Conflict detection and resolution as a service**: Companies with decentralized data sources (e.g., global supply-chain partners, trading desks) feed claims into a MELD backbone. The service learns which conflicts matter, routes them to human experts, and learns their resolution patterns. Premium tier: AI-assisted contradiction triage.
4. **Multi-agent simulation platform for robotics**: Industrial or research teams running large swarms of robots. MELD handles memory coherence for thousands of agents across edge compute, improving coordination without centralized overhead. Sell as enterprise software with on-prem or edge deployment.
5. **Open-source distributed knowledge graph middleware**: A Kubernetes-friendly, protocol-compliant implementation of MELD that integrates with popular graph databases (Neo4j, ArangoDB). Community adoption, commercial support tiers.

## Technical Limitations
1. **No ground-truth arbitration**: MELD flags contradictions but doesn't resolve them; systems still need a human or external authority to decide truth. In high-stakes domains (medicine, finance), the cost and latency of adjudication could be prohibitive.
2. **Embedding similarity is brittle across domains**: The paper uses a fixed embedding model; semantic similarity thresholds may not generalize from HotpotQA (reading-comprehension distractor) to, say, chemical formulas or medical codes where synonymy is domain-specific.
3. **Scalability unknowns**: Experiments use three tiers (edge, HPC, local); unclear how MELD scales to 100s of agents or 1000s of claims per second. CRDT merge complexity could become expensive.
4. **Natural language inference is fallible**: The NLI verdict is one of three signals but is itself learned/imperfect. False positives (merging distinct facts) or false negatives (missing logical entailment) directly corrupt the knowledge graph.
5. **Benign-fault assumption**: The protocol assumes agents won't actively forge or replay claims (only Byzantine faults are excluded). In adversarial settings (e.g., competing traders), this breaks down.
6. **Configuration sensitivity**: The decision procedure uses "context and freshness gates" (thresholds) that are "empirically calibrated"; no guidance on how to set these for new domains, and small changes likely shift false-merge rates significantly.

## Open Questions
1. **Can MELD learn adjudication rules?** If human experts resolve contradictions consistently (e.g., "always trust sensor readings over user-input estimates"), can the system learn to auto-resolve future conflicts with similar patterns?
2. **How does performance degrade with Byzantine agents?** The benign-fault model is realistic for many scenarios, but what happens if one agent intentionally floods the network with false claims? Can reputation or cryptographic signatures tighten security?
3. **What is the theoretical lower bound on message complexity?** The paper shows ~3× reduction with semantic routing; can this be proven optimal, or is there headroom for further optimization?
4. **How do embedding models perform across modalities?** HotpotQA is text-only. Can MELD reconcile claims from structured data (time-series, images, code) with the same embedding framework?
5. **Does merging improve downstream inference?** The paper shows recall on HotpotQA; what about downstream tasks like question-answering or entity linking on the merged graph? Does coherent memory improve agent reasoning?
6. **Can MELD work without publish/subscribe?** The design binds tightly to pub/sub transport; how portable is it to direct peer-to-peer or blockchain-based communication?

## Key Takeaway
Distributed agents can now share knowledge automatically, merging contradictions without losing data or needing a central coordinator.
