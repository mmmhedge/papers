# Breaking the Uniformity Trap: Scaling Video Diffusion Model via SplitMoE

**Authors:** Yu Xu, Yuxin Zhang, Xiao Yang, Haotian Yang, Yizhi Wang et al.
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.38140v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.38140v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes SplitMoE, a new architecture for scaling video generation models using a split expert pool that avoids uniform routing constraints. Current Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) systems treat all tokens identically, causing semantic information to scatter across experts—but video data has natural semantic structure and temporal redundancy that gets lost. SplitMoE separates experts into "semantic" and "generic" roles, letting tokens cluster by meaning rather than arbitrary load balancing, resulting in faster training and better video quality.

## Theoretical Background
Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) is a sparse scaling technique where input tokens are routed to different expert subnetworks conditionally—only a subset of experts activate per input, keeping computational cost manageable while scaling model capacity. MoE has driven recent successes in large language models like GShard and Switch Transformers by enabling parameter efficiency.

Video diffusion models generate videos through iterative denoising—starting from noise and progressively refining it using learned gradients. Existing visual MoEs apply token-wise routing uniformly: each token independently chooses experts, and training regularizes expert usage toward equal load. This "uniformity trap" assumes all data is homogeneous, which breaks down for video where many frames are spatially/temporally redundant and semantic content is long-tailed (some concepts appear far more often than others).

## Core Contributions
1. **Identifies the uniformity trap**: Demonstrates that existing visual MoEs fail on video because uniform load-balancing regularization forces semantically similar patches into different experts, fragmenting coherent information.

2. **Split-role expert pool**: Bifurcates experts into semantic experts (capture high-level meaning) and generic experts (preserve low-level details and flexible capacity), allowing natural semantic clustering without forcing balance.

3. **Prototype-guided routing and pull-push regularization**: Replaces uniform load balancing with soft constraints that let tokens organize by semantic attributes while maintaining training stability. Prototype-guided routing clusters tokens toward learned semantic anchors rather than arbitrary expert assignments.

4. **Emergent coarse-to-fine denoising**: Reveals that the split architecture naturally learns a two-stage denoising process—coarse semantic refinement followed by fine detail generation—providing insight into how large-scale video models should scale.

## Connections to Other Work
This work extends [[Mixture-of-Experts]] systems (popularized by [[Switch Transformers]] and GShard) from language to vision. It critiques uniform load-balancing constraints used in visual MoEs, contrasting with recent work like V-MoE that applies vanilla MoE to images without addressing long-tailed structure.

Relates to [[diffusion models]] for video generation (e.g., Imagen Video, Make-A-Video) by proposing a more efficient scaling path. The semantic/generic split echoes hierarchical decomposition ideas in other domains but applies them specifically to expert routing rather than layer design.

The discovery of emergent coarse-to-fine denoising connects to cascade and hierarchical diffusion approaches, suggesting this structure is learnable rather than hand-designed.

## Practical Applications
1. **Efficient video content generation**: Streaming platforms (YouTube, TikTok) generating personalized or on-demand video at lower computational cost, enabling real-time or near-real-time synthesis at scale.

2. **Game engine enhancement**: Real-time video generation for game environments, replacing or augmenting asset pipelines by conditionally generating gameplay footage or cutscenes.

3. **Autonomous vehicle simulation**: Generating diverse synthetic driving scenarios for training perception systems, with semantic experts handling road structure and generic experts handling weather/lighting variation.

4. **Film/VFX post-production**: AI-assisted shot completion, inpainting, or re-rendering with full semantic control and faster iteration.

5. **Scientific simulation and visualization**: Generative modeling of physical processes (fluid dynamics, molecular behavior) where semantic experts capture high-level physics and generic experts handle noise/turbulence.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Prototype initialization and stability**: Prototype-guided routing requires initializing semantic prototypes; unclear how sensitive the method is to initialization or how prototypes drift during training.

2. **Scalability to larger models**: Tested on moderate scales; unclear whether the semantic/generic split remains beneficial at 100B+ parameters or whether semantic structure becomes so diverse that the split offers minimal gain.

3. **Domain generalization**: Experiments shown on standard video benchmarks; unclear how well semantic prototypes transfer across domains (e.g., trained on natural video → applied to medical or scientific video).

4. **Routing overhead**: Split-role routing and prototype updates add computational overhead; the paper claims "equivalent activated-parameter budget" but doesn't fully account for routing computation itself.

5. **Semantic expert interpretability**: No visualization or analysis of what semantic experts actually learn—unclear whether they capture interpretable semantic categories or remain black-box clusters.

6. **Training data assumptions**: The method assumes semantic long-tailedness in video; may be less effective on artificially balanced datasets or domains with uniform semantic distribution.

## Open Questions
1. **How do prototypes evolve during training?** Do they converge to stable semantic clusters, or do they drift? How does this affect routing stability in later training phases?

2. **Can semantic experts be made interpretable?** Can we visualize or label what each semantic expert specializes in (e.g., "human faces," "water," "text")? This could enable controllable generation.

3. **Does this scale to multimodal video?** The paper focuses on visual-only generation; how does SplitMoE handle video+audio or video+text conditioning?

4. **Can we transfer semantic prototypes across datasets?** Pre-train prototypes on large video corpora and fine-tune on smaller domains—could this improve sample efficiency?

5. **What is the optimal semantic/generic split ratio?** The paper doesn't explore how many experts should be semantic vs. generic; systematic ablation would clarify this design choice.

6. **How does this interact with other scaling dimensions?** Combining SplitMoE with other efficiency techniques (quantization, distillation) or larger models—what are the interaction effects?

## Key Takeaway
Splitting expert pools by semantic role, not load balance, lets video diffusion models learn faster and generate better videos.
