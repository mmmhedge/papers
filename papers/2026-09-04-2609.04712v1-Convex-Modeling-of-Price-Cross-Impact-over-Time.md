# Convex Modeling of Price Cross-Impact over Time

**Authors:** Vincent Yinjun-Wang, Madeleine Udell
**Published:** 2026-09-04 | **arXiv:** [2609.04712v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.04712v1)

## Summary
This paper builds a mathematical model of how trades affect prices—both in the same contract (self-impact) and in related contracts (cross-impact)—and how that impact decays over time. The model uses convex optimization to help traders plan multi-leg trades (like commodity spreads) without overestimating their costs, which matters because even small improvements in cost estimates can unlock profitable strategies in thin markets.

## Theoretical Background
Price impact models in finance describe how a large trade moves the market price against the trader. Traditional models capture self-impact: your purchase of crude oil futures pushes the price up. But two well-documented effects are often ignored. Cross-impact means a trade in one contract (e.g., crude oil) also moves prices in related contracts (e.g., heating oil). Transient impact means price moves are not permanent—prices partially recover after the trade, so unwinding a position later recaptures some of the cost paid at entry. This paper frames the problem in convex optimization — finding the best execution plan by minimizing a cost function with no local optima — and uses power-law decay kernels (mathematical functions that model how impact fades over time) to couple trades across multiple periods.

## Core Contributions
1. **Joint modeling of cross-impact and transient impact** in a single convex quadratic cost function, rather than treating them separately or ignoring them.
2. **No-arbitrage guarantee**: the model is constructed so that it cannot be exploited for price manipulation even if liquidity conditions change over the trading horizon.
3. **Practical parameterization** using market-observable quantities (volatility, volume, correlation forecasts) to build the positive semidefinite coupling matrix, making it implementable without exotic assumptions.
4. **Empirical validation** on calendar spreads of crude oil futures during the commodity index roll—a realistic, high-stakes trading scenario where edges are measured in basis points.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Market Microstructure]] (the study of how trades move prices and how markets work mechanistically) and [[Algorithmic Trading]] execution. It builds on the classical Almgren-Chriss framework for optimal execution but adds the realistic complications (cross-impact and decay) that that model omitted. The convex formulation connects to broader [[Optimization]] work in finance, ensuring solutions are globally optimal. The no-arbitrage construction echoes foundational work in derivatives pricing that enforces consistency with no-profit conditions.

## Practical Applications
1. **Commodity relative-value trading**: Traders executing multi-leg spreads (e.g., crude oil calendar spreads) can now plan entry and exit timing more accurately, capturing strategies that would appear unprofitable under naive cost models.
2. **Execution algorithm design**: Algorithmic execution systems can use this cost model to decompose large orders into smaller child orders across time and related contracts, minimizing total market impact.
3. **Portfolio rebalancing**: Asset managers rebalancing across correlated positions (e.g., long crude, short heating oil) can estimate true liquidation costs before committing to the trade.
4. **Risk management systems**: Banks and hedge funds can feed this model into their pre-trade analytics to decide which trades are economically viable given real impact costs.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Impact Estimation API**: A cloud service that ingests a proposed trade or portfolio rebalance and returns realistic impact costs, powered by live market microstructure data (volume, volatility, correlation), integrated into execution algorithms.
2. **Execution Optimization Software**: A SaaS platform for mid-size hedge funds and asset managers that optimizes order timing and routing across related contracts using this convex model, with backtesting and live monitoring.
3. **Commodity Trading Analytics**: A specialized tool for commodity traders that combines this cross-impact model with rolling futures mechanics to identify calendar spread trades that clear the cost bar.
4. **Market Microstructure Data Service**: Sell pre-computed volatility, volume, and correlation matrices (the inputs to this model) for different asset classes and time windows, enabling funds to run their own impact optimization.
5. **Pre-trade Analytics Console**: A plug-and-play dashboard for sell-side traders that shows, in real-time, the estimated cost of a proposed trade decomposition before it hits the market.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Model assumes stationary forecasts**: Volatility, volume, and correlation are assumed constant or forecastable over the planning horizon; in crisis regimes these can shift suddenly, breaking the model.
2. **Power-law decay is empirical**: The choice of a power-law decay kernel is motivated by observation but not derived from first principles; it may not hold across all asset classes or market regimes.
3. **Assumes liquid, continuous markets**: The model breaks down in thin markets, at market-open/close, or during halts—situations where the continuous convex formulation no longer reflects reality.
4. **Ignores second-order feedback**: The model assumes your execution doesn't trigger other traders' algorithms; in reality, large executions can cascade through the market.
5. **Requires accurate correlation forecasts**: Cross-impact coupling depends on forecasted correlations, which can be unstable or misspecified, especially in stress periods or new regimes.
6. **Backtest overfitting risk**: Empirical results on crude oil calendars don't guarantee the model generalizes to other commodities, equities, or strategies.

## Open Questions
1. How robust is the power-law decay assumption across different asset classes (equities, FX, bonds) and market regimes (normal vs. stressed)?
2. Can the model be extended to account for non-linear or regime-dependent impact, or does the convexity requirement force simplification?
3. How should practitioners update volatility, volume, and correlation forecasts in real-time, and how much estimation error can the model tolerate?
4. What is the magnitude of second-order feedback (other agents reacting to your trade), and can it be bounded or incorporated into the convex framework?
5. Does the no-manipulation property hold under strategic adversaries, or only under passive market conditions?
6. How can this model be combined with execution algorithms that also consider market-impact mitigation tactics (e.g., VWAP, TWAP)?

## Key Takeaway
A new convex model captures how trades move related markets and how that impact decays, helping traders plan cheaper multi-leg trades.
