# Do LLMs Take Care of Their Own? Similarity Signals Can Induce Cooperation

**Authors:** Akash Kundu, Emanuel Tewolde, Ratip Emin Berker, Samuel F. Brown, Vincent Conitzer
**Published:** 2026-08-12 | **arXiv:** [2608.12125v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12125v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how large language models (LLMs) behave in strategic interactions when given information about each other's similarity. The researchers find that similarity signals can encourage cooperation in classic game-theory problems like the Prisoner's Dilemma, but different LLM models respond to these signals in drastically different ways. The work matters because as AI agents become deployed in the real world, understanding how they cooperate or compete with each other is critical for safety and efficiency.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on game theory — the mathematical study of strategic interactions between rational agents — and prior work showing that cooperation in social dilemmas becomes possible when agents know they make decisions similarly (monocultural ecosystems). It draws on the Prisoner's Dilemma, a classic game-theoretic scenario where individually rational choices lead to collectively poor outcomes. The authors connect this to LLM behavior by asking: if we tell an LLM that another agent thinks similarly to it, will it cooperate more? The underlying assumption is that LLMs can serve as computational agents with identifiable decision-making patterns, and that these patterns can be detected and communicated via "similarity signals" (explicit information about how alike two agents' reasoning processes are).

## Core Contributions
1. **First framework for evaluating LLM cooperation under graded similarity signals** — prior work assumed binary knowledge of similarity; this paper introduces a spectrum of similarity information and measures its effect.
2. **Empirical demonstration that LLM cooperation is highly model-dependent** — models vary drastically in whether and how they exploit similarity signals across different games and payoff structures.
3. **Surprising finding: dataset choice for computing similarity has minimal impact** — the source data used to measure similarity doesn't strongly determine cooperation outcomes.
4. **Self-identification bias** — LLMs systematically rate other models as highly similar to themselves when asked to evaluate their reasoning.
5. **LLM-behavioral game-theoretic model** — a formal model capturing how LLMs reason about similarity and cooperation, showing equilibrium cooperation is possible at high similarity thresholds.

## Practical Applications
1. **AI ecosystem governance** — if multiple LLM-based systems are deployed by different organizations, understanding how they cooperate or defect in resource allocation, pricing, or information sharing becomes operationally critical.
2. **Autonomous negotiation systems** — two AI agents negotiating a contract or trade agreement could benefit from explicit similarity signaling to reach mutually beneficial terms faster.
3. **Multi-agent reinforcement learning training** — curriculum design for training teams of AI agents could leverage similarity signals to encourage cooperation and avoid wasteful conflict.
4. **Marketplace design** — platforms hosting multiple AI agents (e.g., trading bots, recommendation engines) might use similarity disclosure to encourage pro-social behavior.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Cooperation Auditor** — a SaaS platform that tests whether deployed LLM-based agents will cooperate or defect under various scenarios, with similarity-signal recommendations to improve outcomes.
2. **Multi-Agent Orchestration Platform** — infrastructure that manages multiple LLMs in shared environments, automatically computing and communicating similarity signals to maximize system-wide cooperation and efficiency.
3. **Game-Theoretic API for LLMs** — a library/API that wraps LLM calls with game-theoretic analysis, automatically detecting strategic interactions and suggesting cooperation-enhancing prompts or signals.
4. **AI Agent Reputation & Matchmaking Service** — a service that profiles LLM agents' decision-making patterns and matches compatible agents (high similarity) for collaborative tasks, with transparency fees for similarity-signal access.
5. **Cooperative Multi-Model Inference Engine** — a system that splits inference workloads across multiple LLMs and uses similarity signals to ensure they coordinate decisions (useful for redundancy, adversarial robustness, or domain specialization).

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited model diversity** — the paper tests a handful of LLM architectures; generalization to future models or closed-source proprietary systems is uncertain.
2. **Artificial game environments** — Prisoner's Dilemma and similar games are simplified; real-world strategic interactions (negotiation, resource allocation) have richer structure and information asymmetries.
3. **Prompt engineering sensitivity** — LLM behavior is notoriously sensitive to phrasing; the paper acknowledges some robustness across framings but doesn't exhaustively test all reasonable prompt variants.
4. **No alignment with human norms** — cooperation in the game-theoretic sense may not align with human values; two agents cooperating to exploit a third party is still problematic.
5. **Self-identification bias unresolved** — the finding that LLMs rate themselves as similar to others is concerning and suggests the similarity signal may be manipulable, but the paper doesn't fully explore adversarial robustness.
6. **Limited behavioral realism** — LLMs don't have persistent memory, evolving preferences, or true strategic reasoning; they're pattern-matching engines, which may limit applicability to repeated real-world interactions.

## Open Questions
1. How do similarity signals interact with adversarial or deceptive prompting? Can an LLM be tricked into thinking it's similar to an agent that will exploit it?
2. What happens in iterated games over many rounds? Do LLMs learn to defect after initially cooperating, or maintain stable strategies?
3. How do larger or smaller LLMs respond differently to similarity signals? Is there a scaling law for cooperation?
4. Can we design similarity signals that correlate with *actual* value alignment rather than just surface-level reasoning similarity?
5. How do LLMs behave when similarity is partial or contradictory (e.g., similar on some dimensions, dissimilar on others)?
6. In real-world applications, who controls the similarity signal, and what prevents gaming or manipulation?

## Key Takeaway
LLM agents cooperate more when told they're similar to each other, but individual models respond inconsistently to this signal.
