# PrivDrift: Auditing User-Secret Leakage Under Topic Drift in Active LLM Conversations

**Authors:** Luciano Maldonado
**Published:** 2026-09-24 | **arXiv:** [2609.30094v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30094v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces PrivDrift, a benchmark for testing whether language models leak user secrets even after conversations shift to unrelated topics. The work shows that current LLMs retain sensitive information throughout extended dialogues—38–55% leakage rates across tested models—suggesting privacy risks are a persistent failure mode rather than just a training-data memorization problem. This matters because LLMs now act as persistent assistants that users trust with sensitive information over long conversations.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two distinct research streams. First, it extends work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] and information retention in neural networks—how trained models encode and preserve information through their parameters and activations. Second, it engages with emerging literature on LLM safety and [[alignment]], particularly how models behave under adversarial or persuasion-based prompting (related to jailbreak research). The core tension is between two privacy threat models: (1) static memorization of training data, which existing defenses target, and (2) active behavioral leakage during live conversations, which is understudied. The paper treats privacy not as a training-phase problem but as a runtime behavioral property—once a model generates a response containing a secret, that secret becomes embedded in the conversation's token sequence and context window, making it available to future inference steps.

## Core Contributions
1. **PrivDrift Benchmark**: A structured evaluation dataset with 1,000 multi-turn dialogues containing seeded secrets, controlled topic-drift turns, and standardized extraction probes—enabling reproducible measurement of conversational privacy leakage.

2. **Empirical Finding on Topic Drift**: Demonstrates that additional topical drift within extended context windows does NOT reliably reduce secret leakage, contradicting the intuition that changing subjects should "bury" sensitive information.

3. **Privacy-as-Behavioral-Failure Framing**: Shifts the analytical lens from memorization and jailbreaking to persistent behavioral leakage—secrets remain recoverable not because they're in training data, but because the model's decision-making processes continue to condition on previously revealed information.

4. **Model and Secret-Type Variation Analysis**: Shows that leakage rates vary substantially by model architecture, secret type (e.g., financial vs. personal), and persuasion strategy intensity, enabling more granular risk characterization.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[RLHF]]-era safety research and mechanistic studies of context usage in transformers. It complements recent work on long-context failures (e.g., papers on position bias and information loss in extended windows) but focuses specifically on *retention* rather than forgetting. It differs from standard privacy-preserving ML (differential privacy, federated learning) by accepting that models will be deployed without technical guarantees and instead auditing residual risks. The persuasion-based extraction methodology echoes jailbreak research but treats it as a privacy audit tool rather than a security boundary test.

## Practical Applications
1. **Compliance & Risk Assessment**: Regulatory bodies (finance, healthcare) need concrete leakage metrics to assess whether LLM-based assistants meet privacy obligations (GDPR, HIPAA). PrivDrift provides quantified benchmarks.

2. **User Warnings & Transparency**: Consumer-facing LLM apps (ChatGPT-like assistants) can use results to inform users that sensitive disclosures may remain recoverable mid-conversation, supporting informed consent.

3. **Model Procurement Decisions**: Enterprise buyers comparing LLM vendors can use PrivDrift-style benchmarks as a due-diligence filter to avoid high-leakage models in sensitive domains.

4. **Context Window Policy Design**: Organizations deploying LLMs can set session or context-length limits based on empirically measured leakage curves, trading utility for privacy.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Privacy Audit-as-a-Service**: Offer PrivDrift-based testing as a SaaS product for enterprises deploying LLMs—benchmarks their models against standardized secret types and reports leakage percentiles.

2. **Conversation Privacy Sandbox**: A browser/app extension that runs PrivDrift-style extraction tests on LLM outputs in real time, warning users before they send sensitive data to a specific model.

3. **LLM Privacy Scorecard**: A public ranking/database (like model cards) showing leakage profiles for all major LLM vendors, comparable to energy ratings or security certifications.

4. **Context-Aware Redaction Engine**: Middleware that sits between user and LLM, automatically detects and quarantines sensitive topics, then selectively clears context or injects privacy prompts as drift occurs.

5. **Regulatory Compliance Toolkit**: Pre-built policies and audit templates for legal/compliance teams to test LLM assistants against PrivDrift benchmarks and generate audit reports for regulators.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited Model Coverage**: Only three LLMs tested; results may not generalize to smaller open-source models, older architectures, or future designs with different context management.

2. **Synthetic Secrets**: The benchmark uses "seeded" secrets (artificially injected into dialogue), not organic secrets users would naturally disclose. Behavioral leakage of realistic secrets may differ.

3. **Extraction Methodology**: The paper uses persuasion-based probing (explicit requests to recall secrets), which is a specific attack. Other extraction methods (indirect inference, statistical analysis of token probabilities) may yield different leakage rates.

4. **Drift Window Scope**: The drift window tested is bounded; behavior over truly massive context windows (100k+ tokens) or across multiple sessions is unclear.

5. **No Mitigation Evaluation**: The paper audits the problem but does not systematically evaluate potential defenses (e.g., prompt-based privacy instructions, context truncation, fine-tuning), limiting actionability for practitioners.

6. **Conversation Dynamics**: Real conversations are heterogeneous; the controlled, multi-turn template may not capture the complexity of organic user behavior or LLM adaptive responses.

## Open Questions
1. **Why does topic drift fail to reduce leakage?** Is it a limitation of attention mechanisms, training dynamics, or context packing? Can mechanistic analysis of model internals explain the phenomenon?

2. **What's the theoretical leakage floor?** Is there a lower bound below which leakage cannot be driven, or can it be eliminated with the right architecture/training?

3. **Do mitigation strategies work?** Which of these reduce leakage: explicit privacy prompts, context truncation, fine-tuning on privacy-aware datasets, or architectural changes (e.g., limited attention scope)?

4. **Cross-session persistence**: Do secrets leaked in one conversation session increase leakage in future sessions with the same model (shared parameters)?

5. **Adversarial prompt optimization**: Can an attacker craft more effective extraction prompts than those in the benchmark, driving leakage even higher?

6. **User studies**: Do real users disclose secrets differently in conversations they believe are privacy-preserving? Does awareness of this paper's findings change behavior?

## Key Takeaway
Language models leak user secrets throughout conversations even after topic changes; privacy is a runtime behavior problem, not just training-data memorization.
