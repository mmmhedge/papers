# LLM2Jev: LLMs Are Already Jev-Style Decision Models -- When and How to Fine-Tune Them

**Authors:** Yinheng Li, Justin Wagle
**Published:** 2026-10-01 | **arXiv:** [2610.02076v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.02076v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether general-purpose LLMs can directly output categorical decisions (like multiple-choice answers) without generating free-form text, and when fine-tuning is needed. The authors present LLM2Jev, a framework that extracts calibrated decisions from a model's token probabilities, showing that modern LLMs are already effective decision models out-of-the-box — but fine-tuning helps primarily for weaker models and specific tasks like intent routing.

## Theoretical Background
Large language models (neural networks trained to predict the next token in a sequence) naturally assign probabilities to all possible next tokens. Traditional approaches either generate text answers or use "letter-logit readouts" (reading out probabilities of A, B, C, D tokens). This paper builds on the insight that LLMs already assign probabilities to numeric tokens in brackets, which can be read directly as decision scores. The work anchors fine-tuning objectives using KL divergence penalties — a divergence measure between probability distributions — to prevent the model from degrading at its original task (conversational generation) while optimizing for categorical decision-making.

## Core Contributions
1. **LLM2Jev framework**: A training-free recipe to extract calibrated decisions directly from next-token probabilities over bracketed numeric identifiers, without architectural changes.
2. **Empirical finding**: Modern LLMs (tested on Qwen 3.5-4B and Qwen 3-0.6B) are inherently effective decision models; the 4B model matches purpose-built Jev-style baselines without any fine-tuning.
3. **Fine-tuning strategy**: A tree-factorized listwise loss with KL-divergence anchors that improves weaker models and specific tasks (e.g., many-option intent routing) while preserving conversational ability.
4. **Flexibility**: The framework supports arbitrary numbers of options and natively handles multimodal decisions (e.g., decisions over images).

## Practical Applications
1. **Chatbot intent classification**: Routing user messages to the correct service (customer support, sales, billing) without generating explanatory text.
2. **Medical triage systems**: Assigning patient cases to appropriate specialists or urgency levels based on symptoms.
3. **Content moderation**: Categorizing user posts as safe, unsafe, review-needed, etc., for system-level enforcement.
4. **E-commerce product categorization**: Classifying user queries into product categories for rapid retrieval.
5. **Multi-turn dialogue state tracking**: Extracting discrete dialogue acts (user intent, slot values) from conversation history.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Decision API**: A hosted service wrapping LLM2Jev for enterprises to add categorical decision layers to their LLM pipelines without retraining, with fallback to GPT for edge cases.
2. **Low-Latency Intent Router**: A lightweight inference engine that uses small fine-tuned Jev-style models for intent routing at the edge, reducing API calls to larger models by 70%+.
3. **Modular Classification Blocks**: Pre-fine-tuned decision models for common taxonomies (intent, sentiment, urgency, category) that plug into any LLM-based product, reducing per-task labeling overhead.
4. **Multimodal Triage Platform**: A healthcare or logistics SaaS that uses LLM2Jev to classify images + text (e.g., injury photos + patient description) into triage buckets, enabling faster dispatch.
5. **Model Efficiency Toolkit**: Developer tools to profile when an LLM actually needs fine-tuning for a decision task vs. already performs well zero-shot, helping teams avoid unnecessary compute spend.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited backbone diversity**: Only evaluated on Qwen models; generalization to other architectures (Llama, Mistral, GPT) is untested.
2. **Calibration assumption**: Assumes next-token probabilities over numeric tokens are well-calibrated confidence scores, which may not hold uniformly across model sizes or domains.
3. **Option representation bias**: Numeric bracketed format may not be equally accessible in all training distributions; some models may have higher affinity for certain digits.
4. **Scalability to very large option spaces**: Performance with hundreds or thousands of options is not explored; computational complexity of listwise loss may become prohibitive.
5. **Multimodal evaluation sparsity**: Multimodal results mentioned briefly but not rigorously evaluated; vision-language alignment in decision-making is underexplored.
6. **Task diversity**: Evaluation focuses on intent routing and classification; applicability to more nuanced decision tasks (ranking, open-ended scoring) is unclear.

## Open Questions
1. **Why does the 4B model match Jev baselines out-of-the-box?** Is this a quirk of Qwen's training, or do all modern LLMs encode natural decision-making ability? Can you predict which tasks benefit from fine-tuning a priori?
2. **What is the ceiling for zero-shot decision performance?** Does scaling (larger backbone) always help, or is there a saturation point where task-specific fine-tuning becomes necessary?
3. **How does KL anchoring trade off between decision accuracy and conversational fidelity?** Can you tune this trade-off dynamically per input?
4. **Can you extend this to ranking or scoring tasks** (not just categorical classification)? Does the framework generalize to continuous or ordinal outputs?
5. **What happens with out-of-distribution options** — i.e., when inference requires choosing between options the model has never seen during fine-tuning?

## Key Takeaway
Modern LLMs already make good categorical decisions without fine-tuning; targeted tuning helps weaker models and specific tasks while preserving text generation.
