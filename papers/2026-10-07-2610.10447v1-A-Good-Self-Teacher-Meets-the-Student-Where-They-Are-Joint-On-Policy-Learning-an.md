# A Good Self-Teacher Meets the Student Where They Are: Joint On-Policy Learning and Teaching

**Authors:** Randy Ardywibowo, Arnav Dalal, Jiantao Jiao
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10447v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10447v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental problem in training AI agents: when a stronger teacher trains a weaker student through demonstration, the teacher often takes "shortcuts" the student can't follow, providing unhelpful guidance. The authors derive conditions for when teaching actually helps, and propose JOLT—a method where a single policy learns to be both a good teacher and a good student, using KL regularization to keep the teacher's solutions aligned with what the student can actually learn from. Testing on math, coding, and tool-use tasks shows this improves learning efficiency.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on [[On-Policy Distillation]] and self-distillation approaches, which try to solve the sparse-reward problem in reinforcement learning by using dense supervision from a teacher policy instead of waiting for rare successful outcomes. Prior work assumes that a high-performing teacher automatically provides good guidance, but this paper shows that's not always true—especially when the teacher can exploit "privileged information" (e.g., intermediate steps or oracle outputs) that the student doesn't have access to at test time. The key insight draws on gradient alignment theory: the paper characterizes when a distillation update actually moves the student in the direction of improving rewards (mathematically, when the KL divergence toward the teacher acts as a positive multiple of the reward gradient).

## Core Contributions
1. **Necessary and sufficient condition for positive distillation**: Derives a formal criterion showing when a privileged teacher's update helps rather than harms the student—the teacher must balance task performance with staying close to the student's own behavior distribution.

2. **JOLT algorithm**: Proposes joint training of a single policy in two roles using a KL-regularized objective for the teacher role and on-policy distillation for the student role, avoiding the need for separate teacher and student models.

3. **Empirical validation across diverse domains**: Demonstrates improvements in training efficiency and final performance on mathematical reasoning, code generation, tool use, and terminal use tasks—showing the method generalizes beyond toy problems.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[reinforcement learning]], [[generative models]] (for sequential decision-making), and [[alignment]] concerns. It extends [[On-Policy Distillation]] by solving a critical failure mode: misaligned supervision. The KL-regularization approach echoes techniques in [[RLHF]] and [[DPO (Direct Preference Optimization)]], which also use divergence penalties to keep learned policies grounded. The gradient alignment analysis connects to interpretability work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] of policy updates.

## Practical Applications
1. **Language model training at scale**: Reducing sample efficiency in LLM fine-tuning for reasoning tasks (math, code) where ground-truth labels are expensive.
2. **Robotics and control**: Training embodied agents where oracle demonstrations are expensive but intermediate feedback is available (e.g., vision-based manipulation).
3. **Complex game playing**: Training agents on long-horizon games where successful trajectories are rare and curriculum-like learning is essential.
4. **Autonomous systems**: Improving efficiency of training for tool-use agents (APIs, terminal commands) where sparse success signals are the bottleneck.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Math + coding tutor SaaS**: Fine-tune open-source LLMs on customer problem datasets using JOLT for higher accuracy with fewer labeled examples—sell to educational platforms.
2. **Robotics-as-a-Service training platform**: Offer efficient policy training for robotic manipulation tasks using privileged simulation data; charge per trained policy deployed.
3. **Enterprise code generation**: Build a model-as-API service optimized for domain-specific code generation (e.g., SQL, Kubernetes configs) using JOLT for faster task-specific adaptation.
4. **RL benchmarking SaaS**: Provide a platform for companies to benchmark and train agents on their environments (games, simulations) using JOLT's efficient learning.
5. **Vertical AI agents**: Develop domain-specific agents (e.g., legal document analysis, financial analysis) that self-improve via JOLT-style training on proprietary datasets.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Single policy / scalability trade-off**: Using one policy for both teacher and student may bottleneck performance on very large-scale domains where the two roles require different capacities.
2. **KL-regularization hyperparameter**: The method introduces tuning burden for the KL coefficient; unclear how sensitive results are across different task families or reward scales.
3. **Privilege structure assumption**: The theoretical result assumes a specific form of privileged information (e.g., token-level feedback); generalization to other privilege structures is unstudied.
4. **Computational cost not detailed**: No analysis of wall-clock time or compute cost compared to baseline—joint training could be expensive.
5. **Limited diversity in evaluation**: Tested mainly on language-model-based tasks (reasoning, coding); unclear if insights transfer to continuous control or vision-based domains.
6. **No exploration of conflicting roles**: What happens when the teacher and student objectives strongly conflict? The paper doesn't deeply explore failure modes.

## Open Questions
1. Can you decompose the method into separate teacher and student networks without losing the benefits—is the joint training essential, or is the KL regularization the real secret sauce?
2. How does performance scale with the degree of privilege gap (e.g., what if the teacher only has slightly privileged information)?
3. Does JOLT help when combined with other modern techniques like [[DPO]], majority voting, or mixture-of-experts routing?
4. Can the gradient alignment condition be extended to hierarchical or multi-stage RL settings (e.g., meta-learning)?
5. What is the empirical sample complexity improvement compared to outcome-reward RL as a function of task horizon and reward sparsity?

## Key Takeaway
Training a policy to be its own teacher, regularized to stay close to the student's capabilities, improves learning efficiency on long-horizon tasks.
