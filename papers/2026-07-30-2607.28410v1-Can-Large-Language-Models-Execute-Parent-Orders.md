# Can Large Language Models Execute Parent Orders?

**Authors:** Zane Shen, Xinli Xu, Guangyi Zhang, Jialong Chen, Jinsong Zhou et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28410v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28410v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether large language models can effectively execute large stock orders by breaking them into smaller pieces over time—a core problem in algorithmic trading. The authors propose PACE, a framework that uses LLMs to plan and execute orders without needing pre-specified market models or task-specific training, and show it outperforms traditional and learning-based methods on real stock exchange data.

## Theoretical Background
Parent-order execution is the problem of splitting a large trade order into smaller sub-orders to minimize costs (execution slippage and market impact). Traditional approaches like TWAP (Time-Weighted Average Price) execute uniformly over time, while Almgren-Chriss models optimize execution under explicit assumptions about market dynamics and volatility. Recent work has explored reinforcement learning for adaptive execution, but these methods often require retraining for new assets or market regimes. LLMs have shown surprising capability in financial reasoning tasks—from price prediction to investment analysis—by leveraging their ability to capture patterns from training data without explicit feature engineering.

## Core Contributions
1. **First systematic LLM application to order execution**: Extends LLM use in finance from trading decision (what to buy) to execution strategy (how to buy).
2. **PACE framework**: A two-level hierarchy where LLMs first plan a high-level execution trajectory (when to trade more/less), then execute short-term decisions (exact timing and volume). This decomposition avoids both explicit market assumptions and task-specific training.
3. **Empirical outperformance**: On Shenzhen Stock Exchange data, PACE beats TWAP, Almgren-Chriss, and learning baselines by 0.65 basis points, a meaningful margin in trading.
4. **Behavioral insights**: LLMs trade differently from humans—they trade *earlier* in the window and their confidence in decisions *correlates positively* with returns (unlike human overconfidence), suggesting complementary decision-making.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[reinforcement-learning]] approaches to execution (which learn adaptive policies but need retraining) and classical finance models (which are interpretable but brittle). It parallels recent applications of LLMs to [[market-microstructure]] problems and financial time-series forecasting. The hierarchical decomposition (long-horizon planning + short-horizon execution) echoes hierarchical RL and planning methods, though applied here via prompting rather than explicit policy learning. The behavioral analysis connects to [[mechanistic-interpretability]] questions: how do LLMs make financial decisions differently from human heuristics?

## Practical Applications
1. **Institutional trade execution**: Asset managers and hedge funds can use PACE to execute large blocks more cheaply, directly improving returns.
2. **Responsive execution in volatile markets**: Because the model adapts without retraining, it can handle regime changes (earnings announcements, macro shocks) that break traditional models.
3. **Multi-asset execution**: Train once, apply to new stocks or asset classes without retraining—useful for brokers managing diverse portfolios.
4. **Compliance and transparency**: LLM reasoning can be logged and explained, potentially better than black-box RL for regulatory purposes.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **PACE-as-a-Service API**: White-label execution algorithm for retail brokerages and fintechs, charging per trade or as % of savings captured.
2. **Smart execution for pension funds**: Institutional product that learns client-specific constraints and risk aversion, lowering execution costs for large endowments.
3. **Market-adaptive order router**: Route parent orders across multiple venues (lit exchanges, dark pools, wholesalers) using PACE to optimize venue and timing.
4. **Real-time execution assistant for human traders**: Desktop tool that suggests when/how to execute blocks, with confidence scores to help traders override when domain knowledge says otherwise.
5. **FX and commodity execution**: Extend to less-liquid markets (currency pairs, commodities) where traditional models struggle; charge % of spread saved.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited to liquid, quoted instruments**: Paper uses Level-1 data (bid/ask only); doesn't address block trading, OTC markets, or extreme illiquidity.
2. **No explicit transaction costs**: Doesn't model broker fees, exchange rebates, or crossing networks that real traders exploit.
3. **Backtesting only**: No live trading results; LLM behavior under real market stress or with latency constraints is unknown.
4. **Scalability unclear**: How does PACE perform on much larger orders that move the market significantly? Does the model's training (likely on smaller institutional trades) transfer?
5. **Prompt sensitivity**: Success may depend heavily on how execution instructions are phrased; no ablation on prompt engineering.
6. **Causal claims weak**: Claim that LLM confidence predicts better returns, but this could reflect the model overfitting to in-sample noise.

## Open Questions
1. **Out-of-distribution markets**: Does PACE generalize to new exchanges, asset classes, or market regimes (e.g., flash crashes, rate hikes) without retraining?
2. **Adversarial robustness**: Can sophisticated market makers exploit predictable LLM behavior? How does performance degrade as more traders use similar strategies?
3. **Latency and real-time constraints**: How much does adding realistic network delays and order-book staleness hurt performance?
4. **Explainability**: Can we understand *why* the LLM trades early vs. late? Does mechanistic interpretability help us improve or trust the model?
5. **Hybrid execution**: How does PACE compare to human-in-the-loop approaches (human sets bounds, LLM optimizes within them)?
6. **Multi-objective trade-offs**: How does the model balance execution cost vs. information leakage (avoiding signaling your intent to the market)?

## Key Takeaway
Large language models can split and execute large stock orders more cheaply than traditional algorithms, without retraining for new markets.
