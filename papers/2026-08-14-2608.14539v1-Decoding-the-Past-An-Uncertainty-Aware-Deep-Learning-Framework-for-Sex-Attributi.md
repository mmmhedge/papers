# Decoding the Past: An Uncertainty-Aware Deep Learning Framework for Sex Attribution in Prehistoric Hand Stencils

**Authors:** Karel Becerra, Boris Mederos, Dean Snow, Ramón A. Mollineda
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14539v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14539v1)

## Summary
This paper applies deep learning with explicit uncertainty quantification to determine the biological sex of individuals who created prehistoric hand stencils from the Upper Paleolithic period. The researchers built an ensemble framework that combines multiple neural networks and validation techniques to handle the degradation and ambiguity inherent in ancient rock art, producing both predictions and confidence measures that distinguish between anatomically clear and ambiguous cases.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on two complementary research streams. First, traditional morphometric analysis — measuring physical features of skeletal structures to infer biological traits — has long been used in archaeology and anthropology, but suffers from high overlap between male and female hand dimensions and poor generalization across different populations. Second, the paper draws on modern deep learning for image classification, particularly ensemble methods (combining multiple neural networks to reduce error) and uncertainty quantification frameworks that treat predictions not as point estimates but as probability distributions. The paper specifically leverages EfficientNet and MobileViT architectures — efficient convolutional neural networks designed for resource-constrained deployment — and incorporates explainability techniques like LayerCAM (which visualizes which image regions most influenced a model's decision) and unsupervised dimensionality reduction via UMAP (which maps high-dimensional data into 2D space for visualization and clustering).

## Core Contributions
1. **Uncertainty propagation pipeline**: Instead of treating image degradation as noise to minimize, the framework explicitly models it by generating twelve plausible silhouette variations per stencil through dual image processing and structured augmentation, then propagates this uncertainty through twin ensembles (20 total neural networks across two architectures).

2. **Triangulated validation scheme**: Combines three independent analytical streams — ensemble classification, unsupervised latent-space analysis (UMAP + k-NN), and spatial attribution maps (LayerCAM) — to cross-validate predictions and flag anatomically inconsistent results.

3. **Uncertainty as a measurable archaeological signal**: Rather than collapsing uncertainty into a single prediction, the system outputs confidence measures of inter-ensemble agreement, enabling archaeological interpretation that distinguishes between morphologically stable and ambiguous specimens.

4. **Demonstrated performance on prehistoric data**: Achieves >88% accuracy on contemporary hands and produces interpretable, anatomically consistent predictions on actual Upper Paleolithic stencils despite the absence of ground truth labels.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[computer vision]] (hand segmentation and classification from degraded images), [[ensemble learning]] methods, and uncertainty quantification in deep learning — a growing area driven by the need for trustworthy AI in high-stakes domains. It parallels recent work in medical imaging that uses ensemble methods and uncertainty to handle noisy or incomplete data. The explainability component (LayerCAM and latent-space inspection) aligns with the broader push toward [[mechanistic interpretability]] in deep learning, particularly for applications where understanding *why* the model decided something matters as much as *what* it decided. The archaeological application mirrors the challenge faced in other humanities-adjacent domains (paleontology, art history) where ground truth is unavailable and validation must rely on multiple indirect signals rather than labeled data.

## Practical Applications
1. **Archaeological site analysis**: Rapidly screen and sex-attribute hand stencil collections at large rock art sites, enabling population-level inference about who created rock art across different periods and regions.

2. **Forensic anthropology**: Similar methodology could apply to modern degraded or fragmentary skeletal remains where morphological sex estimation is uncertain, producing confidence-stratified results for case prioritization.

3. **Museum digitization and reanalysis**: Systematically re-examine existing hand stencil collections in museum archives or databases using the framework, enabling cross-institutional comparative studies with quantified uncertainty.

4. **Educational archaeology tools**: Build interactive systems that let students or researchers upload hand stencil images and receive both predictions and explanatory visualizations of which anatomical features drove the classification.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Training data bias**: The model is trained on 14,036 contemporary hands, which are drawn from modern populations with different average hand dimensions and morphology than Upper Paleolithic populations; cross-population generalization is explicitly acknowledged but may still fail for ancestrally divergent groups.

2. **Absence of ground truth in test data**: Prehistoric stencils have no definitive sex labels, so validation relies on internal consistency checks (ensemble agreement, latent-space structure, anatomical plausibility) rather than held-out accuracy. A stencil could be consistently wrong across all validation signals if a systematic bias exists.

3. **Image degradation assumptions**: The framework assumes degradation follows learnable patterns. If stencils were made with highly variable techniques, pigments, or preservation conditions, the twelve augmented silhouettes may not capture the true uncertainty distribution.

4. **Silhouette extraction brittleness**: Contour extraction is deterministic (or nearly so) and depends on image quality; poor photographs or unusual pigmentation patterns could produce silhouettes that don't represent the original hand, introducing errors before the neural network sees them.

5. **Ensemble overhead**: Running 20 neural networks per stencil is computationally expensive; the framework may not scale to very large datasets without specialized hardware or model compression.

6. **Limited anatomical validation**: While LayerCAM shows which regions influenced predictions, the framework doesn't embed explicit anatomical priors (e.g., the biology of sexual dimorphism in hand bones); relying on learned features risks capturing spurious correlations.

## Open Questions
1. How much of the ensemble disagreement on prehistoric stencils reflects genuine ambiguity in the morphology versus artifacts of training on a different population? Could fine-tuning on ancient skeletal datasets (if available) improve transfer?

2. Does the uncertainty quantification correlate with actual classification error on a held-out contemporary test set, and does it generalize to other body parts (foot prints, finger marks)?

3. Can the framework integrate additional modalities — e.g., hand size measurements, pigment composition, or contextual dating — to improve confidence, or does adding non-image features break the ensemble structure?

4. How sensitive is the latent-space validation (UMAP + k-NN) to hyperparameter choices (n_neighbors, metric), and do different choices substantially change which prehistoric stencils are flagged as ambiguous?

5. Could the framework be extended to multi-class problems (sex + age estimation, or ancestry inference) while maintaining uncertainty quantification?

## Key Takeaway
Deep learning ensembles with explicit uncertainty can reliably sex-attribute degraded prehistoric hand images by measuring disagreement across multiple analytical pathways.
