# OmegaUse-OfficeVal: Benchmarking LLM Agents on Long-Horizon Office-Suite Tasks with Economic Grounding

**Authors:** Jingbo Zhou, Yusai Zhao, Qi Bao, Jingjia Cao, Zhenghai Chen et al.
**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **arXiv:** [2607.27155v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27155v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces OmegaUse-OfficeVal, a benchmark for testing whether LLM agents can complete real office tasks (like spreadsheet work, document editing, scheduling) at a reasonable cost. The key innovation is pairing each task with economic signals — human labor time and estimated task price — so you can directly compare whether using an LLM is cheaper and faster than hiring a human. Testing shows LLMs are faster and cheaper than humans but don't yet match human-level quality on complex multi-step office workflows.

## Theoretical Background
This work builds on the emerging field of LLM agents — systems that use large language models not just to generate text, but to plan and execute multi-step tasks by calling external tools (APIs, code execution, file systems). Prior benchmarks like WebShop and Mind2Web evaluated agents on web tasks, but lacked economic grounding — a way to measure whether an AI solution is actually practical and cost-effective compared to human labor. The paper assumes that office-suite applications (Microsoft Office, Google Workspace) are a natural testbed because they are widespread, involve diverse task types, and have clear human labor baselines for comparison.

## Core Contributions
1. **Economic grounding framework**: Each of 100 tasks is paired with human labor time (averaging 2.32 hours per task) and a task price proxy, enabling cost-benefit analysis between human and LLM approaches — not just success/failure metrics.
2. **Real-world task curation**: Tasks are derived from actual practitioner requests and privacy-preserving adapted, making the benchmark more realistic than synthetic alternatives.
3. **Fine-grained code-based verifiers**: Rubric-driven automated checkers for stable, reproducible evaluation of deliverable quality, addressing the challenge that office-suite outputs are complex and subjective.
4. **Comprehensive frontier LLM evaluation**: Systematic assessment of state-of-the-art models, showing cost/speed advantage over humans but persistent quality gaps.

## Connections to Other Work
This extends the broader trajectory of agent benchmarking (e.g., [[WebShop]], [[Mind2Web]]) by explicitly integrating economics — an angle most prior work overlooks. It relates to [[RLHF]] and alignment in that it measures whether agent behavior matches human expectations on practical tasks. The cost-comparison framing echoes work on AI cost-effectiveness in [[quantitative finance]] and labor displacement, though applied to office workflows rather than trading or scientific computing.

## Practical Applications
1. **Enterprise AI assistant deployment**: Companies can use this benchmark to decide whether adopting an LLM agent for routine office tasks (report generation, meeting scheduling, data aggregation) pays for itself relative to hiring temporary staff.
2. **Hybrid human-AI workflows**: Identifying which tasks are best delegated to LLMs (e.g., data entry, formatting) and which require human oversight (strategic decisions, complex edits).
3. **SaaS pricing and packaging**: Office software vendors can use the benchmark to design LLM-assisted features with transparent ROI expectations for customers.
4. **Regulatory and procurement decisions**: Government and enterprise IT departments can make vendor-neutral assessments of whether LLM agents meet cost and quality thresholds for production use.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Office Agent-as-a-Service**: A SaaS platform that integrates with Google Workspace / Microsoft 365 to automatically handle recurring office tasks (weekly reports, expense summaries, calendar optimization) with cost and quality metrics visible to users.
2. **Benchmarking-as-a-Service for enterprise AI**: A consulting tool where companies upload their own office workflows and get a custom cost-benefit analysis comparing LLM agents vs. human labor + recommendations for task delegation.
3. **Task-level micro-payments for office AI**: A platform where freelancers bid on office-suite tasks evaluated by OfficeVal-style verifiers, allowing companies to see predicted LLM cost vs. human bid side-by-side before deciding.
4. **Quality assurance layer for office automation**: A monitoring and verification service that runs OfficeVal rubrics on LLM agent outputs in production, flagging deliverables below quality thresholds and routing to humans.
5. **Skills-based office workforce training**: A B2B tool that uses OfficeVal tasks to benchmark and train human office staff, showing employees where they lag or excel compared to LLM performance — helping them focus on high-value, non-automatable work.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Quality-cost tradeoff is task-specific**: The benchmark shows LLMs are cheap but lower quality overall, but doesn't deeply analyze which task categories are closest to production-ready and which remain far from human performance — limiting practical deployment guidance.
2. **Economic grounding is coarse**: Task price proxy and labor time are estimates or averages; real cost varies widely by geography, skill level, and context. Doesn't account for training data, model updates, or long-term infrastructure costs.
3. **Limited scope to office-suite**: Results may not generalize to other LLM agent domains (scientific computing, coding, logistics). Office workflows have specific structure that may not reflect challenges in other domains.
4. **Human baseline is synthetic**: Human labor time is self-reported or estimated; no experiments with real human workers on the same tasks, so quality comparisons may not be apples-to-apples if humans operate under different time pressure or incentives.
5. **Static task set**: 100 tasks is modest; no discussion of how results change as LLMs improve, new models emerge, or office-suite APIs evolve.

## Open Questions
1. **Where is the quality ceiling?** Are LLMs failing on specific task types (e.g., tasks requiring domain knowledge, creative judgment, or multi-document synthesis) or is the gap more uniform? Fine-grained error analysis by task category would guide research.
2. **Does multi-agent coordination help?** Could a system of specialized agents (one for spreadsheets, one for documents, one for scheduling) outperform single monolithic agents on complex workflows?
3. **How do different inference cost models (batch vs. real-time, on-device vs. cloud, model-specific pricing) change the economics?** Sensitivity analysis would make the benchmark more actionable.
4. **Can rubric-based verification be automated further, or does human-in-the-loop verification become necessary for edge cases?** What is the cost of perfect verification?
5. **Does fine-tuning on office-specific tasks (as opposed to frontier base models) close the quality gap at acceptable cost?** Transfer learning from OfficeVal tasks to proprietary agent systems would be a natural follow-up.

## Key Takeaway
LLM agents can handle office tasks cheaper and faster than humans, but quality still falls short of human performance in production scenarios.
