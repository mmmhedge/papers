# Competitive mediator games and urban CAV routing markets

**Authors:** Grzegorz Jamróz
**Published:** 2026-08-10 | **arXiv:** [2608.09894v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09894v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces "competitive mediator games," a new game-theoretic framework that generalizes correlated equilibria to model markets where multiple intermediaries compete to route autonomous vehicles. The work proves that under certain conditions, these markets naturally collapse to monopolies, and applies these insights to understand future autonomous routing and driving (ARAD) service markets.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on game theory foundations: Nash equilibria (strategy profiles where no player benefits from unilateral deviation) and correlated equilibria (randomized strategy recommendations that no player wants to deviate from). Recent work has shown correlated equilibria can be more socially efficient than Nash equilibria and emerge naturally from multi-agent learning algorithms with no-regret dynamics (where agents minimize cumulative losses over time). The paper also draws on congestion games — models where many agents choose routes or resources, and the cost to each agent depends on how congested their choice is. The new framework adds mediators (intermediaries who propose routing strategies) competing in a market setting.

## Core Contributions
1. **Formal model**: Introduces competitive mediator games as a generalization of correlated equilibrium concepts, enabling analysis of competing intermediaries in routing markets.
2. **Monopoly characterization theorem**: Proves that in anonymous congestion games with market-share-maximizing mediators, if one mediator is weakly preferred by all users, then all competitive mediator equilibria result in monopoly (that mediator captures the entire market).
3. **ARAD market analysis**: Applies the theoretical results to autonomous vehicle routing markets, showing how competition between routing service providers may naturally concentrate.

## Connections to Other Work
This extends the recent surge in [[correlated equilibria]] and their computational properties. It connects to mechanism design literature on intermediation and platform competition. The work relates to multi-agent systems where agents use learning dynamics, and to routing and congestion game analysis. The monopoly result parallels industrial organization findings on platform markets — though here derived from first principles in a game-theoretic setting.

## Practical Applications
- **ARAD service design**: Understanding when autonomous vehicle routing platforms naturally monopolize helps regulators anticipate market structure and design policy (e.g., interoperability mandates or competition remedies).
- **Routing service competition**: Helps competing routing mediators (e.g., autonomous taxi dispatchers, ride-sharing platforms) understand equilibrium market shares and pricing.
- **Urban traffic modeling**: Informs city planners about congestion consequences when autonomous routing is mediated by competing commercial providers.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Routing mediator platform**: A neutral intermediary routing platform for autonomous vehicles that aggregates supply and demand, competing against manufacturer-owned routing services — value comes from transparency and user control.
2. **Market simulation software**: SaaS tool for cities and fleet operators to model equilibrium outcomes of competing ARAD mediators before they enter the market.
3. **Mechanism design consulting**: Advisory service helping cities design bidding/franchise rules for ARAD mediators that prevent monopoly lock-in while maintaining efficiency.
4. **Interoperability layer**: Open protocol and software layer enabling users to switch between ARAD routing mediators, undercutting natural monopolies.
5. **Regulatory analytics platform**: Tool for competition authorities to detect and quantify monopoly formation risk in emerging autonomous routing markets.

## Technical Limitations
- **Generic setting assumption**: Results apply only to "generic" congestion games; special structures (e.g., particular network topologies) might behave differently.
- **Market-share maximization**: Assumes mediators maximize market share rather than profit; in reality, mediators may trade share for margin, changing equilibrium outcomes.
- **Stylized routing model**: Real urban routing involves dynamic demand, time-varying congestion, and heterogeneous user preferences not captured in anonymous congestion games.
- **Equilibrium existence and computation**: The paper does not address whether competitive mediator equilibria always exist or how to compute them algorithmically.
- **User preferences**: Assumes user preferences over mediators are homogeneous or captured by a weak preference order; real users have multidimensional preferences (cost, wait time, safety, environmental impact).

## Open Questions
1. **Dynamics and stability**: Do no-regret learning algorithms converge to competitive mediator equilibria? Under what conditions?
2. **Equilibrium uniqueness and multiplicity**: When do competitive mediator equilibria exist? Are they typically unique or multiple?
3. **Mechanism design**: Can cities or regulators design rules (subsidies, taxes, interoperability requirements) to prevent monopoly while maintaining efficiency?
4. **Computational complexity**: What is the complexity of finding competitive mediator equilibria in realistic-sized networks?
5. **Heterogeneous users**: How do results change when users have diverse preferences over mediators beyond a single ordering?
6. **Dynamic entry**: What happens if new mediators can enter over time, or existing ones exit?

## Key Takeaway
Competing autonomous vehicle routing services naturally tend toward monopoly when one is universally preferred, raising regulatory concerns for future urban mobility markets.
