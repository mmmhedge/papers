# Grading the Graders: Verification Autonomy Levels (L0-L5) for LLM Reasoning

**Authors:** Yajie Yin
**Published:** 2026-08-19 | **arXiv:** [2608.19009v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.19009v1)

## Summary
This paper proposes Verification Autonomy Levels (VAL), a classification system for evaluating how well different verification schemes can check LLM reasoning. The key insight is that most verification systems have a fundamental blind spot: they can confirm a proposed answer is correct, but cannot guarantee no better answer exists. The paper clarifies a confused literature by showing that formal completeness is only possible for properties that can be mathematically specified—everything else (fact-checking, diagnosis) tops out at checking correctness of individual steps.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on a growing literature pairing LLMs with verifiers—systems that attempt to catch model errors. Prior work uses "level" to mean different things (granularity of checking, abstraction tier, risk ranking, system-stack layer, epistemic source). The paper also assumes familiarity with [[RLHF]] and step-by-step reasoning approaches that separate generation from checking. The core theoretical anchor is the distinction between soundness (if the verifier says something is correct, it is) and completeness (if something correct exists, the verifier will find it)—standard concepts from formal logic and theorem proving.

## Core Contributions
1. **VAL meta-standard**: A single axis classifying verifiers by source of specification and what the verdict guarantees (L0=LLM self-declaration; L1=external data anchor; L2=objective correctness; L3/L4=decidable systems with single-property or domain-level completeness; L5=impossible in unrestricted settings).
2. **The completeness blind spot**: Formal statement and empirical documentation that sampling/substitution-based verifiers cannot prove absence of better candidates.
3. **Decidability–empiricism dichotomy**: Proof that completeness requires formal specifiability; open-world empirical verification (fact-checking, diagnosis) is fundamentally capped at L2.
4. **Orthogonality result**: Resolves systematic conflation in 17 papers by showing granularity, abstraction, risk, and stack-layer are independent of VAL.
5. **Cross-domain assessment**: Evaluation across symbolic math, behavior monitoring, medical diagnosis, and code generation, including strongest formal baselines.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to [[Verification and Uncertainty]], [[Self-consistency in reasoning]], and step-by-step verification paradigms. It complements [[mechanistic-interpretability]] work (which aims to understand model internals) by focusing on external verification. The paper also engages with formal methods and theorem-proving traditions (e.g., Coq, Lean) where completeness is well-studied. It challenges [[RLHF]] and process-reward approaches by clarifying what they can and cannot guarantee. The completeness argument is related to Gödel-style incompleteness intuitions in formal systems.

## Practical Applications
1. **Medical AI audit trails**: Regulatory frameworks can now classify whether a diagnostic AI's verifier can only flag incorrect steps (L2) or guarantee all plausible diagnoses were considered (L3/L4).
2. **Code generation in safety-critical systems**: Distinguish between verifiers that check syntax/unit tests (L2) vs. formally verified program logic (L4).
3. **Financial fraud detection**: Clarify whether a fact-checker catching false claims (L2) vs. proving no false claims exist in a closed ledger (L3).
4. **Math competition grading**: Formal verification of olympiad proofs (L4) vs. step-correctness checking for homework (L2).
5. **Autonomous system monitoring**: Classify whether a behavior monitor can detect bad actions (L2) or guarantee all safe actions are recognized (L4).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **VAL Certification Service**: Audit LLM verification systems in enterprises (medical, finance, code) and issue VAL-level certificates; recurring revenue from annual re-audits.
2. **Formal Specification Marketplace**: Platform where domain experts write formally specifiable properties; LLM builders query to upgrade verifiers from L2→L3/L4; SaaS pricing per property.
3. **Compliance Dashboard**: Real-time VAL-level reporting for regulated AI deployments (healthcare, autonomous vehicles); auto-flags when verifiers degrade below contractual L-level.
4. **Hybrid Verifier Tool**: IDE plugin that automatically classifies which verification approach (sampling, formal, empirical) is appropriate for each code segment; suggests L-level upgrades.
5. **Open-World Fact-Checking API**: Service explicitly marketed as L2-only (correct individual claims, not completeness); prevents misuse; pricing by claims checked.

## Technical Limitations
1. **VAL does not rank verifiers within a level**: Two L2 systems may differ vastly in accuracy, latency, or cost; the framework is ordinal, not cardinal.
2. **Assumes clear problem specification**: Real-world tasks often have ambiguous or contested ground truth (e.g., medical diagnosis); VAL assumes this can be resolved.
3. **Formal completeness is rare**: L3/L4 is only reachable for narrow, formally specifiable domains; most practical applications are capped at L2.
4. **Does not address scalability**: A verifier might be L3 in theory but L1 in practice if formal proofs are too slow; VAL is theory-facing.
5. **Orthogonality result is descriptive, not prescriptive**: The paper clarifies that granularity, risk, and stack-layer are independent, but does not guide practitioners in choosing them jointly.
6. **Sampling bias in open-world domains**: Even L2 verifiers may miss rare failure modes if the test distribution is skewed.

## Open Questions
1. **Hybrid L-levels**: Can a single system be L2 for some properties and L4 for others? How do we compose verifiers at different levels?
2. **Approximate completeness**: Is there a measurable notion of "coverage" (e.g., 95% of valid candidates) that bridges L2 and L3?
3. **Verifier-generator co-design**: Does knowledge of the verifier's L-level change how to train the LLM? Should generators aim for L2-verifiable or L4-verifiable outputs?
4. **Human-in-the-loop VAL**: Where do human annotators / appeals processes land in the VAL taxonomy? Are they a separate axis?
5. **Dynamic VAL**: Can a verifier adaptively shift L-levels (e.g., L2 in fast mode, L3 in slow mode) or does that violate the framework's single-axis design?
6. **Cross-domain transfer**: If a verifier is L3 for math, can insights transfer to code generation or diagnosis?

## Key Takeaway
Most LLM verifiers can't prove they found the best answer—only that proposed steps are correct—and this limit is fundamental to open-world domains.
