# A Hierarchy of Entropy-Shapley Games for Multivariate Predictive Uncertainty

**Authors:** Niklas Koenen, Claudia Battistin, Jeriek Van den Abeele, Martin Jullum
**Published:** 2026-09-28 | **arXiv:** [2609.35217v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35217v1)

## Summary
This paper introduces a framework for understanding which input features drive uncertainty in machine learning models that predict multiple correlated outputs—like multi-step forecasts or joint price movements. Rather than collapsing multivariate predictions into single numbers before analysis, the authors propose a hierarchy of attribution methods that separately identify whether features affect individual output uncertainty, their dependence structure, or both.

## Theoretical Background
The work builds on [[Shapley values]] — a game-theoretic concept that fairly distributes a model's prediction among input features by averaging over all possible feature coalitions. It extends these to a probabilistic setting using [[entropy]] (a measure of uncertainty) rather than raw predictions. The key innovation is handling multivariate outputs with complex dependence structures; prior Shapley-based uncertainty attribution either reduced outputs to scalars (losing information about which components are affected) or analyzed components independently (missing how features influence relationships *between* outputs). The paper grounds this in information theory concepts like [[conditional total correlation]] — a measure of how much information is shared among multiple random variables given some context.

## Core Contributions
1. **Hierarchy of entropy-Shapley games**: Three attribution schemes ranging from per-component marginal entropy (independent analysis) → conditional entropy (accounting for known components) → joint entropy (full multivariate structure).
2. **Cross-component attribution term**: A novel quantity isolating how features shift *dependence* between output components — invisible to component-wise methods.
3. **Chain-rule decomposition**: Formal proof that joint attribution decomposes into marginal + conditional + cross-component terms, with closed-form and sample-based estimators.
4. **Empirical validation**: Demonstrates the framework distinguishes learned joint structures across distributional regression models and zero-shot time-series foundation models.

## Connections to Other Work
Extends [[Shapley-based feature attribution]] from classification/regression to uncertainty quantification. Related to [[SHAP]] (model explanation) but adapted for probabilistic outputs. Connects to [[feature importance]] in probabilistic modeling and [[uncertainty quantification]] literature. Differs from prior work (e.g., entropy-based sensitivity analysis) by explicitly decomposing marginal vs. dependence effects through a principled hierarchy. Relevant to [[causal inference]] interpretability where understanding feature effects on both means and covariances matters.

## Practical Applications
- **Risk management**: Banks use multi-asset forecasts; understanding whether forecast uncertainty comes from individual asset volatility or asset correlation shifts determines hedging strategy.
- **Weather & climate modeling**: Multi-variable forecasts (temperature, precipitation, wind); attributing uncertainty to input features helps prioritize data collection or model improvements.
- **Supply chain optimization**: Demand forecasts for multiple products with correlated demand; knowing if a feature drives shared or independent uncertainty informs inventory strategy.
- **Medical diagnostics**: Joint prediction of multiple biomarkers; distinguishing whether a test affects marginal or joint uncertainty of outcomes supports clinical decision-making.
- **Energy grid forecasting**: Wind and solar predictions are correlated; decomposing whether weather features drive individual or joint volatility informs grid stability planning.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Uncertainty Explainability SaaS**: Dashboard tool for probabilistic forecasting platforms (weather APIs, financial data providers) showing per-component vs. cross-component uncertainty drivers; sell to risk teams.
2. **Feature diagnostics for foundation models**: Audit service for zero-shot time-series models (like the one tested in the paper) that identifies which input features reliably affect joint forecast structure; market to enterprises using LLMs for forecasting.
3. **Risk-aware feature selection**: Tool for portfolio managers and energy traders to automatically select which market data feeds to ingest based on their impact on joint uncertainty of correlated predictions (asset returns, grid load, etc.).
4. **Model debugging toolkit**: Integration with MLflow/Weights & Biases for probabilistic ML teams to diagnose why learned dependence structures differ across model architectures, accelerating model selection.
5. **Regulatory compliance reporting**: Automated reports for banks/insurers showing attribution of multivariate risk forecasts to observable market factors, supporting model governance and stress-testing documentation.

## Technical Limitations
- **Computational cost**: Shapley value estimation is expensive (requires many model evaluations); the paper's sample-based estimators may require large sample sizes for high-dimensional outputs.
- **Dependence on entropy estimation**: Accurate conditional total correlation requires density estimation, which is notoriously hard in high dimensions; limited guidance on robust estimators for realistic data.
- **Ordering assumption**: The hierarchy is defined for *ordered* outputs (e.g., time steps t=1,2,3 for forecasts). Extension to unordered multivariate outputs (e.g., three independent asset prices) is unclear.
- **Model assumptions**: Assumes the probabilistic model is well-calibrated; misspecified uncertainty will propagate into misleading attributions.
- **Scalability with output dimension**: As the number of output components grows, the cross-component term becomes harder to interpret and estimate; unclear how method scales beyond ~10 outputs.

## Open Questions
- How does performance degrade with high-dimensional outputs (e.g., image generation, long time series)?
- Can the framework handle *unordered* multivariate outputs, or does it fundamentally require a structural assumption on components?
- How robust are sample-based estimators to density estimation error? Should other divergences (KL, Wasserstein) replace entropy?
- Can the framework be extended to causal settings, where you want to know if a feature affects dependence *structurally* vs. merely statistically?
- Does the cross-component term have interpretability limits beyond a certain output dimension, and if so, how should practitioners simplify it?

## Key Takeaway
Multi-output models need attribution that separates whether features drive individual output uncertainty or their dependence — this paper provides a principled hierarchy to measure both.
