# Do You Really Need to Pretrain Q-Functions for Online RL Fine-Tuning?

**Authors:** Perry Dong, Ron Polonsky, Dorsa Sadigh, Chelsea Fin
**Published:** 2026-07-29 | **arXiv:** [2607.27203v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.27203v1)

## Summary
This paper challenges the assumption that Q-functions (value estimators in reinforcement learning) should be pre-trained on offline data before fine-tuning on new tasks. The authors discover that naive Q-function pre-training often doesn't help and propose Initialization via Policy Ensemble (IPE), which bootstraps Q-function learning by collecting data from multiple diverse policies. On standard benchmarks, IPE achieves 1.26x better performance than conventional pre-training approaches.

## Theoretical Background
Value-based reinforcement learning learns Q-functions — functions that estimate the long-term reward (value) of taking an action in a given state. Pre-training and fine-tuning has become standard in RL: a policy is trained on offline data first, then adapted to a new task with online interaction. The conventional assumption is that Q-functions should also be pre-trained on this offline data to accelerate learning. This work builds on recent findings that randomly-initialized Q-functions can actually work well in online RL, questioning whether pre-training is necessary.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical finding**: Demonstrates that naive Q-function pre-training provides minimal or no benefit over random initialization when fine-tuning a pre-trained policy — contradicting conventional wisdom.

2. **Mechanistic insight**: Identifies the root cause: the Q-function learned during pre-training targets the pre-trained policy's value function, not the Q-function that online fine-tuning actually converges to. This distribution mismatch persists even with offline value maximization techniques.

3. **Method (IPE)**: Proposes Initialization via Policy Ensemble — train multiple diverse policies during pre-training, aggregate their rollouts, and use this pooled data to initialize the Q-function. This decouples Q-function initialization from any single policy's value estimates.

4. **Empirical validation**: Shows 1.26x average improvement over naive pre-training on continuous control benchmarks.

## Practical Applications
- **Robotics**: Fine-tuning pre-trained manipulation policies to new tasks without re-learning value estimates from scratch.
- **Autonomous systems**: Adapting pre-trained driving or navigation policies to new environments or conditions.
- **Game AI**: Transferring pre-trained game-playing policies to new levels or game variations.
- **Sim-to-real transfer**: Pre-training in simulation, then efficiently adapting to real robot control with minimal online data.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Transfer learning for robotics platforms**: Offer a SaaS API where robotics companies upload their pre-trained policies; IPE automatically optimizes fine-tuning on new robot variants or tasks, reducing engineering and data collection costs.

2. **RL optimization layer for autonomous systems**: Middleware that wraps existing pre-trained policies and applies IPE to dramatically reduce fine-tuning time before deployment in new domains (autonomous vehicles, drones).

3. **Data-efficient policy adaptation tool**: Commercial software for enterprises with large offline RL datasets (game companies, logistics simulators) to quickly adapt policies without maintaining separate Q-function pre-training pipelines.

4. **Benchmark-as-a-service for RL fine-tuning**: Platform that standardizes and benchmarks different Q-function initialization strategies (including IPE) for practitioners, similar to how MLPerf benchmarks model performance.

5. **Foundation model for control**: Train a large ensemble of diverse policies once, sell access to the pooled initialization data as a building block for downstream RL practitioners, reducing their pre-training compute costs.

## Technical Limitations
- **Distribution mismatch assumption**: The paper's explanation that pre-trained Q-functions target the wrong policy may not hold in all domains or when the pre-trained and fine-tuned policies are very similar.
- **Ensemble diversity**: IPE requires training multiple diverse policies during pre-training, increasing computational cost upfront. The paper doesn't fully specify how to guarantee diversity or when it breaks down.
- **Limited scope**: Evaluation is restricted to continuous control benchmarks; unclear how well results transfer to discrete action spaces, image-based observations, or very large action dimensions.
- **Comparison fairness**: Unclear whether baseline Q-function pre-training methods used state-of-the-art techniques (e.g., conservative offline RL methods like CQL or IQL) or simpler baselines.
- **Scalability**: Requires multiple policy rollouts, which could be prohibitive in high-dimensional or expensive-to-simulate environments.

## Open Questions
- How sensitive is IPE to the number of diverse policies in the ensemble? Is there a diminishing return?
- Does IPE help when the pre-trained and fine-tuned tasks are very different (true transfer), or only when they're closely related?
- Can you theoretically characterize when the "distribution mismatch" problem is severe vs. minor?
- How does IPE compare to other Q-function initialization schemes beyond naive pre-training (e.g., uncertainty-aware initialization, optimism-based methods)?
- Does IPE scale to high-dimensional observations (images, point clouds) or very large action spaces?
- Can the ensemble rollouts be reused across multiple downstream tasks, or does diversity degrade with reuse?

## Key Takeaway
Pre-training Q-functions doesn't help fine-tuning as much as expected; pooling data from diverse policies works better.
