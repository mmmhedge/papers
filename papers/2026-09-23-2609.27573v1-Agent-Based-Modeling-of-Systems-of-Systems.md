# Agent-Based Modeling of Systems of Systems

**Authors:** Jean-Baptiste Soyez, Gildas Morvan, Rochdi Merzouki, Daniel Dupont
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.27573v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27573v1)

## Summary
This paper presents a formal framework for modeling and simulating "systems of systems" — large, complex networks where many diverse interacting subsystems operate together in changing environments. The authors combine agent-based modeling with organizational and functional specifications to handle the full complexity of such systems, demonstrating their approach on autonomous vehicle logistics in ports.

## Theoretical Background
Agent-based modeling (ABM) represents systems as collections of independent agents that interact according to local rules; the system's overall behavior emerges from these interactions. This paper builds on three established conceptual foundations: the **Agent-Group-Role (AGR) model**, which organizes agents into hierarchical structures; **functional specifications**, which define goal-oriented behaviors; and the **Influence Reaction Model for Multilevel Simulation (IRM4MLS)**, a meta-model designed to handle systems with multiple levels of abstraction where actions at one level cascade through others. "Systems of systems" (SoS) are characterized by loose coupling, independent management, operational and managerial independence, and evolutionary development — think supply chains, smart cities, or autonomous vehicle networks.

## Core Contributions
The paper's main novelty is a unified formalism that integrates organizational, functional, and multilevel aspects into a single agent-based modeling framework for SoS. Specifically: (1) it formalizes how AGR models can represent the organizational structure and hierarchies within and across subsystems; (2) it adds functional specifications to guide subsystems toward global goals despite local autonomy; (3) it extends IRM4MLS to handle dynamic reorganization when goals change or subsystem capacities shift; and (4) it validates this integrated approach on a real-world port logistics case study with autonomous vehicles.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[multi-agent systems]], organizational modeling, and simulation science. It extends classical ABM by incorporating hierarchical structure (related to organizational design theory) and bridges to distributed systems and coordination problems. The focus on dynamic reorganization connects to literature on adaptive systems and self-organization. The use case resembles problems studied in [[swarm robotics]] and autonomous fleet management, though this paper emphasizes the modeling formalism rather than control algorithms.

## Practical Applications
- **Port and terminal logistics**: Automating container movement with autonomous vehicles coordinating within dynamic operational constraints.
- **Smart city systems**: Modeling traffic, energy distribution, emergency services, and public transit as interacting autonomous subsystems.
- **Supply chain networks**: Simulating resilience and reorganization when suppliers, distribution hubs, or demand patterns change.
- **Manufacturing systems**: Coordinating autonomous production lines, robots, and quality control as interdependent agents.
- **Disaster response**: Modeling interaction of emergency services, rescue units, and resource allocation during crises.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **SoS Simulation Platform**: A low-code/no-code tool for enterprises to build, test, and optimize their own systems-of-systems (supply chains, factory floors, logistics networks) before deployment; sell as SaaS with domain-specific templates.
2. **Port Optimization Service**: A specialized simulation and optimization consultancy for container ports, using this formalism to reduce congestion, automate vehicle routing, and predict bottlenecks under different scenarios.
3. **Autonomous Fleet Orchestration Middleware**: Software that takes SoS models as input and generates control code and coordination protocols for real autonomous vehicles, drones, or robots in dynamic environments.
4. **Digital Twin for Logistics Networks**: Enterprise software that creates a continuously-updated digital replica of a company's supply chain or warehouse, using this framework to simulate "what-if" scenarios and predict system failures.
5. **Government Infrastructure Planning Tool**: Government consultancy product to model critical infrastructure (water, power, transport) as systems-of-systems and test policy changes, resource reallocation, or resilience interventions.

## Technical Limitations
- **Scalability unclear**: No detailed analysis of computational complexity as the number of agents or organizational levels grows; unclear how this scales to thousands or millions of agents.
- **Validation gap**: The paper presents one case study (port logistics); generalization to other SoS domains is assumed but not empirically tested.
- **Goal specification**: The formalism assumes goals can be precisely defined and communicated; real-world systems often have conflicting, emergent, or poorly-specified objectives.
- **Dynamic reorganization overhead**: The paper acknowledges that reorganization happens but doesn't address the computational cost or optimality of reorganization algorithms.
- **Real-world grounding**: No comparison against actual system behavior; unclear how well simulated emergent properties match real port operations or other SoS domains.
- **Heterogeneity handling**: While heterogeneous subsystems are mentioned, the paper doesn't deeply address how different communication protocols, ontologies, or control philosophies interact.

## Open Questions
- How does computational cost scale with system size and reorganization frequency? Can this scale to real-world ports with hundreds of vehicles?
- What metrics validate that simulated SoS behavior matches real-world dynamics? How sensitive is the model to parameter uncertainty?
- Can the framework automatically infer organizational structures or functional specifications from observational data, or must these always be hand-specified?
- How do you balance local agent autonomy with global coordination? Are there provable guarantees about goal achievement or system stability?
- How does this integrate with actual control systems or middleware in deployed autonomous vehicles? Is there a code-generation path?
- Can the formalism handle adversarial or Byzantine subsystems, or subsystems with hidden information?

## Key Takeaway
A formal framework for simulating complex multi-subsystem networks using agents organized by roles, functions, and hierarchy levels.
