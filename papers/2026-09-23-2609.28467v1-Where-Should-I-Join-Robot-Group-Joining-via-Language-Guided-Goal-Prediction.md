# Where Should I Join? Robot Group Joining via Language-Guided Goal Prediction

**Authors:** Zilin Fang, Zishuo Wang, Gim Hee Lee, David Hsu
**Published:** 2026-09-23 | **arXiv:** [2609.28467v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28467v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles a social robotics problem: how can a robot understand which group of people it should join and where to position itself to enter naturally? The authors combine language understanding with visual perception and geometric reasoning to let robots predict both group membership and socially-appropriate joining poses, demonstrated on real robots in conversations, queues, and audiences.

## Theoretical Background
Social navigation — the problem of robots moving through human spaces while respecting social norms — typically assumes the robot already knows its destination. This work builds on that foundation but adds a semantic layer: identifying *which group* among many and *where to stand* within that group's formation. The approach draws on human proxemics (the study of spatial relationships) and formation analysis. The method uses spectral partitioning — a graph-based clustering technique that recursively divides a set of points based on connectivity — to identify candidate groups, then ranks them using a language-conditioned model that fuses vision and geometry. The goal prediction component uses energy-orientation maps, a spatial representation that scores feasible robot poses based on learned human-formation priors.

## Core Contributions
1. **Problem formulation**: Explicitly framing robot group joining as a two-stage task — grounding (which group?) and pose prediction (where to join?) — rather than assuming pre-specified goals.
2. **Language grounding for group identification**: A pipeline that generates candidate group subsets via spectral partitioning and ranks them using a language-conditioned image-geometry model, enabling robots to understand natural descriptions like "the group having a conversation about lunch."
3. **Multimodal goal prediction**: A method that combines human-formation priors with geometric feasibility to produce energy-orientation maps over poses, capturing the multimodal nature of acceptable joining positions.
4. **Real-robot validation**: Demonstrations on actual robots in static and dynamic social scenarios (conversations, queues, audiences) with varying crowd densities and visual ambiguities.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[computer-vision]] and robotics through language grounding — a direction gaining traction in embodied AI. It relates to recent trends in using language models and vision-language models for robot instruction, though here language serves to clarify *which* group rather than providing step-by-step commands. The formation prediction component connects to work on trajectory forecasting and human-robot interaction. It complements multi-agent navigation systems by adding semantic group understanding; most prior work assumes groups are pre-identified or focuses on collision avoidance rather than natural group entry. The energy-based pose ranking is conceptually similar to optimization-based social navigation, but adapted for the group-joining context.

## Practical Applications
1. **Robotic service robots**: Tour guides, museum robots, or hospitality robots that need to approach and interact with groups of visitors naturally.
2. **Autonomous mobility devices**: Wheelchairs and mobility scooters that navigate crowded indoor spaces (airports, malls, hospitals) and can identify and join groups during transit.
3. **Delivery robots**: Robots that must locate and approach groups (e.g., a family on a beach or office workers in a lounge) to deliver items without disrupting social dynamics.
4. **Interactive robots in retail**: Robots that identify customer groups and approach them at appropriate positions to offer assistance.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **"SocialBot" — an autonomous retail assistant robot**: Market a humanoid or wheeled platform optimized for group detection and natural joining; target department stores and hotels as early adopters to reduce labor costs while improving customer experience.
2. **Robotic guide dog upgrade**: License this technology to existing assistive robotics companies; the group-joining capability lets guide robots safely navigate their owners through crowds and toward human companions.
3. **Crowd navigation API for robotics**: Build a cloud service that processes camera feeds and returns group identities and suggested joining poses; sell as middleware to logistics, hospitality, and security robotics firms.
4. **VR/metaverse training platform**: Create a simulation tool where robots (digital avatars) learn to join human groups in virtual spaces; monetize via subscriptions for roboticists and spatial-UI designers.
5. **"SmartQueue" robotic assistant**: A wheeled kiosk that automatically identifies customer groups waiting in lines (restaurants, ticket counters, banks) and joins to provide information or upsell services without breaking formation.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Visual ambiguity in dense crowds**: The method assumes sufficient visual clarity to identify group boundaries; in truly crowded settings (concert venues, packed subways), spectral partitioning may fail or produce false group candidates.
2. **Language grounding scalability**: The language-conditioned model is trained on a limited domain (conversations, queues, audiences); generalization to novel group types or non-English descriptions is unclear.
3. **Real-time dynamic groups**: The paper handles "dynamically changing interactions," but it is unclear how quickly the system adapts if a group suddenly merges, splits, or rotates; latency in re-grounding could lead to awkward or inappropriate behavior.
4. **Sim-to-real gap**: Experiments on real robots appear limited in scope; robustness to variations in lighting, clothing, and robot morphologies beyond the tested platforms is unknown.
5. **Inference speed vs. accuracy trade-off**: While sub-second inference is claimed, no detailed ablation on the cost of language grounding vs. pose prediction is provided; adding richer language descriptions might slow the pipeline unacceptably.

## Open Questions
1. How does performance degrade with increasing crowd density? At what point does the spectral partitioning fail, and can more robust clustering methods (e.g., DBSCAN with adaptive parameters) help?
2. Can the language grounding generalize to zero-shot scenarios (e.g., groups performing activities never seen during training, or group types in other languages)?
3. What is the actual inference latency distribution, and how does it vary with image resolution, crowd size, and language description length?
4. How does the method handle groups that have no clear spatial structure (e.g., a loose gathering rather than a tight formation)?
5. Could reinforcement learning or online adaptation allow the robot to improve its joining pose in real-time based on human reactions (e.g., moving if someone in the group appears uncomfortable)?

## Key Takeaway
Robots can now naturally join human groups by understanding language descriptions and predicting socially appropriate positions without pre-specified goals.
