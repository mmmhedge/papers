# PriceBench: A Diagnostic Benchmark for Price, Quality, and Brand Preferences in LLM Booking Agents

**Authors:** Pavel Kireyev
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31468v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31468v1)

## Summary
This paper reveals that large language models (LLMs) acting as autonomous purchasing agents have measurable, predictable preferences for price, quality, and brand—and these preferences vary dramatically across different LLM providers and models. By analyzing 28 LLMs booking hotel rooms across 3,600 real New York City properties, the authors show that capability doesn't predict *what* an LLM prefers to buy, only *how consistently* it chooses, which has immediate consequences for pricing, consumer welfare, and manipulation risk.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on discrete choice modeling—a microeconomic framework that infers preferences from observed choices. Specifically, it applies logit choice models (a statistical method for predicting which option a decision-maker selects from multiple alternatives based on their underlying utilities or preferences). The work treats LLMs as economic agents making real purchasing decisions, extending behavioral economics beyond human decision-makers to AI systems. It also relates to work on LLM alignment and robustness, since consistent preference extraction reveals whether an LLM makes stable, understandable decisions or is vulnerable to subtle manipulations like listing order bias.

## Core Contributions
1. **PriceBench**: A diagnostic benchmark with 3,600 hotel booking tasks across 179 real NYC properties, applied to 28 LLMs, designed specifically to recover preference parameters (price, quality, brand) from choice behavior.
2. **Empirical finding on capability**: Demonstrates that LLM capability correlates with *consistency* of choice, not with the direction of preferences—capable models make stronger, more stable trade-offs, while weaker models are either rigid or nearly indifferent.
3. **Quantified heterogeneity**: Shows that price sensitivity spans more than an order of magnitude across providers, and price/quality trade-offs shift mean booking price by $146 (from $247 to $393) on identical tasks—practical stakes for commerce.
4. **Reproducible toolkit**: Releases tasks, code, and all 28 response sets, enabling future researchers to audit LLM purchasing behavior.

## Connections to Other Work
This work bridges [[large-language-models|LLM]] reliability and [[alignment]] by asking whether autonomous agents make predictable, auditable economic choices. It relates to concerns about [[agents|AI agents]] in high-stakes domains (here: commerce). It also extends mechanistic interpretability questions—instead of understanding internal activations, it asks what an LLM's *behavior* reveals about its learned preferences. The logit choice model echoes classical work in industrial organization and demand estimation, now applied to AI decision-making rather than human consumer choice.

## Practical Applications
- **E-commerce auditing**: Hotels, airlines, and travel platforms can test whether LLM-powered booking agents exhibit biased or exploitable preferences for their competitors' products.
- **Consumer protection**: Regulators can use PriceBench-style diagnostics to detect whether LLMs systematically overcharge or favor certain brands, informing AI agent certification.
- **Price discrimination risk**: Companies deploying LLMs for B2B procurement (e.g., corporate travel) can measure whether the agent pays predictably more or less than humans would, quantifying cost impact.
- **LLM provider comparison**: Enterprises can benchmark which LLM models make purchasing choices closest to their cost or quality targets before deployment.
- **Adversarial testing**: Security teams can verify that LLMs don't fall into exploitable preference patterns (e.g., always picking the first listed option due to low capability).

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Agent Auditor**: SaaS platform that auto-runs PriceBench-style diagnostics on any LLM, producing a "purchasing fingerprint" (price sensitivity, quality bias, brand preference) for procurement teams and compliance officers.
2. **Agent Preference Insurance**: InsurTech product that hedges corporate risk from LLM purchasing agents—covers unexpected cost overruns due to LLM preference drift or capability degradation.
3. **LLM Preference Benchmark API**: Hosted service offering pre-computed preference profiles for all major LLMs across hospitality, travel, and B2B procurement categories; enables instant agent selection for cost/quality targets.
4. **Travel Agent Optimizer**: AI-native travel booking tool that lets users set their own price/quality/brand trade-off, then selects the LLM model that most closely aligns with those preferences.
5. **Adversarial Testing Suite for AI Commerce**: Toolkit for enterprises to probe their deployed LLMs for preference vulnerabilities (listing order bias, price anchoring, brand loyalty) before launching to users.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Domain specificity**: Findings are grounded in hotel booking—a domain with clear, comparable attributes. Preferences may not generalize to categories with subjective quality (e.g., fashion, food) or high information asymmetry.
2. **Static snapshots**: The benchmark measures LLM preferences at one point in time per model; preferences may drift with fine-tuning, in-context learning, or system prompts, requiring re-testing.
3. **Revealed vs. true preferences**: The logit model assumes choices directly reveal underlying utilities, but LLMs may make trades for other reasons (e.g., pattern-matching training data, sensitivity to prompt formatting) not captured by the model.
4. **Limited scope of properties**: 179 NYC hotels are a narrow slice; preferences may differ for regional properties, luxury segments, or budget chains.
5. **No causal mechanism**: The paper identifies *that* preferences vary but not *why*—whether differences stem from training data, RLHF objectives, or architectural differences remains opaque.

## Open Questions
1. How do preferences evolve with model updates, fine-tuning, or prompt engineering? Is there a stable "preference fingerprint" for an LLM, or is it malleable?
2. Can we trace preference differences back to training data or objective functions (e.g., RLHF reward signals)? What causal mechanisms drive brand or price bias?
3. Do preferences generalize across domains (e.g., does price sensitivity in hotels predict price sensitivity in flights or rental cars)?
4. How exploitable are low-consistency LLMs? What specific manipulations (listing order, decoy products, framing) succeed, and can we quantify the cost to the buyer?
5. Can we design prompts or in-context examples that steer LLM preferences toward user-specified trade-offs without breaking consistency?
6. How do multi-LLM architectures (ensemble agents) combine preferences, and can they be more robust than single-model agents?

## Key Takeaway
LLMs quietly encode price and brand preferences when buying things—and these vary wildly across providers, with measurable impact on what you pay.
