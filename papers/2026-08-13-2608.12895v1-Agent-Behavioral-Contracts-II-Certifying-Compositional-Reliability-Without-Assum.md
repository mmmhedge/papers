# Agent Behavioral Contracts II: Certifying Compositional Reliability Without Assuming Independence

**Authors:** Varun Pratap Bhardwaj, Garima Singh, Arun Pratap Bhardwaj
**Published:** 2026-08-13 | **arXiv:** [2608.12895v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.12895v1)

## Summary
This paper challenges a dangerous assumption in multi-agent system reliability: that component failures are independent. The authors show that two instances of the same language model fail together on 90% of shared failures, not the 10% predicted by independence math. They develop a new certification method that doesn't assume independence away and actually works in practice.

## Theoretical Background
Compositional reliability relies on multiplying probabilities — if Agent A succeeds 90% of the time and Agent B succeeds 85% of the time, naive multiplication gives 76.5% joint success. This math only works if failures are *conditionally independent* — i.e., Agent A's failure tells you nothing about Agent B's failure. This assumption is standard in reliability engineering and system design but is rarely validated empirically.

The paper builds on classical statistical tools: bootstrap methods for coverage guarantees, linear programming for set identification without structural assumptions, and finite-sample concentration bounds (Clopper-Pearson intervals). The key insight is that fitting a dependence model post-hoc creates an "identification gap" — the distance between what you can identify from data and what is actually true — that shrinks too slowly as data grows, making fitted certificates unreliable even with more observations.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical validation of dependence**: Large-scale preregistered study (18,000 missions) proving that identical model instances fail together far more often than independence predicts (log OR = 6.66, phi = 0.916).

2. **Proof that fitting dependence models backfires**: Bootstrap bounds on fitted dependence models lose coverage as n grows because the identification gap is O(1) while the statistical haircut is only O(n^{-1/2}) — more data makes the certificate worse with no warning signal.

3. **Sound assumption-free certification method**: A linear program that bounds joint failure probabilities using only measured co-execution moments, without assuming any dependence structure. It's sharp (tight) for the information available and monotone (more moments always help).

4. **Practical moment enrichment**: Expanding from 10 to 14 moment functionals narrows the certified failure interval by 85.7%, lifting the reliability floor from 0.2455 to 0.4116.

5. **Anytime-valid certificate**: A sequential version that maintains type-I error control under optional stopping (α = 0.0471), enabling adaptive certification during deployment.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper directly critiques the classical fault-tree and reliability-block-diagram approach used in aerospace and safety-critical systems. It relates to [[RLHF]] and [[alignment]] work insofar as multi-agent deployments (e.g., committees of LLMs) are proposed as a safety mechanism, but that only works if failures are independent — which this paper disproves.

The linear-programming approach to set identification connects to causal inference (Manski bounds, partial identification) but applies it to a new domain: compositional system reliability. The anytime-validity result bridges classical sequential testing with modern adaptive certification.

The paper also implicitly critiques papers that assume conditional independence in multi-agent settings without empirical validation.

## Practical Applications
1. **LLM ensemble deployment**: Before deploying three language models to vote or pass off work, verify they don't share failure modes. This paper shows you can't assume they're independent just because they're different instances or vendors.

2. **Safety-critical multi-agent systems**: Autonomous vehicle fleets, medical diagnosis committees, or robotic swarms often assume redundancy provides independent safety margins. This work enables honest certification without that assumption.

3. **Supply chain and logistics**: Multi-agent planning systems that delegate tasks across agents (warehouses, routing, scheduling) can now certify end-to-end reliability without assuming component independence.

4. **Risk aggregation in financial systems**: If you're combining forecasts or decisions from multiple trading agents or risk models, this provides a way to certify the joint failure bound honestly.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Multi-Agent Reliability Auditor**: A SaaS tool that ingests logs from multi-agent deployments, computes co-failure statistics, and generates sound reliability certificates. Sell to enterprises deploying LLM committees or robotic systems.

2. **Dependence-aware LLM orchestration**: A framework that automatically tests pairwise agent dependence, then routes tasks to independent agents when possible, and downgrades redundancy claims when dependence is detected.

3. **Insurance underwriting for AI systems**: Underwrite liability for multi-agent AI systems using honest reliability certificates. Charge premiums based on actual co-failure dependence, not mythical independence.

4. **Regulatory compliance platform**: Help regulated industries (healthcare, finance, autonomous vehicles) generate legally defensible reliability claims for multi-agent systems without overstatement. Certifications are auditable and preregistered.

5. **Adversarial robustness testing service**: Use the moment-enrichment framework to test whether agents fail together under adversarial conditions. Sell to companies deploying LLM ensembles against adversaries (content moderation, fraud detection).

## Technical Limitations
1. **Bounded applicability of linear program**: The LP-based certificate is "often vacuous" — it may not rule out unreliable systems even when components are individually reliable. Enriching moments helps but requires domain knowledge about which moments to add.

2. **Preregistration doesn't scale**: The study required preregistration and deterministic code for scoring (no LLM judge). This is expensive and doesn't work if ground truth is subjective or expensive to compute.

3. **No guidance on moment selection**: The paper shows that enriching moments from 10 to 14 helps dramatically, but gives no principled way to choose which moments to include. This is a manual, domain-specific tuning problem.

4. **Limited to pairwise analysis**: The study focuses on two agents and handoff failures. The theory extends to many agents in principle, but the empirical validation and practical moment selection likely become harder in higher dimensions.

5. **Assumes deterministic failure**: The framework assumes failures are well-defined and reproducible. Real systems with stochasticity or adversarial inputs may have failures that are fuzzy or context-dependent.

6. **No false-negative control**: The anytime-valid certificate controls type-I error (false positives: claiming reliability you don't have). It doesn't control false negatives (claiming unreliability of actually reliable systems).

## Open Questions
1. **Optimal moment selection**: Can you automatically or efficiently select which moment functionals to measure to minimize the identified interval? Or is this always domain-specific?

2. **Scaling to many agents**: How does the moment-enrichment approach scale beyond pairs? Does the LP become intractable? Do empirical moment estimates blow up in variance?

3. **Causal structure of failure dependence**: Why are failures dependent? Do identical models share weights (yes), but does this also explain dependence across different models from the same vendor? Can you decompose shared vs. idiosyncratic failure modes?

4. **Active design to break dependence**: Can you deliberately architect agents to have *anti-correlated* failures (e.g., via diversity in training data or architecture)? How would you certify success?

5. **Comparison with Bayesian approaches**: Could a Bayesian prior on dependence structure do better than frequentist assumption-free bounds? What's the accuracy/confidence tradeoff?

6. **Heterogeneous moment information**: What if you have high-confidence estimates of some moments but low-confidence estimates of others? Can you exploit this asymmetry in the LP?

## Key Takeaway
Identical agents fail together, not independently—redundancy is over-credited exactly when you need it most.
