# Which Rollout Taught It That? BehaviorTrace and the Limits of Training-Data Attribution in Online RL

**Authors:** Amit Nautiyal
**Published:** 2026-10-07 | **arXiv:** [2610.10422v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10422v1)

## Summary
This paper investigates whether we can identify which training rollouts taught a language model a specific behavior during online reinforcement learning fine-tuning. The authors release BehaviorTrace, an evaluation framework that reveals most existing attribution methods are largely driven by confounds like gradient magnitude and model fluency rather than genuine causal signals—a sobering finding that challenges current interpretability assumptions in RL.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on training-data attribution methods, which attempt to trace model predictions back to specific training examples (e.g., TracInCP, TRAK). In the context of RL, the problem becomes harder: instead of static training data, you have dynamic rollouts generated during fine-tuning, and the "behavior" you're attributing to may emerge gradually. The work uses full-gradient sketching (a computational technique for efficient gradient analysis) and plants a known behavior with a controlled cause to test whether attribution methods can recover the true source. The core tension is between correlation (methods find statistical patterns) and causation (methods truly identify what caused the behavior).

## Core Contributions
1. **BehaviorTrace evaluation harness**: A rigorous open-source framework that combines planted behaviors, full-gradient sketching, and multiple confound controls (gradient magnitude, fluency, headroom, seed variation, generation variation).
2. **Empirical finding of confound dominance**: Demonstrates that simple baselines (e.g., ranking by gradient size alone, with no behavior target) often match or outperform targeted attribution estimators, suggesting these methods are picking up spurious signals.
3. **Fluency as a confounder**: Shows that model fluency predicts behavior labels as reliably as gradient-based methods—a crucial finding that separates genuine causal attribution from statistical artifacts.
4. **Robust signal for trigger tokens**: Identifies one attribution signal that holds across all three seeds tested: the gradient of trigger tokens (the tokens actually associated with the target behavior) aligns with meaningful signal.
5. **Practical checklist for RL attribution evaluation**: Distills findings into concrete guidance for future work.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper critiques existing gradient-based attribution methods ([[TracInCP]], [[TRAK]]), including renormalized variants like GAS. It relates to broader work on [[mechanistic-interpretability]] in language models and the challenge of understanding how [[reinforcement-learning]] changes model behavior. The planted-behavior methodology parallels causal-inference approaches in ML and connects to debates about [[alignment]] — if we can't reliably trace where behaviors come from, how can we ensure models learn intended values? The work also resonates with critiques of post-hoc explanation methods in deep learning more broadly.

## Practical Applications
1. **RL safety auditing**: Before deploying fine-tuned models, practitioners could use BehaviorTrace to verify that unwanted behaviors (or their absence) can be reliably traced to training data, helping catch deceptive or misaligned rollouts.
2. **Data quality assessment**: Identify which training rollouts genuinely contributed to desirable behaviors, enabling targeted curation or reweighting of high-quality rollouts.
3. **Model debugging**: When a model exhibits unexpected behavior after RL fine-tuning, this framework helps diagnose whether it's a real learned behavior or an artifact of training procedure (e.g., fluency bias).
4. **Interpretability research**: Provides a benchmark for evaluating new attribution methods and preventing overconfident claims about causal tracing in RL.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RL Model Debugger (B2B SaaS)**: A tool for LLM labs and AI safety teams that runs BehaviorTrace-style audits on fine-tuned models, flagging suspicious behaviors and providing attribution reports.
2. **Training-Data Provenance Platform**: Help companies track and certify which training rollouts led to each model capability, essential for regulatory compliance and safety documentation.
3. **Attribution Benchmark Suite**: Sell an extensible evaluation harness (building on BehaviorTrace) that researchers and practitioners use to validate new attribution methods before relying on them.
4. **RL Fine-Tuning Verification Service**: Offer a third-party attestation service that certifies a model's learned behaviors can be reliably attributed to intended training data, competitive advantage for safety-conscious ML shops.
5. **Adversarial Training-Data Search**: A tool that applies this framework to *reverse-engineer* which modifications to training data would be needed to teach a model a specific (desired) behavior, useful for alignment research.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Scale and compute**: Tested only on Qwen2.5-1.5B with three seeds; unclear how findings generalize to larger models or different architectures where gradient-based methods might behave differently.
2. **GRPO specificity**: Uses only GRPO (a specific online RL algorithm); other fine-tuning methods (e.g., DPO, PPO variants) may have different attribution properties.
3. **Fluency confound remains unresolved**: The paper identifies fluency as a major confounder but doesn't fully explain *why* or provide a principled way to separate fluency-driven attribution from causal attribution in all cases.
4. **Single-behavior setup**: Tests planted behaviors on a relatively small scale; real-world models learn dozens or hundreds of behaviors simultaneously, and interactions may complicate attribution.
5. **Gradient-only scope**: Only examines gradient-based methods; other attribution paradigms (e.g., causal models, intervention-based approaches) are not explored.
6. **Computational cost**: Full-gradient sketching is expensive at scale, limiting practical deployment to smaller models or representative subsets of data.

## Open Questions
1. **Does the trigger-token signal scale?** The paper identifies gradient alignment on trigger tokens as a robust signal—how well does this generalize to more complex behaviors without obvious "trigger" tokens?
2. **Can we separate fluency from causation?** What statistical or causal techniques could cleanly partition fluency effects from genuine training-data attribution?
3. **Multi-behavior interference**: When a model learns multiple behaviors simultaneously, how do confounds interact? Can we still attribute cleanly?
4. **Larger models**: Do the confound findings hold at 7B, 70B scale, or do gradient-based methods become more reliable as model capacity increases?
5. **Non-gradient methods**: Could attention-weight analysis, direct causal intervention, or mechanistic probing find more robust attribution signals than gradient sketching?
6. **Downstream implications for RLHF**: How do these attribution limitations affect our confidence in claims about what RLHF teaches models about alignment or safety?

## Key Takeaway
Most training-data attribution methods in RL are driven by confounds like gradient size and fluency; only trigger-token gradients hold up under scrutiny.
