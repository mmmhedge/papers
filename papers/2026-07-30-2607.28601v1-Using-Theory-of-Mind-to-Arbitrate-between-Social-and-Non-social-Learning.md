# Using Theory of Mind to Arbitrate between Social and Non-social Learning

**Authors:** Lance Ying, Ryan Truong, Joshua B. Tenenbaum, Samuel J. Gershman
**Published:** 2026-07-30 | **arXiv:** [2607.28601v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28601v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how people decide when to learn from others versus learn through direct experience. The researchers propose a "Rational Mentalizing" model — an AI system that reasons about what other agents know and what they'll do next, then weighs whether learning from them is worth the cost. Experiments show humans make these social-learning decisions in a way that matches this theory-of-mind-based model.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on two major foundations. First, **social learning** — the observation that humans and animals learn efficiently by watching others rather than exploring everything themselves. Second, **Theory of Mind (ToM)** — the cognitive ability to model what other agents believe, want, and will do next. Prior work has shown that humans use ToM to interpret actions and predict behavior, but this paper extends that to a decision-theoretic framework: ToM becomes a tool for *evaluating* whether learning from someone is actually useful. The model is grounded in **rational decision theory**, which assumes agents maximize expected utility — here, the trade-off between information gain (how much you learn) and cost (time, cognition, observation effort).

## Core Contributions
1. **A formal computational model** ("Rational Mentalizing") that predicts when agents will choose social over non-social learning by explicitly reasoning about another agent's goals and future informativeness.
2. **Quantitative human behavioral validation**: a novel experimental paradigm (a two-player game where participants choose to observe others or explore) that directly tests the model's predictions against human choices.
3. **Evidence that selective social learning is goal-directed**: humans don't just copy others blindly; they use implicit models of others' minds to decide whether observing them is worth it.

## Connections to Other Work
This relates to broader work on [[reinforcement-learning]] and [[multi-agent-systems]] — how agents cooperate and learn from each other. It also connects to mechanistic studies of ToM in cognitive science and recent AI work on *recursive modeling* (agent A models agent B, who models agent A, etc.). Unlike pure imitation learning or multi-agent reinforcement learning, this framework explicitly *costs* the act of social learning, creating a rational arbitration problem. The utility-maximization framing echoes [[RLHF]] and preference-based learning, though applied to a simpler, more transparent setting.

## Practical Applications
1. **Recommendation systems that know when to ask**: systems that decide when user behavior is informative enough to influence others versus when direct exploration is better.
2. **Autonomous agent teams**: robots or drones that choose whether to copy a teammate's strategy or try their own approach based on mental models of teammates' expertise.
3. **Educational technology**: systems that decide when a student should learn from peers (peer tutoring, collaborative learning) versus independent practice.
4. **Organizational workflows**: algorithms that route workers' attention — when to observe what colleagues are doing versus doing their own work.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Assumes rational agents**: the model presumes people (and other agents) have well-defined goals and behave roughly optimally. Real humans are bounded-rational, subject to biases, and often opaque in their true goals.
2. **Simple experimental domain**: the game used for validation is relatively constrained. Scaling to richer, more ambiguous real-world settings (where agents' true goals are hard to infer) is unclear.
3. **Computational cost not explicit**: the model itself requires reasoning about another agent's policy, which can be computationally expensive. How people approximate this tractably is not addressed.
4. **Limited to dyadic interactions**: unclear how the framework extends to many agents or competitive settings where some agents might deliberately mislead.
5. **No learning dynamics**: the model treats goal and policy as fixed; real agents learn and update their goals over time.

## Open Questions
1. How do people *infer* the goal of someone they're observing? The model assumes goals are known; in practice, goal inference is hard and error-prone.
2. Can this framework explain social learning in more naturalistic, open-ended environments (not just structured games)?
3. How do people update their mental models of others when those models are wrong?
4. Does the same arbitration principle apply when social learning carries *social costs* (e.g., asking for help signals weakness, or violates group norms)?
5. Can this model be extended to *teaching*: when should an informed agent invest in explaining things to others?

## Key Takeaway
Humans choose whether to learn from others by reasoning about their minds — implicitly calculating whether observing them is worth the cost.
