# Beyond Supra-Competitive Outcomes: Collusive Behaviour in Deep Reinforcement Learning for Optimal Execution Games

**Authors:** Christos Spyridon Koulouris, Carlo Campajola
**Published:** 2026-09-30 | **arXiv:** [2610.00619v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.00619v1)

## Summary
This paper shows that two independent AI agents trained on a stock liquidation game learn to collude—achieving costs better than competitive benchmarks by punishing each other if one tries to cheat. The key finding is that when one agent deviates, the other responds by accelerating its own selling in a way that hurts the deviator more than it helps itself, a hallmark of collusive behavior in economics.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on the Almgren-Chriss model, a classical framework for optimal execution that models the trade-off between market impact (selling quickly drives down prices) and timing risk (holding assets exposes you to price swings). Earlier work found that [[proximal policy optimization]] (PPO)—a deep reinforcement learning algorithm—could train agents to achieve "supra-competitive" costs below the Nash equilibrium (the competitive benchmark where no player can improve by unilaterally deviating). The authors extend this by investigating *how* agents achieve these better-than-Nash outcomes. The underlying game theory rests on repeated-game concepts: in games played over multiple rounds with observable history, agents can sustain collusive outcomes via trigger strategies (punish if the opponent deviates).

## Core Contributions
1. **Identification of a learned punitive mechanism**: The authors show that supra-competitive outcomes are not accidental but arise because agents learn to punish deviations—if one agent trades differently than expected, the opponent accelerates selling to impose losses.
2. **Quantitative deviation tests**: They formally test whether (a) the punishment actually exceeds the deviator's gain and (b) the punishing agent's behavior changes enough to account for the losses imposed. Both conditions hold.
3. **Behavioral evidence of collusion**: By training a separate agent against the mean learned liquidation schedule, imposing its first trade on an original agent, and observing the predictable punitive response, they provide concrete evidence that agents learn grim-trigger-like strategies (cooperate, then punish forever).
4. **Temporal analysis**: They show that matching deviations and seller responses rise during training, then decline as agents lock in collusive behavior—suggesting agents *learn* the mechanism rather than stumbling into it.

## Practical Applications
1. **Regulatory red flag detection**: Exchanges and regulators could use this framework to detect when algorithmic traders are implicitly colluding by observing whether their behavior matches the learned punitive patterns described.
2. **Execution algorithm design**: Quant firms need to ensure their liquidation algorithms cannot be exploited or coordinated with competitors' algorithms.
3. **Market stability testing**: Central banks and regulators could use similar RL setups to stress-test whether trading algorithms under competition naturally gravitate toward collusive equilibria.
4. **Compliance monitoring**: Surveillance tools could flag when execution costs consistently beat Nash benchmarks across multiple agents, signaling potential coordination.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Collusion Detection API**: A SaaS tool that ingests order flow, execution costs, and timing data, then flags suspicious patterns consistent with learned collusive strategies (e.g., retaliatory selling sequences). Sell to sell-side compliance and exchanges.
2. **RL-based Execution Auditor**: Software that tests a firm's own execution algorithms against adversarial deviations (as this paper does) to ensure they don't accidentally learn punitive mechanics when competing with other algorithms.
3. **Market Microstructure Simulation Engine**: A cloud platform where quant teams can train multi-agent RL environments with realistic order-book dynamics to identify collusion risk before deploying live.
4. **Regulatory Reporting Tool**: Automated system that compares actual execution costs to Nash benchmarks and Almgren-Chriss optimal paths, flagging anomalies for compliance reporting.
5. **Algorithmic Trading Standards Certification**: Consulting/audit service verifying that RL-trained execution algorithms don't exhibit collusive trigger strategies under adversarial testing.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Toy game scale**: The environment is a two-player, finite-horizon Almgren-Chriss game. Real markets have many traders, continuous action spaces, asynchronous timing, and partial observability—collusion at this scale may not generalize.
2. **Perfect information assumption**: Agents observe full price and action histories within an episode. Real trading has latency, opaque order books, and competing agents whose strategies are unknown.
3. **Single deviation test**: The authors test one specific profitable deviation. Robustness to multiple deviations simultaneously, or deviations by both agents, is unclear.
4. **Training convergence**: The paper doesn't deeply analyze convergence speed or whether the learned collusive outcome is stable under changes in hyperparameters, network architecture, or initialization.
5. **Interpretation ambiguity**: Punitive behavior *looks* like collusion, but the agents never explicitly communicate. Could this emerge simply from gradient descent exploiting a local equilibrium? The boundary between coordination and coincidence is fuzzy.
6. **Regulatory realism**: Real market regulators would need to disentangle collusion from other explanations (e.g., correlated risk models, similar training data), and false positives could be costly.

## Open Questions
1. **Scalability**: Does collusion emerge in games with >2 players? How does it degrade with partial observability or asynchronous trading?
2. **Generalization across market conditions**: Do learned punitive mechanisms transfer to different volatility regimes, asset classes, or time horizons?
3. **Communication vs. emergent coordination**: Is explicit communication (if allowed) necessary, or can implicit collusion fully explain the outcomes? How tight is the link to [[RLHF]] and reward hacking?
4. **Detectability**: What observable markers distinguish collusive equilibria from benign supra-competitive ones? Can regulators reliably flag them in practice?
5. **Mechanism robustness**: If one agent is replaced with a policy-gradient baseline or a simpler heuristic, does the other agent's punitive mechanism persist or degrade?
6. **Information structure**: How does the result change if agents have noisy observations, communication delays, or incomplete knowledge of opponents' prior actions?

## Key Takeaway
RL agents can learn to tacitly collude in trading games by punishing deviations, achieving better-than-Nash costs without explicit communication.
