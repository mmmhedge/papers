# Towards Mitigating Fabricated Consensus: The Active Provenance Gate for Multi-Agent Debate Synthesis

**Authors:** Jakub Masłowski, Jarosław A. Chudziak
**Published:** 2026-09-25 | **arXiv:** [2609.31422v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31422v1)

## Summary
This paper addresses a critical safety problem in multi-agent debate (MAD) systems: summarization models that fabricate plausible-sounding consensus unsupported by the actual debate logs. The authors introduce the Active Provenance Gate (APG), a verification layer that audits claims against debate history and blocks unsupported statements before publication. In human studies, users strongly preferred explicit reports of disagreement over fluent fabricated consensus, even though they found the fabrication more readable.

## Theoretical Background
Multi-agent debate systems use multiple language models to explore different perspectives on a problem, then synthesize their outputs into a final decision or summary. This approach builds on the insight that diverse viewpoints can improve reasoning quality. However, the synthesis phase — where a model reads all debate logs and produces a coherent summary — creates a dangerous capability gap: LLMs are biased toward generating fluent text even when the source material doesn't support their claims. This relates to hallucination problems in language models and the broader challenge of ensuring generated text remains faithful to its sources (a problem tackled by retrieval-augmented generation and citation mechanisms). The paper treats this as a grounding and provenance-tracing problem: ensuring that every claim in a summary can be traced back to specific debate statements.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical demonstration** that standard LLM-based debate synthesis fabricates consensus — a concrete characterization of the failure mode in multi-agent systems.
2. **Active Provenance Gate architecture**: a post-debate verification layer that moves from passive logging to active blocking. APG audits each claim, applies self-correction, and explicitly generates divergence reports when consensus cannot be reliably established.
3. **Human evaluation** showing that users prefer transparent failure signals (explicit disagreement statements) over fluent fabricated consensus, even when they subjectively rate the fabrication as more readable.
4. **Quantitative metric (Provenance Fidelity)** that measures the grounding of synthesis claims in source debates; demonstrated 2× improvement in difficult scenarios with self-correction enabled.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper sits at the intersection of several research threads:
- **Hallucination mitigation** in LLMs (related to [[RLHF]], [[Constitutional AI]], and fact-checking frameworks)
- **Multi-agent reasoning systems** and their robustness (related to [[Chain of Thought]] variants and collaborative reasoning)
- **Alignment and truthfulness**: the fabricated consensus problem is fundamentally an alignment issue — the model optimizes for fluency at the expense of faithfulness
- **Provenance and attribution**: connects to work on citation generation and source-grounded text generation
- **Safety in distributed decision pipelines**: relevant to work on AI governance and verification in high-stakes systems

The APG approach is novel in treating provenance verification as a hard constraint gate rather than a soft scoring function.

## Practical Applications
1. **Crisis management and emergency response systems**: Multi-agent debate over response strategies, with APG ensuring recommendations are grounded in actual evidence and threat assessments rather than smoothly fabricated consensus.
2. **Medical decision support**: Synthesis of multiple diagnostic perspectives (different specialist agents) with explicit blocking of unsupported clinical recommendations.
3. **Legal and compliance document generation**: Synthesis of case analysis or regulatory interpretation where each claim must be traceable to source law or precedent.
4. **Enterprise governance dashboards**: Multi-stakeholder consensus-building on resource allocation or policy decisions, where transparent disagreement is preferable to false consensus.
5. **News and fact-checking pipelines**: Aggregation of multiple sources into a report that explicitly signals where sources diverge rather than smoothing contradictions.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Verification Layer SaaS for LLM Pipelines**: A middleware service that integrates APG into existing LLM applications (customer support, research synthesis, report generation) to certify claims against source documents before output.
2. **Consensus Auditor for Enterprise Governance**: Software that runs multi-agent debate on organizational decisions and produces trustworthy synthesis with explicit dissent flagging for board-level or legal review.
3. **Crisis Decision Support System**: Real-time synthesis of multi-agent analysis during disasters/incidents, guaranteed provenance-checked with transparent uncertainty signals to decision-makers.
4. **Regulated Content Generation Platform**: A no-code tool for compliance-heavy industries (finance, pharma, legal) to generate reports and summaries with automatic provenance verification built in.
5. **Academic and Research Paper Assistant**: Synthesizes findings from multiple research agents into structured literature reviews with explicit flagging of contradictions and unsupported claims, certified for academic integrity.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Computational overhead**: APG requires re-reading debate logs and auditing every claim in the synthesis — significant latency for large debates. Scalability to complex, long-running multi-agent discussions is unclear.
2. **Scope of "supported"**: The paper doesn't fully address edge cases where claims are *implications* of debate content rather than direct statements. Where is the line between inference and fabrication?
3. **Self-correction loop limits**: The paper shows 2× improvement with self-correction, but doesn't characterize when iterative correction plateaus or fails.
4. **Domain dependence**: Human preference for explicit disagreement may be context-dependent (crisis simulations may differ from, e.g., customer service contexts). Generalization across domains is untested.
5. **Adversarial pressure**: If users or downstream systems explicitly reward fluent synthesis, APG's conservative blocking may be circumvented or disabled.
6. **Debate quality assumption**: APG assumes the debate logs themselves are coherent and high-quality. Garbage-in, garbage-out remains a risk.

## Open Questions
1. **How does APG perform on multi-step reasoning?** If claim B depends on claim A, and A is unsupported, does APG block both or create partial syntheses?
2. **What is the cost of false negatives?** How often does APG block *valid* inferences or claims that are true but not explicitly stated?
3. **Can APG scale to real-time debate systems?** The paper tests on crisis simulations; how does it perform in production systems with hundreds of agents or continuous debate streams?
4. **How should APG weight different sources of disagreement?** When agents diverge, is that treated identically to unsupported claims?
5. **What training or fine-tuning improves APG performance?** Is this a task that requires instruction-tuning, or does it work with off-the-shelf models?
6. **How do users interact with divergence reports in practice?** The human study was small and controlled; real-world deployment may show different behaviors.

## Key Takeaway
Active verification gates can prevent language model systems from fabricating false consensus in multi-agent debates, and users prefer transparent disagreement over fluent falsehood.
