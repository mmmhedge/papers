# Propose, Don't Judge: An Anytime-Valid Referee for LLM Agents That Mine Investment Factors

**Authors:** Bo Qu, Mingguang Chen, Licheng Wang
**Published:** 2026-09-22 | **arXiv:** [2609.27051v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.27051v1)

## Summary
This paper studies how to safely deploy LLM agents in quantitative finance factor research—the process of discovering and validating trading signals. The key idea: let the agent *propose* investment factors, but have a frozen, independent "referee" *judge* them using only future market outcomes. This approach guarantees statistical validity (no false discoveries) at any stopping time, while empirically showing LLMs outperform simpler baselines at factor generation, though with a cost in validation speed.

## Theoretical Background
Quantitative factor research is the hunt for patterns in market data that predict future returns. Traditionally, researchers propose factors, backtest them on historical data, select the winners, and deploy them. The problem: cherry-picking and overfitting create false positives—factors that look good in hindsight but fail in live trading.

This paper builds on two foundations. First, **false-discovery control** from statistics—methods that guarantee a bound on spurious discoveries even when you stop testing at any moment (anytime-valid inference). Second, **agent-based automation**—using LLMs to replace parts of the research pipeline. The referee mechanism is rooted in sequential hypothesis testing and betting-based calibration, where a factor's credibility is measured by how much cumulative market loss you'd suffer betting on it using pre-committed stakes.

## Core Contributions
1. **Frozen-referee design**: A statistical procedure that acts as an independent gatekeeper, scoring proposals only on future market data (never historical backtests), with formal false-discovery guarantees that hold whenever you stop.

2. **Empirical decomposition**: Clear evidence that *who proposes* and *who judges* drive different outcomes—judging should be procedural (statistical), proposing should be agentic (LLM), and instrument design (writing diagnostic tests) should remain agent-driven.

3. **Walk-forward validation**: Tested on real CSI 500 data over 10 years, showing the frozen referee admits 5–11× fewer false factors than "leaky" alternatives, though true factors take ~500 trading days to certify.

4. **LLM capability beyond generation**: The LLM agent's ability to *author diagnostic probes* (custom tests to understand factors) outstrips bandit algorithms, which can only select from predefined factor families.

## Connections to Other Work
This paper sits at the intersection of [[alignment]] (controlling agent behavior via external oversight) and [[quantitative-finance]] automation. It echoes concerns in [[RLHF]] and broader AI safety about delegating decisions to LLMs—here solved via separation of concerns rather than retraining.

The "frozen referee" pattern resembles constitutional AI and external auditing paradigms: the agent has autonomy within guardrails set by an immutable judge.

## Practical Applications
1. **Hedge fund research automation**: Deploy LLM agents to discover factors 24/7, with statistical guarantees that submitted factors won't hurt capital allocation.

2. **Regulatory compliance**: A deterministic, auditable gatekeeper for factor discovery appeals to compliance teams; the 5–11× reduction in false admissions reduces regulatory and reputational risk.

3. **Multi-manager platforms**: Institutional investors can deploy this as a layer between fund managers' proposals and portfolio inclusion, providing objective vetting.

4. **Retail factor marketplaces**: Platforms allowing quant researchers to submit factors; the referee ensures quality and protects users from overfitted strategies.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Factor Vault**: A SaaS platform where quants submit factor ideas via API; the platform's frozen referee validates and ranks them in real time, charging subscription + performance fees on certified factors.

2. **LLM-Powered Quant Lab**: Managed service where a customer's proprietary LLM agent (tuned to their domain) mines factors continuously; the vendor supplies the referee and handles compute, scaling from single PMs to multi-hundred-person teams.

3. **Insurance for Factor Hedges**: A product that bundles the certified factor strategy with option hedges, allowing PMs to get *immediate* capital access (not 500 days) while the referee validates in the background.

4. **Regulatory Audit Tool**: Package the frozen-referee mechanism for compliance teams at large asset managers, automating proof that no p-hacking occurred in approved factors.

5. **Backtesting-as-a-Service 2.0**: Traditional backtesting is optimistic; offer forward-testing via the referee, charging based on validation speed vs. confidence guarantees.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Validation latency**: True factors take ~500 trading days to certify, making the system unsuitable for high-frequency or trend-chasing strategies. The ungated portfolio has meaningfully better Sharpe ratio.

2. **Scale of false negatives**: The paper doesn't quantify how many *true* factors are rejected—only false admissions. High false-negative rates could waste opportunity.

3. **Frozen referee brittleness**: If market regimes shift dramatically (e.g., COVID crash), the referee's calibration may break. The paper uses 10 years of CSI 500 (relatively stable); global stress scenarios untested.

4. **Proposal-judge asymmetry**: The agent can evolve and adapt; the referee cannot. If market microstructure changes, the referee's betting-based scoring may become stale.

5. **Cost not fully priced**: The paper mentions validation time as a cost but doesn't quantify opportunity cost of capital tied up during the 500-day wait, or compute cost of LLM proposals.

## Open Questions
1. How does the referee perform during market crises or regime switches? Does it need periodic retraining, and if so, can formal guarantees survive?

2. Can the referee be *partially* unfrozen—e.g., allow parameter tweaks based on new market data without sacrificing false-discovery guarantees?

3. What is the false-negative rate? How many true factors does the referee reject, and how does this trade-off compare to classical backtesting?

4. How much does the LLM's factor proposal quality depend on prompt engineering, fine-tuning, or domain-specific knowledge injection? Can a generic LLM compete, or is this specific to finance-trained models?

5. Can you combine multiple referees (e.g., different betting mechanisms, different markets) to speed certification while maintaining guarantees?

## Key Takeaway
Freeze the judge, free the proposer: LLM agents excel at generating factors; statistical referees ensure none are false.
