# Photonic Quantum Computing vs. Classical Solvers in Constrained Factor Portfolio Optimization

**Authors:** Nirvik Sahoo, Chyng Wen Tee, Paul Robert Griffin
**Published:** 2026-08-14 | **arXiv:** [2608.14134v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.14134v1)

## Summary
This paper compares three optimization approaches for building diversified stock portfolios: photonic quantum annealers, classical mixed-integer programming solvers, and deep reinforcement learning agents. The key finding is that while quantum hardware can find mathematically superior portfolio allocations in specific scenarios, classical solvers remain more reliable for real-world constraints like controlling tail risk, and reinforcement learning agents struggle when optimizing for higher-order statistics like skewness.

## Theoretical Background
Portfolio optimization seeks to allocate capital across assets to maximize expected returns while managing risk — typically measured by volatility. The classical framework uses mean-variance optimization, but modern institutional mandates add constraints on tail risk (skewness, kurtosis) and factor exposure. Photonic quantum annealers use quantum physics principles to search solution spaces faster than classical computers for certain NP-hard problems. Mixed-integer programming (MIP) solvers like Gurobi use branch-and-bound algorithms to find provably optimal solutions to discrete optimization problems. Deep reinforcement learning agents learn policies through trial-and-error; SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) is a state-of-the-art off-policy algorithm that balances exploration and exploitation. The Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen library provides 13 systematic factors (momentum, value, quality, etc.) used to explain equity returns.

## Core Contributions
1. **First rigorous empirical comparison** of photonic quantum annealers, MIP solvers, and deep RL on real institutional portfolio optimization, using a 164-month live test window rather than synthetic benchmarks.
2. **Identifies structural failure modes**: quantum annealers excel in narrow hyperparameter ranges but become unstable outside them; RL agents systematically fail when optimizing higher moments (skewness) without explicit anchoring.
3. **Demonstrates MIP superiority for constrained mandates**: classical solvers achieve tighter tail-risk control and cross-seed stability (reproducibility), critical for institutional deployment.
4. **Translates empirical results into mandate-specific deployment guidelines**: provides quantitative thresholds for when each solver should be used in practice.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of three research streams:
- **Quantum finance**: extends [[QAOA]] and quantum annealing research from toy problems to practical portfolio problems at realistic scale.
- **Advanced optimization in quantitative finance**: relates to ongoing work on [[algorithmic-trading]] and [[risk-management]] optimization, particularly tail-risk constraints.
- **RL in finance**: connects to literature on [[reinforcement-learning]] for portfolio management (e.g., Markowitz-constrained agents), while documenting failure modes not previously studied at this scale.
- The paper implicitly critiques hype around quantum advantage — showing that problem hardness alone doesn't guarantee superior real-world performance when constraints and stability matter.

## Practical Applications
1. **Institutional asset managers** deciding which optimization engine to deploy for factor tilting strategies; the paper provides explicit decision trees (e.g., "use quantum for unconstrained alpha-seeking mandates, MIP for liability-matching").
2. **Risk management teams** validating that tail-risk constraints (Value-at-Risk, Expected Shortfall) are reliably enforced — MIP's reproducibility is critical for regulatory compliance.
3. **Quantitative fund startups** benchmarking whether investing in quantum hardware access (expensive, limited availability) is justified versus upgrading classical solver licenses.
4. **Hedge funds pursuing higher-moment optimization** (skewness targeting) now have evidence that naive RL will fail; they need explicit regularization or hybrid approaches.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Hardware availability**: photonic quantum annealers (Dirac-3) remain extremely limited access and expensive; results may not generalize to future hardware improvements or other quantum platforms (superconducting, trapped-ion).
2. **Factor library specificity**: tested only on 13 factors from Jensen-Kelly-Pedersen; results may not transfer to factor libraries with different dimensionality, correlation structure, or regime dynamics.
3. **Hyperparameter sweep incompleteness**: 48 configurations tested, but optimization spaces are high-dimensional; optimal configurations for each solver may exist outside this sweep.
4. **RL evaluation methodology**: SAC trained with unspecified reward shaping; different RL algorithms (PPO, A3C) or reward engineering could perform differently — not a general indictment of RL.
5. **Live vs. backtested risk**: paper uses historical backtesting; real-world transaction costs, market impact, and portfolio rebalancing frequency not modeled.
6. **Stability metrics vague**: "cross-seed stability" not formally defined; different random seeds may produce allocations with different tail-risk profiles that are harder to detect with standard metrics.

## Open Questions
1. **Does quantum advantage improve with problem scale?** How do quantum annealers perform on much larger factor libraries (500+ factors) or when adding constraints like sector limits, position limits, etc.?
2. **Can RL be fixed for higher-moment optimization?** What regularization, reward shaping, or architectures would allow RL agents to reliably optimize skewness without failure?
3. **How do these solvers perform in regime shifts?** All testing on historical data — what happens during regime changes (e.g., 2008 crisis, COVID, interest rate shocks)?
4. **What is the hardware roadmap for practical advantage?** As photonic quantum improves, at what problem size/complexity does quantum reliably beat MIP on wall-clock time and solution quality simultaneously?
5. **Hybrid approaches?** Can combining quantum (alpha generation) + classical (constraint satisfaction) outperform either alone?

## Key Takeaway
Classical solvers outperform quantum and RL for real portfolio optimization, but quantum excels in narrow operating ranges — choose based on your constraints, not hype.
