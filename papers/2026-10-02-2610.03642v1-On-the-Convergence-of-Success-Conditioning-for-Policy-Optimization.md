# On the Convergence of Success Conditioning for Policy Optimization

**Authors:** Matthew Brun, Xu Andy Sun
**Published:** 2026-10-02 | **arXiv:** [2610.03642v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03642v1)

## Summary
This paper analyzes success conditioning—a simple technique where policies are updated by increasing the probability of actions that led to successful outcomes—and proves it converges to optimal behavior in Markov decision processes. The work fills a theoretical gap: while success conditioning is widely used in practice, its convergence guarantees and rates were unknown. The authors show convergence is possible and characterize how many iterations are needed depending on the problem structure.

## Theoretical Background
The paper builds on classical reinforcement learning theory, which studies how agents learn optimal decision policies in stochastic environments. Markov decision processes (MDPs) are the standard mathematical model: they describe systems where the next state depends only on the current state and action, not history. Success conditioning itself is conceptually simple—it's a form of policy gradient or importance-weighted update—but sits at the intersection of reinforcement learning and stochastic optimization. The convergence analysis draws on techniques from mathematical optimization, proving when iterative algorithms reach optimal solutions and how fast they get there. Prior work on policy optimization methods (like policy gradient algorithms and actor-critic approaches) provides context, though those typically use different update rules or stronger assumptions.

## Core Contributions
1. **Convergence guarantee**: Proves that success conditioning converges to an optimal policy on a broad class of MDPs, removing prior uncertainty about whether the method actually works in theory.
2. **Convergence rate bounds**: Derives explicit rates—$\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^p)$ iterations for discounted MDPs (where $p$ is problem-dependent) and $\mathcal{O}(\log(1/\varepsilon))$ for single-period MDPs—giving concrete iteration complexity for different settings.
3. **Problem-dependent exponent**: Shows the convergence exponent $p$ depends on problem structure, not a universal constant, which is more nuanced than simpler analyses.

## Connections to Other Work
This work relates to the broader landscape of [[Reinforcement Learning]] algorithms. Success conditioning is a variant of policy gradient methods and shares conceptual ground with [[RLHF]] (reward-weighted maximum likelihood)—both boost probability of good outcomes. It also connects to importance-weighted and offline RL techniques. The convergence analysis uses machinery similar to analyses of vanilla policy gradient and natural policy gradient, though those methods use different update rules. The paper complements recent work on simplifying RL algorithms by showing even straightforward heuristics can have solid theoretical foundations. This contrasts with methods requiring trust regions or other constraints to ensure convergence.

## Practical Applications
1. **Imitation learning from demonstrations**: Increase probability of actions matching expert behavior in recorded trajectories; useful for robotics and autonomous systems.
2. **Offline RL and batch learning**: When only logged data is available (no live interaction), success conditioning can extract policies without importance sampling corrections.
3. **Safe exploration**: Emphasize actions that avoid failure modes in safety-critical domains (healthcare, autonomous driving) by treating "no crash" as success.
4. **Reward learning**: When reward signals are sparse or delayed, condition on eventual success to bootstrap learning early.
5. **Multi-task learning**: Share success conditioning across related tasks to improve sample efficiency.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **Offline policy optimization platform**: SaaS tool for enterprises to extract decision policies from logs (customer support actions, supply chain decisions) without real-time interaction, powered by success conditioning.
2. **Safe robot learning framework**: Robotics SDK that trains manipulation and navigation policies by conditioning on successful trajectories, with convergence guarantees for regulated industries.
3. **Autonomous systems simulator + optimizer**: Cloud service that takes recorded driving/flight logs, applies success conditioning with proven convergence rates, and outputs validated policies for deployment.
4. **Healthcare decision support**: Train diagnostic or treatment recommendation systems from historical patient records by conditioning on good outcomes (cure, no adverse events).
5. **Personalized learning agent**: Adaptive tutoring system that increases probability of pedagogical choices that led to student mastery, with theoretical guarantees on reaching optimal teaching policies.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Assumption of optimality clarity**: The method requires knowing which outcomes count as "success"—in real domains, defining success is itself a hard problem (credit assignment, delayed rewards, false positives).
2. **State space requirements**: Convergence rates depend on problem data (e.g., mixing time, reward structure); adversarial or ill-conditioned problems could have prohibitively slow $p$ in $\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon^p)$.
3. **Exploration-exploitation tradeoff**: The paper focuses on convergence to optimality but doesn't address how to balance exploring uncertain actions versus exploiting known successes—critical in practice.
4. **Scalability to large state spaces**: Analysis assumes finite or well-behaved state spaces; scaling to continuous, high-dimensional, or partially observable settings is unclear.
5. **Model-free assumption**: No explicit modeling of transition dynamics; this simplicity may slow convergence compared to model-based methods with structure.
6. **Batch vs. online**: Not clear how the rates change in true online (streaming) settings versus batch offline learning.

## Open Questions
1. **Tighter rates**: Can the problem-dependent exponent $p$ be characterized more explicitly in terms of interpretable problem properties (e.g., reward variance, state mixing)?
2. **Exploration strategies**: What active exploration strategies (e.g., optimism, curiosity) can be combined with success conditioning while maintaining convergence guarantees?
3. **Partial observability**: How does success conditioning behave in partially observable MDPs (POMDPs) where the agent doesn't see the full state?
4. **Stochastic approximation**: How do convergence rates change when success conditioning is implemented with function approximation (neural networks)?
5. **Comparison to baselines**: Empirical comparisons—how do convergence rates compare to policy gradient, actor-critic, or Q-learning on standard benchmarks?
6. **Multi-agent extension**: Does success conditioning extend to multi-agent settings, and do convergence guarantees hold?

## Key Takeaway
Success conditioning converges to optimal policies with proven rates; how fast depends on the problem structure.
