# Physical Muon: Orthogonalization as an Equilibrium Computation

**Authors:** Yuren Hao
**Published:** 2026-09-29 | **arXiv:** [2609.37525v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.37525v1)

## Summary
This paper redesigns Muon, an optimizer for neural network training, to work on analog hardware by replacing dense matrix operations with a continuous-time flow that can be approximated using only matrix-vector products and local updates. The key insight is that orthogonalization — a core step in Muon — can be computed as the equilibrium state of a dynamical system rather than through explicit matrix inversion, making it feasible for physical neural networks and in-memory computing devices.

## Theoretical Background
Neural network training on analog hardware promises massive energy savings, but existing optimizers don't map well to physical constraints. [[SGD]] (stochastic gradient descent) works locally but converges slowly on modern architectures like transformers. The [[Adam]] optimizer family converges faster but is sensitive to analog noise and bias. Muon is a recent optimizer that uses a Newton–Schulz iteration to compute orthogonalization of gradient matrices — this improves training stability and performance, but the Newton–Schulz method requires dense matrix-matrix multiplication, which is expensive to implement in analog circuits. The paper's key theoretical move is recognizing that orthogonalization can be framed as finding the fixed point of a continuous-time dynamical system (a flow), which can be approximated using only operations that map naturally to analog hardware.

## Core Contributions
1. **Physical Muon optimizer**: Reformulates the Newton–Schulz orthogonalization step as an equilibrium computation of a continuous-time flow, avoiding dense matrix products.
2. **Probe-based approximation**: Uses random probes (matrix-vector products, reciprocal reads, local rank-1 writes) to approximate the flow dynamics — all operations that analog hardware can implement efficiently.
3. **Empirical validation on transformers**: Demonstrates on a 10.95M-parameter transformer that the flow-based approach loses only ~0.0085 validation cross-entropy compared to the original Muon, and the probe implementation adds a further ~0.0188 overhead.
4. **Circuit-level simulation**: Shows that analog circuit models can reproduce the dynamics and achieve comparable training performance.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of three domains: optimizer design (related to [[Adam]], [[SGD]], and other first/second-order methods), analog computing (physical neural networks), and equilibrium-based computation (dynamic systems perspectives on optimization). It extends Muon by making it hardware-aware, similar to how recent work adapts optimizers for specific accelerators (TPUs, GPUs). The flow-based formulation relates to literature on implicit models and neural ODEs, where computation is viewed as solving a differential equation.

## Practical Applications
1. **Ultra-low-power edge AI**: Training neural networks on specialized analog chips embedded in IoT devices or mobile hardware, reducing energy consumption by orders of magnitude.
2. **In-memory computing systems**: Leveraging memristor or crossbar-based analog computing arrays where data and computation collocate, eliminating data movement overhead.
3. **Neuromorphic processors**: Bringing efficient training to neuromorphic chips designed for spiking neural networks or continuous-time analog dynamics.
4. **Autonomous systems**: Real-time on-device learning for robotics or drones where battery life and latency are critical.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Small-scale validation**: Tested only on a 10.95M-parameter transformer; unclear how the approach scales to billion-parameter or larger models.
2. **Accuracy overhead**: The probe-based approximation introduces ~0.019 cross-entropy loss, which may compound over longer training runs or larger models.
3. **Analog hardware maturity**: Practical analog in-memory computing systems are still nascent; circuit noise, mismatch, and reliability remain open challenges not fully addressed here.
4. **Hyperparameter sensitivity**: No analysis of how the approach behaves under different learning rates, batch sizes, or dataset characteristics.
5. **Limited hardware scope**: Simulations are in software; actual silicon implementation and measurement of energy/speed gains are absent.
6. **Convergence theory**: The paper does not provide convergence guarantees or theoretical analysis of how approximation error in the flow affects final model quality.

## Open Questions
1. How does Physical Muon scale to multi-billion-parameter models and what is the accuracy-to-hardware-cost tradeoff at scale?
2. Can convergence guarantees be derived for the probe-based approximation, and how tight are these bounds?
3. How sensitive is the method to analog circuit noise, mismatch, and drift — and can robustness be engineered in?
4. Can the continuous-time flow framework extend to other optimizer families (e.g., adaptive methods beyond Muon)?
5. What is the actual energy and latency reduction measured on real analog hardware, and how does it compare to highly optimized GPU implementations?
6. Does the method work for other architectures (CNNs, RNNs) or is it primarily suited to transformers?

## Key Takeaway
Orthogonalization for efficient training can be computed as a physical equilibrium, enabling transformers to train on analog hardware with minimal accuracy loss.
