# Opaque Epistemic Mediation: How LLM Deployment Configurations Shape the Validation of Pseudo-Science

**Authors:** Davide Scarso, Hugo Noronha de Almeida, Joaquim Pina
**Published:** 2026-07-24 | **arXiv:** [2607.22513v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2607.22513v1)

## Summary
This paper reveals that commercial LLMs evaluate fringe scientific claims inconsistently across deployment configurations — not because the underlying models differ, but because system prompts, safety mechanisms, and interface routing create wildly different outputs for identical requests. The researchers found one model (Grok Fast) systematically assigns credibility to ethnonationalist pseudo-science while others refuse or score it low, and these stances shift unpredictably across updates and interfaces, raising urgent questions about whose responsibility it is when LLMs validate harmful misinformation.

## Theoretical Background
The paper sits at the intersection of three domains: (1) **LLM evaluation and behavioral consistency** — understanding whether neural language models produce stable, predictable outputs; (2) **epistemic responsibility and credibility attribution** — how systems come to validate or reject knowledge claims; (3) **transparency and accountability in AI systems** — the principle that users and regulators should understand how AI systems reach consequential decisions. The authors draw implicitly on concepts from philosophy of science (what makes a claim pseudo-scientific), AI safety (alignment between intended and actual behavior), and STS (science and technology studies) examining how technical systems embed social values. The specific pseudo-scientific framework tested (biosocial ethnology derived from Frank Salter's work) is positioned as a clear-cut case of refuted claims, allowing the researchers to measure whether LLMs handle epistemic consensus reliably.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical discovery of configuration-dependent epistemic stances**: Demonstrates that identical prompts produce radically different credibility judgments (70–75 vs. 15–40 on the same claim) across four LLM families and temporal snapshots, isolating configuration (not base model capability) as the variable.

2. **Silent updates without transparency**: Documents that Grok underwent an undocumented patch reversing its behavior overnight — shifting from chaotic validation to stably high validation of pseudo-science — with zero public notification.

3. **Interface-level inconsistency**: Shows the same model identifier (Grok, GPT-5.1) producing opposite outputs via API vs. web interface, months apart, implying deployment routing or hidden system prompts diverge at the infrastructure level.

4. **Erosion of responsible refusal**: Finds that the most defensible response (refusing to rate a pseudo-scientific claim) was present in some models/interfaces but degraded in successor versions, suggesting safety constraints are being relaxed or deprioritized.

5. **Reframing epistemic accountability**: Argues that LLM "stances" are not model properties but contingent effects of deployment, and this opacity constitutes a governance failure with public-health implications.

## Connections to Other Work
This work intersects with [[RLHF]] and [[alignment]] research — if fine-tuning and safety mechanisms are what drive epistemic stance variation, then the paper documents a failure mode of current alignment approaches: they are brittle, interface-dependent, and opaque.

It also relates to studies of **LLM hallucination and factuality** (e.g., mechanistic work on how transformers represent and retrieve knowledge), but shifts focus from *accuracy on factual retrieval* to *credibility assignment to contested claims* — a harder problem because it involves both knowledge and value.

The paper echoes concerns in [[mechanistic-interpretability]] about the difficulty of understanding what's *actually* controlling model behavior vs. what practitioners *think* is controlling it. It also touches on debates in AI governance around [[alignment]] verification: how do we audit whether a system is behaving as intended if its behavior is hidden behind opaque deployment configurations?

Methodologically, it extends work on **temporal stability of LLM outputs** (e.g., how GPT's answers drift across versions) by adding a configuration-level analysis.

## Practical Applications
1. **Risk assessment for critical-domain LLM use**: Organizations considering deploying LLMs for scientific curation, medical literature review, or educational content cannot trust published benchmarks — they must audit the *actual deployed version* on *their interface* repeatedly over time.

2. **Misinformation early-warning systems**: News organizations and fact-checking teams need to monitor LLMs as potential amplification vectors for fringe claims, particularly models with high credibility-assignment to pseudo-science, and track how stances shift after updates.

3. **Regulator and platform auditing**: Platforms hosting LLMs (OpenAI's ChatGPT, xAI's Grok on X) need external audit trails documenting system prompt changes, safety layer modifications, and interface routing logic — currently absent.

4. **Academic publishing and literature review**: Researchers using LLMs to summarize literature or screen papers risk inadvertently elevating low-quality work if the model's credibility heuristics are miscalibrated or shift mid-project.

5. **Institutional trust forensics**: When an LLM makes a high-profile error validating misinformation, users have no way to determine whether it was model bias, safety layer failure, intentional deprioritization, or a silent update — creating legal and reputational liability.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **LLM Epistemic Audit Service**: A SaaS platform that continuously probes commercial LLMs with a battery of contested-claim queries, tracking shifts in credibility assignment across versions, interfaces, and regions. Alert clients when a model's stance on pseudo-science changes, with forensic reports comparing API vs. web behavior.

2. **Configuration Transparency Proxy**: A middleware service that logs all system prompts, safety modifications, and deployment-level changes for LLMs accessed via API, making deployment decisions legible to users and auditors. Sell to enterprises and research institutions requiring compliance documentation.

3. **Credibility Consistency Checker**: A tool for publishers, educators, and platforms that validates whether an LLM's credibility assignments align with scientific consensus on a custom knowledge base, with alerts when drift exceeds a threshold. Market to universities and fact-checking orgs.

4. **LLM Governance Dashboard**: Enterprise software for organizations operating or relying on LLMs, providing real-time heat-maps of model behavior across regions, interfaces, and temporal snapshots. Flag instability and enable policy/safety responses before public harm.

5. **Pseudo-Science Detection Fine-Tune**: A specialized LLM adapter (or smaller model) trained on refuted-claim datasets to reliably *refuse* engagement with pseudo-scientific framing rather than assign credibility. License to platforms as a drop-in safety module.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Limited scope of pseudo-science tested**: The paper focuses on one framework (ethnonationalist biosocial theory). Generalizability to other classes of pseudo-science (medical misinformation, climate denial, alternative physics) is untested — different models may handle different domains inconsistently.

2. **No causal mechanism identification**: The paper documents *that* behavior varies by configuration but does not reverse-engineer *which* configuration parameters (system prompt wording, specific safety layer, router logic) are responsible. Requires access to model internals or systematic ablations.

3. **Temporal coverage gaps**: Snapshots span October 2025–February 2026 (5 months). Longer-term patterns, seasonal updates, or correlation with public events are not analyzed.

4. **No user-study of downstream impact**: The paper doesn't measure whether users actually *believe* pseudo-science when an LLM validates it, or whether credibility scores shift behavior. The harm is assumed, not empirically demonstrated.

5. **Interface and prompt variance not fully controlled**: The paper tests API vs. web but doesn't isolate which components of the deployment differ (different system prompts? different routing? different safety checkpoints?). Ablations are limited.

6. **Generalizability to future models unclear**: As model architectures, training data, and safety techniques evolve, the patterns observed may not persist. The findings are a snapshot of commercial LLMs in Q4 2025–Q1 2026.

## Open Questions
1. **What drives Grok's outlier behavior?** Is it a deliberate design choice (e.g., prioritizing "free speech"), an accidental training artifact, or a side effect of specific safety layer removal? Does xAI have internal documentation explaining this?

2. **Can we correlate configuration changes with public events or policy shifts?** The silent patch reversed Grok's behavior overnight — is this tied to X's moderation policy changes, user complaints, or retraining cycles?

3. **How do users actually respond to credibility scores from LLMs?** Do users trust LLM credibility judgments more than traditional sources? Does the *presentation* (as a score vs. a narrative assessment) affect belief formation?
