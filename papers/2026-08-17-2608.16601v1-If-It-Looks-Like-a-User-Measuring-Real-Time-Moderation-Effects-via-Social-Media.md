# "If It Looks Like a User": Measuring Real-Time Moderation Effects via Social Media Simulation

**Authors:** Enrico Verdolotti, Gianluca Nogara, Luca Luceri, Silvia Giordano
**Published:** 2026-08-17 | **arXiv:** [2608.16601v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.16601v1)

## Summary
This paper develops a calibrated agent-based simulator of social media information spread, grounded in real COVID-19 vaccine discourse data, to evaluate how well content moderation methods actually work when applied in real time. The key finding: static lab tests of moderation (removing bad actors after the fact) overestimate effectiveness, because remaining users compensate by resharing more content—a dynamic effect that only shows up in simulation.

## Theoretical Background
Agent-based models (ABMs) simulate systems by representing individual actors ("agents") with simple rules, letting emergent patterns arise from their interactions. Prior work used ABMs to study information diffusion on social networks, but often relied on hand-tuned parameters rather than empirical data. This paper builds on SimSoM, an existing ABM for social media, and upgrades it by fitting parameters to real data using CMA-ES—a derivative-free optimization algorithm that iteratively adjusts distributions to match observed outcomes. The validation framework checks temporal dynamics, statistical distributions, and network structure, ensuring the simulator captures real behavior, not just surface statistics.

## Core Contributions
1. **Empirical calibration method**: Replaces ad-hoc parameter choices with CMA-ES-fitted distributions grounded in a real vaccine-discourse dataset, validated across three independent dimensions (temporal, distributional, structural).
2. **Moderation method transfer**: Demonstrates that misinformation-detection and user-removal strategies transfer between real data and simulated data—establishing credibility for the simulator.
3. **Static vs. dynamic moderation gap**: Shows quantitatively that retroactive removal of top spreaders in static analysis overestimates effectiveness by 10–40% (depending on detector), because in real-time simulation, remaining users increase resharing to compensate. This is a causal insight only observable in simulation.

## Practical Applications
- **Platform risk assessment**: Moderation teams can use calibrated simulators to predict real-world impact of removing specific user cohorts before deploying changes.
- **Misinformation forecasting**: Public health agencies can simulate spread of health misinformation under different moderation policies (bans, labels, throttling).
- **Crisis response planning**: During pandemics or elections, simulators enable rapid testing of moderation strategies without waiting for A/B tests.
- **Regulatory compliance**: Platforms can provide regulators with simulation evidence that moderation strategies achieve claimed effectiveness under realistic conditions.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **ModSim platform**: SaaS tool for social platforms to upload their data, auto-calibrate an ABM, and test moderation policies in simulation before deployment; charge per test or subscription.
2. **Disinformation early-warning system**: Integrate calibrated simulators for specific topics (health, politics, finance) to predict spread under current moderation settings and alert teams to emerging risks.
3. **Content policy audit software**: Help platforms and regulators independently verify that published moderation-effectiveness claims match real-time simulation outcomes.
4. **Digital public health dashboards**: Provide NGOs and health ministries with white-label simulators to forecast health misinformation spread and evaluate countermeasures.
5. **Consulting service for policy design**: Specialize in running large-scale simulation studies for platforms, governments, and NGOs to design data-backed moderation or communication strategies.

## Technical Limitations
- **Domain specificity**: Model is calibrated on COVID-19 vaccine discourse; generalization to other topics (politics, finance) or platforms (TikTok, YouTube) is untested and may fail if user behavior or network structure differ.
- **Agent heterogeneity**: Agents follow simple rules; real users have diverse motivation, knowledge, and strategies that may not be captured by aggregate distributions.
- **Feedback loop fidelity**: The simulator assumes algorithmic feeds work like real platforms, but subtle recommendation-algorithm dynamics may be missing.
- **Scalability**: CMA-ES optimization is computationally expensive; unclear if the approach scales to billion-user networks or multi-year time horizons.
- **Causality assumptions**: Assumes removing users *causes* the observed resharing increase; alternative explanations (e.g., network saturation, topic decay) not ruled out.

## Open Questions
- How does the static-vs-dynamic gap vary across moderation methods (labels, throttling, shadowbans) vs. just removal?
- Can the calibration method transfer to entirely different platforms or topics without retraining?
- What is the sensitivity to initial parameter distributions? How robust is the CMA-ES fit to noisy or biased data?
- Do agent-based rules need explicit modeling of algorithmic recommendation, or do aggregate statistics suffice?
- How would the simulator behave if adversaries actively game the moderation (e.g., account manipulation, coordinated campaigns)?

## Key Takeaway
Real-time moderation is less effective than lab tests suggest because remaining users compensate—simulation reveals this, static analysis hides it.
