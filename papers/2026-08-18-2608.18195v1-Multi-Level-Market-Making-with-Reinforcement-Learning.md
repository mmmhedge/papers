# Multi-Level Market Making with Reinforcement Learning

**Authors:** Patrick Cheridito, Moritz Weiss
**Published:** 2026-08-18 | **arXiv:** [2608.18195v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2608.18195v1)

## Summary
This paper applies reinforcement learning to market making—the task of profitably buying and selling assets by placing orders at multiple price levels in a limit order book. The key innovation is using deep learning to handle variable-length order data and strategic reward shaping to train an agent that dynamically adjusts order sizes and inventory levels in response to different types of market participants.

## Theoretical Background
Market making is a foundational problem in quantitative finance: a dealer profits from the bid-ask spread (the difference between buy and sell prices) while managing inventory risk. Traditional approaches use static formulas or hand-tuned heuristics. This paper builds on two streams of work: (1) reinforcement learning for finance, where an agent learns a policy by maximizing cumulative rewards through trial and error, and (2) limit order book microstructure research, which models how prices and volumes evolve when traders submit orders at different prices. The authors use deep-set encoders—neural network modules that aggregate variable-length sets into fixed-dimensional representations—to handle the complexity of modeling many simultaneous orders. They also employ potential-based reward shaping, a technique from RL that adds information to guide learning without changing the optimal solution.

## Core Contributions
1. **Multi-level order placement with RL**: A framework that learns to submit market orders (immediate execution) and limit orders (patient orders at specific price levels) of varying sizes across multiple price tiers, rather than treating all orders identically.
2. **Deep-set architecture for order aggregation**: Uses deep-set encoders to compress variable-length order information into a fixed latent state, enabling end-to-end learning on realistic order book data.
3. **Potential-based reward shaping**: Applies this RL technique to accelerate convergence without biasing the learned policy toward suboptimal strategies.
4. **Multi-agent simulation validation**: Tests the agent against three realistic opponent types (noise traders, tactical traders responding to volume, strategic traders following signals), not just stylized noise.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of [[Reinforcement Learning]] for finance and [[Market Microstructure]]. It extends prior RL approaches to trading (which typically focus on simple buy-hold-sell decisions) to the more complex setting of continuous inventory management and multi-level liquidity provision. The use of deep-set encoders relates to recent work on [[Graph Neural Networks]] and permutation-invariant learning. The paper implicitly addresses the [[Multi-Agent Systems]] problem: the agent must adapt to behavior of other traders, though no game-theoretic equilibrium analysis is provided. Potential-based reward shaping connects to classical RL theory but is relatively novel in algorithmic trading contexts.

## Practical Applications
1. **Electronic market making**: Directly deployable to automated market makers (AMMs) or proprietary trading desks operating limit order books on equities, futures, or crypto exchanges.
2. **High-frequency trading optimization**: Tuning order submission strategies for firms already running HFT infrastructure, reducing manual tuning of spread and volume parameters.
3. **Micro-execution algorithms**: Improving the execution quality of large institutional orders by interleaving market and limit orders dynamically based on learned patterns.
4. **Exchange simulator calibration**: Using the multi-agent simulation framework to test new market rules or fee structures before deployment.

## Product & Startup Ideas
1. **RL-powered market making SaaS**: A cloud-based platform where retail or small prop firms upload order book data and receive trained agents for their specific market segment, without needing quant expertise.
2. **Backtesting engine for multi-agent scenarios**: Software that lets traders stress-test strategies against realistic opponent profiles (tactical, strategic, noise), helping practitioners understand robustness.
3. **Order placement optimization plugin**: A middleware layer for exchanges or brokers that wraps this RL module to improve execution quality and reduce trader latency costs.
4. **Crypto liquidity provision protocol**: Decentralized market maker using this framework to algorithmically provide liquidity on AMMs, adapting to different market regimes.
5. **Risk-aware market making**: Enhanced product that combines this RL approach with explicit inventory risk constraints, marketed to compliance-heavy institutions (pension funds, asset managers).

## Technical Limitations
1. **Simulation risk**: All validation occurs in synthetic environments with hand-coded opponent models. Real markets have fat tails, regime switches, and adversarial participants that may exploit learned patterns.
2. **Scalability concerns**: Deep-set encoders scale as O(n) in order set size; unclear how performance degrades with hundreds of price levels or ultra-high-frequency snapshots.
3. **Reward specification**: The paper does not discuss how to balance revenue maximization against inventory costs—the reward function choice is underspecified and likely domain-dependent.
4. **Non-stationarity**: Assumes opponent behavior is stationary; real markets exhibit feedback loops where many RL agents could destabilize each other (explored experimentally but not theoretically).
5. **Computational cost**: Training time, compute requirements, and sample efficiency in real markets are not reported; RL in finance is often sample-inefficient.
6. **Transaction costs & slippage**: Ignores realistic market microstructure details like partial fills, latency, and execution delays.

## Open Questions
1. How does the learned policy perform when deployed against truly novel opponent types not seen during training? Does it generalize across assets or market regimes?
2. What is the sample efficiency? How many simulated trades does the agent need to converge, and how would this scale to training on real tick data?
3. Can you provide theoretical guarantees (e.g., regret bounds) or at least empirical stability analysis showing the policy doesn't collapse under perturbations?
4. How sensitive is the final policy to reward shaping design choices? Which potential functions work best for market making?
5. Can the framework handle market impact—i.e., does submitting large orders move the price—or does it assume prices are exogenous?
6. How do multi-agent equilibria emerge if multiple copies of this agent trade in the same market simultaneously?

## Key Takeaway
Deep RL can learn profitable multi-level market-making strategies by aggregating order book data and shaping rewards, outperforming hand-tuned heuristics in simulation.
