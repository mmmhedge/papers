# Belief-Aware Multi-Agent Path Finding under Map Uncertainty

**Authors:** Viraj Parimi, Shao-Hung Chan, Han Zhang, Jingkai Chen, Brian Williams
**Published:** 2026-09-30 | **arXiv:** [2609.40269v1](http://arxiv.org/abs/2609.40269v1)

## Summary
This paper tackles multi-agent path finding when the environment map is partially unknown and changes unexpectedly. Instead of treating each new obstacle discovery as an isolated event, the authors propose MAGIC — a framework that infers the traversability of nearby unseen locations from sparse observations, allowing teams of agents to plan smarter detours before they hit obstacles. The approach scales to hundreds of agents and reduces total travel costs by ~30% compared to existing methods.

## Theoretical Background
Multi-Agent Path Finding (MAPF) is the classical problem of computing collision-free routes for multiple agents simultaneously. Traditional MAPF assumes a known, static map. This paper builds on two key insights: (1) real-world obstacles are spatially correlated (a fallen object at location A makes location B less likely to be traversable), and (2) agents can share observations to update a *shared belief* about the environment. The core technical machinery is a **Gaussian Markov Random Field** — a probabilistic graphical model where traversability estimates at different locations are connected — paired with **Gaussian Belief Propagation**, an approximate inference algorithm that efficiently spreads information across the spatial network. The framework then feeds these updated cost estimates into standard MAPF planners.

## Core Contributions
1. **Belief-aware formulation**: Frames MAPF under map uncertainty where discrepancies are fixed but initially hidden, and observations carry spatial information about unobserved regions.
2. **MAGIC framework**: Combines Gaussian Markov Random Fields and belief propagation to jointly infer traversability online, converting probabilistic beliefs into detour-aware edge costs for MAPF solvers.
3. **Scalability validation**: Demonstrates the approach on MAPF benchmarks with teams up to 800 agents, showing 96.3% improvement over baseline methods without expensive replanning.
4. **Planner-agnostic integration**: Works with multiple existing MAPF planners (decoupled, coupled) without modifying their internals.

## Connections to Other Work
This work sits at the intersection of **uncertainty quantification in planning** and **cooperative multi-agent coordination**. It extends classical [[MAPF]] beyond the perfect-information assumption by incorporating **Bayesian inference**, similar in spirit to [[POMDP|partially observable MDPs]] but in a non-Markovian, spatial setting. Related approaches include contingent planning (pre-compute branches for different obstacle scenarios) and reactive replanning (recompute routes on new observations), but MAGIC's key difference is leveraging *spatial correlation* rather than treating each observation as independent. The belief propagation technique draws from graphical model inference, a mature area in probabilistic AI, and applies it to a concrete coordination problem.

## Practical Applications
1. **Warehouse automation**: Robots navigating fulfillment centers where packages may fall or block aisles; MAGIC learns spatial patterns (congested zones, common blockages) and anticipates detours.
2. **Autonomous vehicle fleets**: Multiple delivery vehicles sharing real-time traffic observations to infer congestion in unobserved streets and coordinate efficient routes.
3. **Disaster response**: Teams of drones or ground robots exploring damaged areas where some routes may be blocked; observations at one site inform traversability estimates for nearby, unvisited areas.
4. **Swarm robotics in dynamic environments**: Hundreds of micro-robots collaborating in factories or labs where local failures (spills, debris) spread predictably.

## Technical Limitations
1. **Gaussian assumption**: The Gaussian Markov Random Field assumes traversability distributions are approximately Gaussian; highly non-linear or multi-modal obstacles may be poorly modeled.
2. **Spatial correlation must be learnable**: The framework requires enough spatial correlation in obstacles for inference to help; if obstacles are perfectly random and isolated, belief propagation provides minimal benefit.
3. **Fixed obstacle assumption**: Obstacles are assumed to be fixed after appearing; moving or dynamic obstacles (other agents, people) are not directly addressed.
4. **Belief propagation approximation**: The algorithm is approximate, so in adversarial or highly constrained problem instances, inference errors could cascade, degrading path quality below replanning baselines.
5. **Scalability of belief state**: As the map grows large or the team is very large, maintaining and communicating a shared belief becomes a bottleneck; the paper doesn't deeply explore communication or memory costs.
6. **Initialization & prior sensitivity**: The initial belief about traversability (prior distribution) may significantly impact performance, and the paper doesn't characterize sensitivity to misspecified priors.

## Open Questions
1. How does MAGIC perform with **non-Gaussian obstacle distributions** (e.g., obstacles clustered in tight corridors)? Can the framework extend to mixture models or non-parametric inference?
2. What is the **communication cost** of maintaining shared beliefs in a distributed setting? Can agents maintain approximate, local beliefs and still coordinate effectively?
3. How does performance degrade if the **spatial correlation structure is misspecified** (e.g., assuming correlation length is wrong)?
4. Can **online learning** of the correlation parameters improve performance over time as the team observes more maps?
5. How does MAGIC interact with **dynamic obstacles** (moving agents, people) that appear and disappear during execution?
6. What theoretical **guarantees** (e.g., completeness, bounded suboptimality) does belief-aware planning provide compared to replanning?

## Key Takeaway
Observing one blocked path reveals information about nearby paths too—MAGIC leverages this to cut multi-agent travel costs by letting robots anticipate obstacles before they waste time rerouting.
